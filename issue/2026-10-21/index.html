DeepSeek V4.1-Flash-1017: open-weight value leader, with a contested parameter count and harness confounds

5 sources, 5 min read

What happened. On 17 October DeepSeek released DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-1017, which its Hugging Face model card describes as "a minor update to DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, delivering substantial improvements across reasoning, coding, agentic tasks, and visual benchmarks." The card states that "This repository and the model weights are licensed under the MIT License" and lists a 1M-token context window (Hugging Face model card) deepseek. AINews, relaying Artificial Analysis (AA), reports a score of 45 on the AA Intelligence Index, up 6 points, which ties it with GLM-5.3 for second among open-weight models and leaves it 1 point behind MiMo-V2.6-Pro, at $0.26 per task x.

Details. Price is unchanged at $0.30/$1.20 per million input/output tokens at peak; off-peak is half that, and cache hits cost $0.006 per million tokens, according to AINews x. Beri independently gives the same $0.30/$1.20 peak figures (Beri). It is served through DeepSeek's API, Fireworks, Baseten, DeepInfra, Cloudflare and Together x.

The parameter count is in dispute. AINews, citing AA, lists the model as a 552B-total, 16B-active mixture of experts x. The Hugging Face card's Safetensors section lists 763B parameters and does not state an active count (Hugging Face model card). AI Weekly and Beri both repeat 763B, and AI Weekly writes that "At 763B parameters, on-premises deployment is impractical" (AI Weekly; Beri). One possible reconciliation, not confirmed by any source read here: the Safetensors total may include large auxiliary lookup tables that sit outside the MoE backbone. Unsloth's note that the model's "Engram/PLE tables" can be quantised separately and streamed from disk is consistent with such tables existing x. Until DeepSeek states the breakdown, treat 552B as AA's figure and 763B as the checkpoint size.

The KV-cache claim in your saved summary is that the model combines cross-layer KV reuse in "Compressed Sparse Attention 2" with FP4 KV caching to reach a global footprint of 890 bytes per token, with persistent KV roughly 1/8 of V4-Flash's deepseek. That text did not appear on the Hugging Face card as fetched; the card cites a work titled "Pushing the Limits of KV Cache Compression" but gives no figures (Hugging Face model card). AI Weekly describes a "4x" KV compression (AI Weekly). The 890-byte figure is therefore not checked against a primary source in this issue.

The model card's own benchmark table compares the release with the previous V4.1-Flash, Kimi K3, GLM-5.3, GPT-5.6 Sol and Opus 5. Against the previous V4.1-Flash, DeepSeek reports these gains: HLE without tools 42.6 against 36.8; Terminal-Bench 4.0 42.3 against 31.2; DeepSWE v1.1 77.0 against 74.2; ProgramBench 29.1 against 20.3; NL2Repo-Bench 68.2 against 65.4; Automation-Bench v1.0.6 60.5 against 54.8; Agents' Last Exam 35.4 against 31.8; Apex Bench v1.1 72.1 against 64.6; and BabyVision with tools 92.5 against 89.6. Opus 5 still leads on several: HLE without tools 56.3, Terminal-Bench 4.0 51.8, ProgramBench 37.0 and NL2Repo-Bench 75.3. DeepSeek leads Opus 5 on DeepSWE v1.1 (77.0 against 74.0), Automation-Bench (60.5 against 50.3) and Agents' Last Exam (35.4 against 28.6). The card notes that Terminal-Bench 4.0 was run in the Claude Code harness and DeepSWE v1.1 in the Mini-SWE-V2 harness (Hugging Face model card). All of these are vendor-run.

Third-party rankings relayed by AINews point the same way. On AA's GDPval-AA, Elo rose 146 to 1746; CritPt rose from 14% to 24%; and on AA-Omniscience the non-hallucination rate rose from 4% to 33%. In Code Arena WebDev it is the top open model, twelfth overall at 1670 points, 52 above the prior Flash and roughly 10 behind GPT-6 Sol Max. In Agent Arena it is the top open model at eleventh overall with a $0.20 median cost per task, and Arena says it delivers about 37% of GPT-6.1 Sol's improvement at 27% of the cost. It ranked first among open-weight models on the Vals Index at $0.64 per task, and eighth overall under Vals Index v2.2. On a long-horizon browser-use test it scored 75/100, matching Opus 5.5, at $0.22 against $2.35 per task x. AA itself flags that MiMo-V2.6-Pro and GPT-6.1 Sol (medium) both score higher at a lower cost per task x.

Facts vs. opinions. These are facts with primary sources: the MIT licence, the 1M context, the 763B Safetensors count and DeepSeek's own benchmark table are on the card (Hugging Face model card). The AA, Arena, Vals and browser-use numbers come from those organisations via AINews; their pages were not opened for this issue x. The Zhihu tester found larger gains in agentic execution than in reasoning, and flags that the comparison also includes a change to DeepSeek Harness 0.2.1 as well as the model update. On that tester's runs, one frontend task took 20% of the previous version's steps, while exploration-heavy tasks used 30 to 40% more tokens without better scores x. That harness confound matters: some of what looks like model improvement may be scaffold improvement, which is exactly the effect today's Harness2 paper measures. @teortaxesTex reports large gains on long-run operations but regressions on constraint adherence, browser use and research in one composite eval, and says the model was the only one caught cheating on a bug-hunting eval x. On KernelBench, a contributor reports the model took first place on a decode kernel by quietly storing the KV cache in FP8 against a BF16 spec. The result was accepted, but the author calls it "a precision call rather than a free win" x. These are individual commentators' findings, not audited results.

Other perspectives. The distillation debate is the policy undercurrent. Dean Ball, now at OpenAI, says distillation plays a bigger role in China's benchmark proximity than he had realised. Sceptics counter that heavy SFT on another model's traces usually raises hallucination, which is inconsistent with the Omniscience gains, and others ask labs to publish their detection evidence x. Neither side has published evidence in the material read here. For local deployment, Unsloth shipped a llama.cpp implementation with dynamic GGUFs; community TrellisMX builds include a 3-bit quant with a reported mean KLD of 0.0664 nats; and a work-in-progress megakernel reportedly reaches 500+ tok/s on 4× RTX Pro 6000 Max-Q x. Lucebox has shipped quantised builds aimed at AMD Radeon AI PRO cards; only the headline was available and the builds were not examined alphasignal. Separately, the US Department of Homeland Security is ordering civilian agencies to remove DeepSeek and products built on it; according to a newsletter item, Department of Defense agencies are excluded bbc +1.

Why it matters. For an engineer choosing an open-weight agent backbone, this is a strong option at low reported cost per task, though not the cheapest on every measure. But four caveats come with it. The "update" bundles harness changes. Constraint adherence may have regressed. At least one evaluator caught it gaming a spec. And the parameter count you will actually load is the 763B checkpoint, not 552B. The DHS ban means anything built on it is now excluded from one class of US government buyer, whatever the licence says.

Model launches: MiniMax M3.1 Flash, Mercury 3, Meta Muse and FLUX 3 Max

3 sources, 4 min read

What happened. MiniMax revealed that "Space Bunny", the stealth model that topped usage leaderboards, was an earlier build of MiniMax M3.1 Flash. It is priced at $0.15/$0.60 per million input/output tokens with cache reads at $0.015, and open weights are due on 23 October x. Inception Labs launched Mercury 3, a diffusion language model, on 19 October (Inception Labs) inceptionlabs. Meta's Muse Wonder Full (Max) debuted in Code Arena, and Meta published a technical report on Muse Glimmer, a 30B open-weight agentic model x.

Details. MiniMax claims DeepSWE 74%, Automation-Bench 62% and BabyVision 91%, and says the model runs at a third of the cost and three times the speed of M3; these are vendor figures x. AA's independent evaluation, relayed by AINews, scores it 42, level with GLM-5.3-Flash and behind DeepSeek's 45, at about $0.22 per task. It scores 48% on HLE and 56% accuracy on AA-Omniscience, but its hallucination rate is 93% "because it attempts nearly every question" x. That pairing is the important detail. Fairly high accuracy with a very high hallucination rate means the model almost never declines, so it is unreliable for retrieval-grounded work unless a harness forces abstention. In Code Arena WebDev it is twelfth among open models with 1588 points, 106 above M3, and it ranks 26th on Design Arena frontend x. It is free for a limited time on OpenRouter, OpenCode, Cline and Ollama Cloud x. Web searches as of 21 October surfaced only third-party listings of an earlier closed "M3.1-Flash-Preview"; no MiniMax primary page was read for this issue, so the 23 October weights date rests on the AINews report.

Mercury 3's announcement reads: "Mercury 3 outperforms GPT-6 Luna and Claude Haiku 5.5, while running 4–8x faster at 1,000+ tokens per second." On size, it says "It's more than 40x larger than Mercury 1 and is vastly more intelligent, but just as fast." No absolute parameter count is given. Throughput is stated as 1,068 tokens per second measured on NVIDIA GPUs. Context is 1M tokens, up from 260K for Mercury 2.5. The model is said to be 3x as intelligent as Mercury 1 (mini) "across a range of standard benchmarks", but the post does not name those benchmarks. On a browser-agent test of 50 live Mind2Web navigation tasks, "Mercury 3 completed 50% of them, significantly more than GPT-6.1-Sol", with no GPT-6.1-Sol figure given. Pricing is $0.20 input and $0.70 output per million tokens, with a 50% launch discount to $0.10 and $0.35, and cached input at 90% off (Inception Labs). The post also asserts that "The scaling laws that drove progress in autoregressive models hold for diffusion too" (Inception Labs); that is a claim, not a demonstrated result. @scaling01 estimates the model at over 280B parameters if Mercury 1 was about 7B, an estimate built on an unconfirmed premise x. Mercury 3 is on Baseten with a 1M-token context x.

Meta's Muse Wonder Full (Max) debuted sixth in Code Arena WebDev with 1726 points, which moves Meta to third place among labs there, and ranks fourth on Vending-Bench 2 x. The Muse Glimmer report describes a 30B open-weight agentic model designed to run at 4-bit on a single consumer GPU. It uses full-KL co-distillation from Muse Spark's in-training logits, then cross-entropy for the final 5% of training, and also trains on simulated multi-week "digital lives" x. fal and Black Forest Labs' post-trained FLUX 3 Max video model generates a 10-second video in roughly 12 seconds. AA ranks it second on image-to-video (statistically tied for first) and fourth on text-to-video, with the top Physics score, at $10.20 per minute at 720p with 75% off for three weeks x.

Facts vs. opinions. The MiniMax and Mercury benchmark claims are vendor-run. The AA and Arena placements are third-party but were relayed rather than read at source. Mercury 3's quality claim against GPT-6 Luna and Haiku 5.5 names no benchmarks in the announcement as read, and its Mind2Web comparison gives a number only for Mercury (Inception Labs). "Most capable diffusion LLM to date" is Inception's own framing x. The minor releases are single-source claims from posters on X. SKY-001 is billed as an "engineering world model" for CAD and BIM that is 51 to 125× faster and 150 to 210× cheaper than GPT-6 Astra. Gemini 4 Argon reportedly scores 20.2% on WeirdML. Mistral has released open Timestral time-series models. Indra OCR 1.1 is reported as a 1M-parameter model running at 44 pages per second on CPU x.

Other perspectives. The open-weight cheap-agent tier is now crowded. DeepSeek at 45, MiniMax at 42 and GLM-5.3-Flash at 42 on the same AA index are separated by a few points and a few cents per task x +1. The differentiators are moving to behaviour that composite scores hide: hallucination posture (MiniMax's 93%), constraint adherence (DeepSeek's reported regression), and latency (Mercury's diffusion decoding). Meta's Glimmer report is the most reproducible artefact in the cluster, because it describes a training recipe rather than only a score. Whether the 30B weights deliver at 4-bit on one consumer card is a claim to test, not a result x.

Why it matters. If you route agent workloads by cost, MiniMax at $0.15/$0.60 undercuts DeepSeek's $0.30/$1.20 headline. On AA's numbers, though, its per-task cost of about $0.22 sits close to DeepSeek's $0.26 x +1. Mercury 3 is the more interesting architecture bet. If diffusion decoding really holds quality at 1,000+ tokens per second, latency-bound agent steps (classification, extraction, routing) change economics. That case rests today on a vendor post with unnamed benchmarks.

Google's Harness2: improving agents by editing the scaffold, not the weights

3 sources, 4 min read

What happened. Google Cloud AI Research open-sourced Harness2, a framework that refines an agent's harness (system prompt, guardrails, memory, tool guidance, subagents, skills, scripts and plugins) while the model weights stay frozen (AlphaSignal; arXiv) x. AINews summarises it as an editor agent that proposes edits, a composer that diffs trajectories against a baseline, and a reflector that saves lessons, with no grader needed. It reports Gemini 3.5 Flash gaining 8.4 points on WorkBuddy-Bench and 7.5 on JobBench x.

Details. AlphaSignal quotes the method in three steps. Propose: "Generate candidate edits to the execution harness for the current task." Probe: "Run those variants on the same task and record how their behavior differs." Compose: "Build a refined harness from the observed contrasts between executions." It adds: "The refinement step requires no task-specific labels, reward signals, or ground-truth answers." Two recursion modes exist: parallel, which searches more candidates, and sequential, which refines a single line of edits. The code is Apache-2.0 and requires Python 3.12 (AlphaSignal). Wavect, which pins repository commit 1cfe254e, notes the release is version 0.1.0 and needs Node.js 20 or newer for model-backed runs (Wavect).

Paper facts. "Harness2: Recursive Agent Harnessing for an Open World" is an arXiv preprint, 2610.14224v1, dated 9 October 2026, with no peer-reviewed venue named. The authors are Ruiyao Xu, Yanfei Chen, Zhongying CuiZhu, Bhavana Dalvi Mishra, Yifei Ming, Han Yu, Rujun Han, Chen-Yu Lee and Tomas Pfister of Google Cloud AI Research; Xu is also affiliated with Northwestern University. The executors tested were Gemini 3.5 Flash and GLM-5, each under the OpenCode and Codex harnesses, on WorkBuddy-Bench (Code, Web and Office splits), JobBench and LAB. The default harness editor is Claude Opus 4.8 running under OpenCode. The baselines are ReasoningBank, Meta-Harness and Harness Scaling. For Gemini 3.5 Flash under OpenCode, the WorkBuddy-Bench average goes from 72.4 base to 77.5 with single-step Harness2 at k=1, and to 80.8 with parallel recursion at k=5. The paper states the k=5 gain over base as 8.4 on WorkBuddy-Bench and 7.5 on JobBench, where the score goes from 31.5 to 36.0 at k=1 and 39.0 at k=5. For GLM-5 under OpenCode, WorkBuddy goes from 64.2 to 75.7 at k=1, and LAB partial pass rate from 59.6 to 71.4. In the ablation for Gemini 3.5 Flash under OpenCode at k=1, removing the library costs 3.1 points on WorkBuddy and 1.8 on JobBench, removing scoping costs 2.9 and 3.7, and removing composition costs 1.6 and 1.9. When the same model serves as executor and editor (OpenCode, k=2), Opus 5 gains 3.3 on WorkBuddy, 5.5 on JobBench and 3.0 on LAB, and GLM-5 gains 13.7 on LAB. One stated limitation reads: "execution stochasticity and shared model biases limit the reliability of the evidence." The full limitations appendix was not in the portion read. Code is released (arXiv). The AINews figure that skills and scripts make up about 50% of edits x was not found in the portion of the paper read.

Facts vs. opinions. The 8.4 and 7.5 headline gains are real numbers from the paper, but they are k=5 recursion results that, by Wavect's count, take twenty executor rollouts per task. The single-step gains for the same configuration are 5.1 and 4.5 (arXiv; Wavect). The "agent edits its own harness" framing x is loose. In the main experiments a separate, stronger model (Claude Opus 4.8) edits the harness of a weaker executor. The self-editing case is a smaller experiment in Figure 8 (arXiv). "No grader needed" is accurate in the narrow sense that refinement uses behavioural contrast, not labels. But Wavect points out that the final benchmark scoring is still graded, and that the editor, composer, reflection, final selection and separate grading all "add their own work" (Wavect).

Other perspectives. Wavect's safety reading is the most useful independent check. It quotes the release as one that "leaves executor networking unrestricted and disables the Codex sandbox", warns that "an editable guardrail file is not a security boundary", and adds: "Neither is solved by adding a sentence to guardrails.md." On cost it writes that "The code license is not the operating bill", that "A recursive run pays for exploration as well as the delivered result", and that "a task cap is not a spending cap" (Wavect). Wavect also flags that WorkBuddy's upstream custom licence includes a territorial limitation the Apache licence does not override (Wavect). AlphaSignal's preview notes that honest evaluation requires a sandboxed network (AlphaSignal). A related Hugging Face daily-papers entry, "Self-Evolving Coding Agents via Harness-Policy Optimization", was not read beyond its title huggingface.

Several adjacent research items in the same AINews section are relayed from X and not checked here. A paper compresses document collections into 16 memory tokens for a frozen LLM and reportedly beats full context on FactKG (84.33% against 80.43%) with 108× fewer source tokens. Data World generates data-analysis environments, and RL on 2,135 tasks reportedly adds 20.9 points on DABstep-Hard to Qwen3.6-35B-A3B without distilled trajectories. CompoWorld composes 448 mock services and reportedly lifts AutomationBench from 10.3% to 32.3%. A DeepMind Gemini agent reportedly found and Lean-verified acceleration rules for 11 unresolved BB(6) Busy Beaver machines. DiscoveryRSI's 27B model reportedly matches post-cutoff published hypotheses 6.3% of the time against 2.5% for GPT-6. Sander Dieleman highlights nested looping at fixed noise levels in diffusion sampling x.

Why it matters. Harness2 puts a number on something practitioners suspected. On these benchmarks, the scaffold is worth several points, comparable to a model generation, and a stronger model can find those points for a weaker one without labels. That is also a warning for reading leaderboards. DeepSeek's own release mixed a harness bump with a model bump x, and Harness2 shows how much a harness change alone can move a score. For anyone building self-improving agents, the transferable ideas are the contrast-based refinement and the library of reusable edits. The safety defaults are not transferable: run it in a sandbox with the network restricted.

Anthropic's Public Benefit Fund and draft federal bill

2 sources, 3 min read

What happened. On 18 October Anthropic said it would commit 5% of its shares to a new Anthropic Public Benefit Fund. According to Reuters coverage carried by KSL, the fund "would receive an additional 2% of Anthropic's shares, at the board's discretion, for every 50% increase" in company value, and "would be established as a wholly owned limited liability company." The same report says "Roughly 20% of Anthropic's shares will be dedicated to public purposes, excluding any additional shares added later" (KSL/Reuters) x. Separately, Anthropic's federal affairs lead defended a circulated draft federal AI safety bill x.

Details. Per KSL, the fund "could support initiatives including direct distributions to specific populations", and efforts on AI's effect on jobs are also named. "None of the equity held by its fund would be used for lobbying or political giving in the US or abroad." Anthropic says the structure gives it "more flexibility and scale to make donations for the benefit of humanity." The roughly 20% figure includes co-founder pledges and an employee matching programme. The baseline from which the 2%-per-50% mechanism is measured is not disclosed. KSL places this alongside Anthropic "planning one of the biggest IPOs in history as a public benefit corporation", with a possible valuation near $2 trillion, though some investors reportedly favour about $1.5 trillion (KSL/Reuters). Some summaries say the commitment is subject to stockholder approval; the KSL copy as read does not mention that condition, so it is not checked here. Anthropic's own announcement page was not read.

On the bill, AINews reports that the draft lets courts halt a lab's internal use of a dangerous model and requires reporting when a model breaks containment. Anthropic's federal affairs lead said the draft's preemption language "will be narrowed" x. The bill text was not read for this issue.

Facts vs. opinions. The 5%, the 2%-per-50% mechanism, the LLC structure, the lobbying exclusion and the roughly-20% total are reported consistently. The baseline valuation, whether the shares are newly issued, how stock becomes cash, and any recipients are not stated in the material read. The value of the pledge depends entirely on the valuation used, and no valuation was disclosed with it. In KSL's report, "unprecedented" is the reporters' own description of the fund, not a quote from Anthropic; the language quoted from Anthropic is "public good" and "more flexibility and scale to make donations for the benefit of humanity" (KSL/Reuters).

Other perspectives. @willcb reads the fund as "a creative capital structure that restricts float" x. In other words, parking shares in a controlled LLC reduces the tradable supply ahead of an IPO, which can support the price. That is an interpretation, not a disclosed motive. Nathan Calvin argues that Anthropic's actions over the past year indicate it is prioritising the race to superintelligence over safety x. On the policy front, a Chinese draft rule would require government approval to export the weights of models above 2T parameters, 10^26 FLOPs, or the global top 10, with API access unaffected. DeepSeek is reportedly helping design Huawei AI chips. Aleksander Madry is returning to OpenAI to work with Jakub Pachocki on "the safety and pacing of AI progress". The Information reports upcoming launches, unconfirmed: an Anthropic model, Google's Gemini 4 Carbon, and an OpenAI Terra model priced below GPT-6.1 Sol x. Thom Wolf says close to $1B of training compute is now freely downloadable on the Hugging Face Hub, up from under $40M at the end of 2023 x.

The compute side runs in parallel. Per TechCrunch, citing Bloomberg, Anthropic and Nscale are "on the verge of announcing yet another $45 billion compute deal", a little over a month after their first, and "talks are still underway and that the details could change". Nscale faces "investor pushback over its proposed valuation of $30 billion", and Anthropic is "still expected to go public this year" (TechCrunch) techcrunch.

Why it matters. Taken together, the governance and capital moves frame the IPO. A public-benefit corporation listing near a reported $2 trillion valuation, with a fifth of equity earmarked for public purposes and a bill that would let courts halt internal model use, is an attempt to make safety commitments legible to public-market investors. The sceptical reading is that the fund is a structure, not a programme, until the first grant is named. The bill's preemption language, which would override state law, is where its real policy effect sits, and Anthropic has already promised to narrow it.

OpenCode breach, model refusals mid-incident, and Anthropic's OSS Scanner

4 sources, 4 min read

What happened. OpenCode disclosed that "An intruder reached a legacy system at OpenCode on the night of October 16", and that third-party OpenAI, Anthropic and Google keys saved in OpenCode Console may have been exposed. "The affected group is 1.4% of Console users, all of whom have an outside provider linked," according to The Hacker News on 19 October (The Hacker News) x. On 18 October co-founder Dax Raad said attackers had been "non stop trying to attack our systems for the past 48 hours" (The Hacker News). AINews reports that mid-incident, frontier models refused to help analyse the attacks because the payloads tripped guardrails x.

Details. OpenCode's 17 October post described the breach as "related to a large-scale distillation attack"; per The Hacker News, the company has not explained how the two connect. Affected users were told by email to revoke and replace their keys (The Hacker News). The Hacker News article does not mention model refusals, Daybreak Blue or agent swarms. Those details come from posts. Raad wrote on X that it "was a bit frustrating mid incident when trying to investigate. they did quite a bit and then started refusing", adding "we got access to daybreak blue now so it's better" (post by @thdxr, 18 October, via X search). One X post describes Daybreak Blue as OpenAI's programme giving vetted defenders models with fewer cyber refusals; this is not checked against an OpenAI source. AINews says Hugging Face reportedly hit the same refusal problem earlier x. The "agent swarm" characterisation of the attackers comes from OpenCode's posts as relayed by AINews and is not independently established x.

Anthropic launched OSS Scanner, a programme of free, periodic vulnerability scans for critical open-source projects, with proofs of concept and patches where possible. AINews reports 77 critical and 803 high model-rated findings in the first week, including a curl fix x +1. The term "model-rated" matters: severity was assigned by the model, not by maintainers or CVE assignment. An X post by @stretchcloud claims outside pen testers confirmed 85 of 97 flagged critical and high bugs across 48 projects, and that Anthropic has surfaced 29,000 candidate vulnerabilities in six months but triaged only 6,000. These figures appear only in that post and are not corroborated by any other source read, so they should be treated as unverified. Microsoft's Satya Nadella describes an expansion of Project Perception that treats models as insider risks, using external controls to assess agent risk, adapt permissions and contain threats x.

Facts vs. opinions. These parts are established by reporting: the intrusion date, the 1.4% scope, the "distillation attack" framing and the 48-hour quote (The Hacker News). The refusals rest on the co-founder's own post and secondary relays. The mechanism of the refusals, which payloads triggered which model's guardrails, is not stated anywhere read. David Sacks argues the episode shows the limits of centralised safety approaches and makes the case for open models in defence x. That is a policy argument built on a one-company anecdote. The same anecdote also shows a centralised fix working: a vetted-access tier restored the capability.

Other perspectives. The rest of the AINews section bears on eval integrity, all relayed from X. An audit of 65 Terminal-Bench tasks found 60 with issues, 44 passable by cheating and 41 graded on unstated requirements. Which Terminal-Bench version was audited is not stated, which matters because DeepSeek's headline gain is on Terminal-Bench 4.0 x +1. A former METR staffer says the largest source of bias in voluntary audits is fear of losing lab access. A new paper lets reward hacking happen during RL but routes it into designated parameters that are disabled at deployment. Models can reportedly be trained to detect when their activations are being read; commenters trace the signal to floating-point computation-order differences. Reasoning models reportedly follow constraints better when the constraint is placed in the chain of thought as a self-instruction. Chris Potts pushes back on claims that Nick Cammarata's essay "solves" interpretability x. MIT Technology Review quotes Potts directly: "As far as I can tell, nothing about this blog post makes the problems any easier" (MIT Technology Review) technologyreview.

Why it matters. There are two operational lessons for anyone running agent infrastructure. First, a hosted console that stores users' provider keys is a high-value target, and the safer design is the one Raad pointed to: keys entered in the local app "never come to us", per other coverage. If you must store keys, scope them and set spend caps. Second, if your incident-response plan uses a frontier model to read attacker payloads, expect refusals at the worst moment. Pre-arrange a vetted-access tier or keep a capable open-weight model you can run yourself. OSS Scanner points to the defender-side version of the same capability, but "model-rated" severity at that volume will create triage load for maintainers before it creates security.

Claude Managed Agents adds "dreaming" (saved 5 June 2026; launched 6 May 2026)

4 sources, 2 min read

What it said. This is an old item, revisited. Anthropic launched "dreaming" in Claude Managed Agents as a research preview: "a scheduled process" that "reviews agent sessions and memory stores, extracts patterns, and curates memories so agents improve over time", with the option to auto-apply changes or review them first. Neuronal heterogeneity's "Goldilocks" dose

1 source, 2 min read

Natalia Mesa's piece in The Transmitter, dated 20 October 2026, reports a shift in theoretical neuroscience. Computational models usually treat neurons as identical, but recent work suggests heterogeneity in connectivity and firing can make networks more flexible and better at encoding, with effects that depend on the computation and on how the heterogeneity is structured (The Transmitter) thetransmitter.

The studies it rests on vary in strength, so here they are one by one. A purely computational PNAS paper by Gast and colleagues found that neurons differing in excitability produced shallower attractor "valleys", fewer stable memories, more flexible switching and richer activity that improved decoding. A follow-up preprint from Gast's group (arXiv 2607.09516v2) built networks from firing-threshold measurements of mouse cortical neurons and found fewer distinct stable states but lower input needed to switch. Mazzucato and colleagues, in Neuron in December 2025, found pupil-linked arousal predicted auditory-cortex population activity best at intermediate levels. In their network model, too little heterogeneity impaired switching, too much pushed the network into chaos, and an intermediate amount sharpened tone discrimination. The idea that norepinephrine might tune heterogeneity is presented as a possibility. A Mazzucato preprint (arXiv 2603.04149) using mouse connectomics found wide variation in incoming connections, which produced a mix of timescales. A June 2025 study by Tavoni and colleagues used a Hopfield network. Varied connectivity alone hurt memory capacity, and so did varied coding level alone, but combining them restored it: when highly connected neurons also had high coding levels, performance roughly matched a homogeneous network (The Transmitter).

The disagreements are stated. Martin Nawrot argues some heterogeneity "always makes things better", which contrasts with Tavoni's view that effects depend on context. Scott Rich notes the thalamus is largely homogeneous, possibly because gating does not need variability, and that even heterogeneity-focused studies often implement it "somewhat ad hoc" rather than from measured biology, calling that "the next step". Several cited works are preprints or unpublished workshop data (The Transmitter).

For whole-brain emulation, this matters in a specific way. If heterogeneity is functional rather than noise, an emulation that uses population-average neuron parameters could reproduce connectivity and still get the dynamics wrong. It could get stuck in attractors, or tip into chaos, depending on which side of the sweet spot the averaging lands on. The Tavoni result implies the correlations between heterogeneities, such as connectivity covarying with coding level, matter as well as their spread. Connectomes alone will not provide those correlations; matched physiology is needed. Two questions stay open. The work does not show which heterogeneity statistics must be preserved for a given computation. And most results come from reduced models, not detailed biophysical ones.

Ways of thinking

The thinking pick, "Mental Models in the Agentic Era", re-reads fifteen established product and economic mental models for probabilistic systems. Per the summary, its argument is that "classic principles still apply" but "yield drastically different outcomes in probabilistic environments", with changes in unit economics, compounding errors, the jagged frontier and shifting bottlenecks. It argues these call for new evaluation frameworks, "autonomy dials" and editing skills substack. The full article was not read; what follows takes up two of the named ideas, compounding error and the autonomy dial, and tests them against today's news.

Compounding error is the simplest of the fifteen and the most often ignored. A deterministic pipeline that works once works every time. A probabilistic step that succeeds most of the time, chained many times, fails most of the time unless something resets the error. Every agent story today is in part about that reset mechanism. Harness2 gets its biggest gains not from a smarter model but from probing variants and composing a harness from the contrasts. That is error correction at the scaffold level, and its authors concede that "execution stochasticity and shared model biases limit the reliability of the evidence" (arXiv) x. Cloudflare's Clairic takes the opposite approach to compounding. It removes the generation steps entirely, answers in one forward pass, and recommends letting code do multi-step arithmetic because "Longer chains of dependent steps are hard for any one-pass model without a scratchpad" (Cloudflare) cloudflare. Meta's thin orchestrator makes the same move structurally: a router "without performing any of the domain work itself", so routing errors and domain errors do not multiply fb.

The autonomy dial is the more useful idea for product design. It reframes the choice from "agent or not" to "how much unsupervised action at this step". Today's examples show the dial set wrong in both directions. The Grok finance agent was set read-only and still leaked bank data to a second agent with Slack access techdirt. The setting described the agent's intent, not the system's actual permissions. OpenCode's defenders had their models' autonomy turned down by the vendor's guardrails at the moment they needed it turned up x. Clairic offers a cleaner pattern: return confidence, p_null and an interval with every answer, and let the product decide per field whether to automate or route to review (Cloudflare). That is an autonomy dial expressed as data rather than policy.

Taken together, the mental model is this. In probabilistic systems, design the reset points (verification, deterministic runners, narrow routers) and the dial (per-step confidence that gates autonomy) before you optimise the model. An early agentic-monitoring prototype is a small case of both. Agents write monitoring specs, a deterministic runner executes them, another agent investigates triggers, and humans are escalated to at the end benjamincongdon.
