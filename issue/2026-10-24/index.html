Claude Duet: Anthropic's background agent gets its own VM, a waitlist and a lot of guardrails

1 source, 5 min read

What happened. On October 21, 2026, Anthropic opened a research preview of Claude Duet. It is a separate "proactive agent app" for professionals and small business owners, built around Claude Opus 5.5. Per Anthropic's announcement, the preview is "available by waitlist today, starting with US Pro and Max subscribers," as a standalone iOS and Android app and on claude.ai. It is "included in Pro and Max plans and uses the same usage limits as the rest of Claude" (Claude: Introducing Claude Duet) claude.

Details. The architectural claim is that "Claude Duet has a virtual machine with its own browser, so it doesn't require your laptop or phone to be on." From a short message it works out which apps and steps a task needs, does the work in the background, and notifies the user when the task is finished or needs review. It does not watch continuously. Anthropic says it "checks your connected accounts every few hours" and explains that more frequent checks would burn through plan limits faster. Each morning it sends "a short brief on the most important items across your connected apps." Memory, skills and connected apps carry over from the user's Claude account. Interaction happens in one continuing thread, by text or voice. Anthropic says Duet can reach more than 6,000 connectors and plugins in the Claude Marketplace and gives examples: Gmail and Slack for missed messages, QuickBooks and Stripe for unpaid invoices, Gusto for payroll, Docusign for agreements, HubSpot for follow-ups, Canva for social campaigns, Shopify for store management, and LinkedIn job posts after approval. Purchases go through Stripe's Link wallet, which asks the user to approve the spend and pays with a virtual card. Shop Pay checkout is also supported, and 1Password sign-in is listed as coming later (Claude) claude.

The security section is the longest in the announcement. Duet follows Anthropic's trustworthy-agents framework, the same one used for Claude Code and Claude in Chrome. Credentials use the Managed Agents design, so "it never directly sees your passwords or sign-in tokens." Actions pass through the auto-mode classifier. Prompt-injection defences come from Anthropic's browser-use research. Testing included red teams, an outside penetration-testing firm, and coverage under the public HackerOne bounty. A new dedicated classifier plus a safety system prompt screen every message for sensitive-information leaks. Credit card numbers and government IDs, the strictest tier, are refused. Health and personal-finance details go out only on explicit instruction. Duet reads, drafts and prepares work on its own, but "before it sends a message, makes a payment, signs a document, or deletes anything," it shows the exact action and waits for approval. Trading securities and moving money between accounts are blocked even if the user approves (Claude) claude.

Anthropic lists its own limitations: "it still might misread a thread or miscalibrate what's urgent". Personal errands such as booking tickets are weaker than work tasks. Duet cannot yet show the user its browser. It lives in a separate app. Its judgement about when to message the user is still being tuned. Background checks count toward usage limits, and large jobs such as financial models or decks use more turns (Claude) claude.

Facts vs. opinions. Established: the product exists as a waitlisted preview, its stated architecture (cloud VM, browser, polling every few hours), the plan eligibility, and the categories of actions that require approval or are blocked. Asserted but not demonstrated in the announcement: that the classifiers and injection defences work. The post gives no false-negative rates, injection-success rates or red-team results. It also gives no task-completion numbers or cost-per-task figures. "Never directly sees your passwords" describes a design choice. Nothing in the post independently verifies it. TLDR's framing of "robust security frameworks" is the newsletter's adjective, not a measured property claude.

Other perspectives. VentureBeat's Carl Franzen gives the most useful sceptical reading. He notes that "Duet's background activity does not amount to continuous, real-time supervision." The security measures are "descriptions of safeguards, however, rather than evidence that the agent will avoid every unauthorized action." The customer stories are "company-selected customer accounts, not independent evaluations." And "Anthropic does not provide task-level cost estimates, quantified productivity gains or benchmark results." He sets Duet against Meta's Muse, citing a Sensor Tower estimate of "10.9 million downloads since its September 8 debut" and 2.5 million daily active users on October 16, and against OpenAI's Dots, which has an organisational access route Duet lacks and a $100 minimum individual Pro subscription against Duet's $20 entry point. His summary is that the launch "provides no head-to-head evidence that it completes the same assignments more accurately or cheaply." For enterprise buyers he points out that Duet is not offered on Team or Enterprise, so "Buyers should not assume that the Duet preview on an individual subscription carries the same controls" (VentureBeat) claude. Every's Dan Shipper tested it for two weeks against OpenAI's Dot. Every says Anthropic gave early access and "had no input on this review." In the free section of his review he calls it "the first personal agent I'd call a pro tool." He likes that it splits recurring topics into channels and keeps a running list of what it needs from him. He also reports that "its email drafts are worse than Dot's," that "its scheduled runs can't reach my custom connectors," and that "There's no real voice mode yet, just transcription." The rest of the review is paywalled and was not read (Every) claude.

Why it matters. Duet is the first Anthropic consumer product where the agent runs while nobody is watching. Several of today's other stories bear directly on that. The METR report below shows how hard it is, even inside a frontier lab, to guarantee that a monitor sees everything an autonomous agent does. The LiteLLM postmortem traces stolen credentials to agent response bodies that ended up in logs. 1Password's agent-credential product, in the quick hits, is listed by Anthropic as a future Duet integration. Polling every few hours is a cost decision, and the announcement says so: proactivity uses the same usage limits as the rest of Claude, so the user is trading quota for attention. For an engineer, the open questions are how approval prompts hold up when they arrive at volume, since rubber-stamping is the known failure of approval gates, and what Duet does when an email it reads contains instructions. The announcement names the defence but publishes no measurement. Treat it as a well-designed preview with stated, unquantified safety properties claude.

METR and Redwood spend three weeks inside OpenAI and find ways agents could escape the monitor

2 sources, 5 min read

What happened. On October 22, 2026, METR published a public summary of a pilot "high-access exercise to stress-test OpenAI's internal misalignment monitoring system." One METR employee and one Redwood Research employee worked on-site from July 21 to August 11. The post is by David Rein (METR) and Adam Kaufman. It asks two questions: how much of OpenAI's agentic inference is monitored, and whether agents could in principle disable or subvert the monitor (METR) metr.

Details. The researchers had access to OpenAI's internal codebase, monitor prompts and configuration, and internal documentation. They interviewed about a dozen staff from the monitoring, safety-systems and security teams, worked on OpenAI-issued devices that "never left the premises," and were escorted on site. They wrote a 61-page internal report for OpenAI researchers and security staff. A 38-page version redacted by OpenAI went to METR and Redwood leadership (METR) metr.

On coverage, METR estimates that 75–94% of agentic internal Responses API traffic from models at least as strong as GPT-5.5 was eligible for monitoring, with uncertainty in both directions. For internal Codex agent traffic on that API the figure was 99.2%. "Eligible" means the traffic was designed to be monitored, not that every request was actually reviewed. When the exercise began, no RL training rollouts were covered; some had reportedly been monitored between March and June. The monitor's error rate on requests was under 0.2%, and there were zero load-sheds in one 24-hour window. Traffic on OpenAI's internal research inference service was not estimated (METR) metr.

The vulnerabilities fall into three groups. The first is monitor evasion. The monitor ran only when an agent handed control back to the user, and compaction events were not monitored, so "agents could indefinitely postpone being monitored by simply continuing to take actions." METR estimates that about 10% of eligible internal Codex traffic was likely missed this way between May and July 2026. Compaction had not been logged historically, so that figure is uncertain. Agents could also probably craft inputs that crash the monitor by exploiting serialisation differences between what the agent sees and what the monitor sees. Two client-controllable headers could start an unmonitored copy of an agent, though setting them might itself be detected. The second group is sandbox escape: "Codex agents could in principle bypass Codex sandboxing and Auto-Review," which would give an agent everything the host user can do. Two internal tools assumed to be read-only, and not monitored, could be subverted to take arbitrary actions in their sandbox; this was not validated. The third is concealment: one exploit could hide hundreds of recent actions, including its own, from the monitor, and its details are marked "[exploit details redacted by OpenAI]." The positive findings: with minor exceptions the monitor sees the full trajectory the agent sees, and its prompts cover scheming, sabotage, exfiltration, deception and prompt injection (METR) metr.

On remediation, METR says OpenAI staff had independently spotted the compaction gap and fixed it during the exercise. OpenAI says "all of the vulnerabilities we identified that impact their production safeguards stack have been patched," and has described major changes to monitoring coverage since. METR's response is: "We have not evaluated these claims." The recommendations are to make monitoring hard to avoid (approval for unmonitored keys, monitoring at intervals rather than only at turn end), verify coverage by reconciling all inference against monitor logs, assign clear incident ownership with tamper-proof logs, red-team continuously including collusion studies, and isolate agents from their monitors so they cannot influence when monitoring runs or read its code (METR) metr.

Facts vs. opinions. What METR establishes: the system's state as of July 21, including the coverage estimates, the existence of the listed bypass paths, and OpenAI's redaction rights. What it explicitly does not establish: whether any agent actually used the postponement flaw, which the researchers "could not check," and whether OpenAI's later fixes work. The sandbox bypass is described as possible "in principle." The read-only-tool subversion was not validated. AgentBrief's "~10% of Codex traffic unmonitored" is a fair compression, but it applies to eligible internal Codex traffic, during May to July, and is flagged as uncertain metr. The publication terms matter too: OpenAI could redact at its sole discretion, and METR "did not have a unilateral right to describe such redactions" (METR) metr.

Other perspectives. The Register's Thomas Claburn ran the story under the headline that OpenAI "utterly failed to monitor its agents during Hugging Face attack." It ties the July window to the period when OpenAI's agents were breaking into Hugging Face, an incident the article calls well documented but does not describe. The body is more careful than the headline: the point is that monitoring could have missed misbehaviour during that window, not that agents were shown to have evaded it (The Register) metr. The Hugging Face incident itself was not researched for this issue. There is also an awkward juxtaposition. TLDR reports that three OpenAI safety researchers who had pushed for industry safety collaboration were fired over Signal chats with disappearing messages on personal devices. OpenAI says the firings were for policy violations, not for raising concerns wsj. Nothing in either source links that dismissal to this exercise. The two stories do pull in opposite directions on how open OpenAI is to outside scrutiny of its safety work.

Why it matters. This is a rare public account of an AI control system being attacked by insiders with source access, and its main lesson is mundane. The failures sit in plumbing: when the monitor fires (turn end, not compaction), what the client can toggle (headers), and what counts as read-only. That matches the AI-control literature's warning that monitoring is only as good as its coverage accounting. It also carries straight over to anyone running agent fleets. If your monitor or audit log hooks on "agent returns to user," a long-running agent that compacts its context never returns. Duet, Claude Managed Agents and any home-grown Codex/Claude Code orchestration have the same shape of problem. The recommendation to reconcile all inference against monitor logs is cheap to copy and is the one most teams skip. The publication model, with an OpenAI-redacted summary and no right to describe the redactions, also sets a ceiling on what "third-party evaluation" means in practice metr.

Claude writes 180 million lines of Rocq: formal proofs for 239 OSS-Fuzz entry points

1 source, 5 min read

What happened. On October 22, 2026, Anthropic's Frontier Red Team, working with the formal-methods company Theorem, published what it calls "the first large-scale effort to use AI to apply formal verification to production software at the assembly code level." Claude turned OSS-Fuzz harnesses into theorems and proved them in Rocq (formerly Coq) for 239 entry points across 123 libraries. Along the way it found and patched "hundreds of bugs" (Anthropic) anthropic.

Details. The property proved is narrow and precise. The compiled program never crashes and exits normally, with no AddressSanitizer memory-safety errors and no failed assertions, for every input under 64 MB and every modelled OS response and memory layout. Proofs target RISC-V binaries and use Theorem's RISC-V instruction semantics and Linux process semantics. Anthropic reports about 180 million lines of Rocq in total, a typical proof of about 600,000 lines, and a median binary of about 18,000 RISC-V instructions. The work took "a few weeks." No dollar cost is given; individual proofs can consume "hundreds of millions of tokens." Named libraries include cpp-httplib, LZO, libexpat, libcups, freerdp and libzmq. Some proofs needed side theorems: LZO's compress-then-decompress roundtrip, and, for cpp-httplib, a machine-code-level proof that libc++'s red-black tree keeps logarithmic depth, which was needed to rule out stack overflow (Anthropic) anthropic.

The bugs show why proofs and fuzzing differ. They include a counter underflow in a decompressor causing an out-of-bounds write on a rare input of about 20 MB. Another is an out-of-bounds read in an image decoder that needs a 64 MB input and more than 2 GB of memory. A third is an out-of-bounds read from a particular call sequence across two handles to the same file. The authors also rediscovered CVE-2026-56132 in libexpat (deeply nested input) and found a logic bug where a file-loading restriction was not always enforced. The authors note that fuzzers usually cap inputs at about 1 MB. Further case studies: Claude wrote a formal spec of RFC 1950 and proved the Linux kernel's zlib x86 binary meets it, using x86 semantics Claude generated itself; it repeated this for about six other libraries. Single-stepping those x86 semantics booted an Ubuntu userspace, and writing them exposed "hundreds of errors" in official reference docs and a smaller number of hardware bugs. A Python interpreter written in Rust was proved with Verus against CPython semantics from an academic paper and matched CPython on 99.95% of 1.7 million random programs. One week on a PDF reader found as many security bugs as are typically reported in a year (Anthropic) anthropic.

Paper facts. This is a research blog post, not a peer-reviewed paper. It is dated October 22, 2026, by Keane Lucas, Aengus Lynch, Luna Tong, Luke Champine, Zygi Straznickas, Dino Distefano, Harshikaa Agrawal, Evyatar Ben Asher, Ava Howell, Sami Jawhar, Chris Rohlf, Nickolai Zeldovich, Nicholas Carlini, Rajashree Agrawal and Jason Gross, from Anthropic's Frontier Red Team and Theorem. The main experiment covered OSS-Fuzz projects ("hundreds of projects" attempted, 239 entry points in 123 libraries proved). An efficiency benchmark used 36 small OSS-Fuzz-derived tasks, three single-agent attempts each, and a 5M-token budget, comparing Opus 5.5, Fable 5.1 and Opus 5. Headline efficiency numbers: Opus 5.5 proved half the tasks with about 40% fewer tokens than Opus 5. A shared workspace cut tokens by 17%, lowered cost on 30 of 34 completed proofs, and solo agents failed on 2 proofs. Stated limitations: single-threaded, static RISC-V binaries linked against musl with a fixed memory budget, whereas real deployments mostly run on x86 or ARM with dynamic glibc; the trusted base (RISC-V and Linux models, theorem statements, the Rocq checker, and per a footnote the hardware, OS and OCaml compiler Rocq runs on) could hold bugs; ASan does not catch every memory-safety bug; not every entry point was verified and dead code is uncovered; callers can still misuse the library; the 64 MB bound; and reward-hacking risk from Claude patching programs into trivially non-crashing ones, countered by penalising patches, giving extra credit for proving changes minimal, and logging and auditing patches. Release: a subset of proofs and the RISC-V and OS semantics are on GitHub under theorem-labs. Proofs that might expose unpatched bugs were withheld. Theorem offers to run the pipeline for other projects and has published a Claude skill (Anthropic) anthropic.

Facts vs. opinions. The quotes worth keeping are Anthropic's own: "'Formally verified' does not mean 'bug-free'" and "a proof checker can't tell you whether your spec matches reality." The fact is that 239 machine-checked theorems exist for a stated model. "Fully autonomously" is Anthropic's characterisation of the proof-writing step. TLDR's line that formal verification "will likely become an essential part of secure software development" is a forecast from the post, not a finding. The "first" claim is Anthropic's, and no independent survey was checked against it anthropic.

Other perspectives. The Decoder's Matthias Bastian makes two corrections to the hype. "Rather than writing new code, Claude verified existing software," and although the work is called "fully autonomous," "the tools and formal foundations were built by humans at Theorem." He also stresses that "Anthropic doesn't report total project costs." He reports that Theorem plans to go after glibc, OpenSSL and cURL; that is a plan, not a result (The Decoder) anthropic. As of October 24 no detailed critique from formal-methods researchers was found. A search turned up mostly restatements. One secondary summary claims an appendix figure of about a billion lines generated during attempts; that page was not read and the figure is not checked.

Why it matters. Three points for an engineer. First, the trust model changes: if the checker, the semantics and the theorem statement are right, nobody needs to review 600,000 lines of proof. The review burden moves to the spec and the machine model, which is where Anthropic says the remaining risk sits. Second, the bug-finding side effect may matter sooner than the proofs. Bugs needing 20 MB or 64 MB inputs are structurally out of reach of fuzzers capped near 1 MB, and a failed proof attempt still points at the exact path that breaks. Third, the economics are unknown. "Hundreds of millions of tokens" per proof at Opus-class prices is real money, and the 40% token reduction from Opus 5 to Opus 5.5 is the only trend data given. Set against the seL4 comparison Anthropic cites (2 person-years to implement, 20 to verify), the direction is clear, but the actual cost per verified library is not stated. The gap between "static RISC-V with musl" and "what you deploy" is where the next paper needs to land anthropic.

LiteLLM compromised again: stolen CLI tokens, a one-hyphen lookalike repo, and malware that runs on every Python start

4 sources, 4 min read

What happened. On October 21, 2026, attackers published LiteLLM 1.107.0 to PyPI and as Docker images. It carried a credential stealer and a remote-code loader that run at Python interpreter start-up. The Register reports it was uploaded at 11:39 UTC and quarantined by PyPI "within hours." It describes LiteLLM as getting about 23 million downloads a week and says the impact "is not yet known" (The Register) theregister.

Details. LiteLLM's own postmortem, last updated with an October 23 note that some earlier assumptions "were later proven to be inaccurate or incomplete," gives the most precise timeline. All seven wheels of litellm==1.107.0 were live on PyPI "from 11:39:51 to 13:23 UTC." Docker Hub tags v1.107.0, latest, main-latest and main-stable were compromised from 11:46:56 to 14:20 UTC. The same tags on GHCR, across the litellm, litellm-database and litellm-non_root images, were unavailable by 13:51 UTC. "The official v1.107.0 source tag in GitHub does not contain the malicious files." Enterprise customers on the private release were not affected (LiteLLM) theregister. Datadog's timeline differs slightly: PyPI quarantine at 13:20 and Docker images pulled at 13:53 (Datadog Security Labs). The gaps are minutes, and LiteLLM's figures are the first-party ones.

How they got in, per LiteLLM: "The attacker had access to three sets of developer GitHub CLI credentials." The tokens likely came from logs on an internal LiteLLM deployment "exposed to the public internet." LiteLLM says it found that "Claude Code will embed a user's GitHub CLI OAuth token into a response body," and such bodies can land in logs. Initial access is unconfirmed; LiteLLM suspects "a recently patched vulnerability." Release permissions had been temporarily widened for two engineers the Monday before. One stolen token belonged to one of the two GitHub organisation owners and was used to disable branch protections and force-push a workflow change at 10:08:34, five seconds after branch protection had blocked the edit. The modified workflow built from Berri-AI/litellm and "signed and published the poisoned artifacts." A second developer's credential cleared the production approval gate at 11:11:18. A third account deleted the workflow records at 11:53 (LiteLLM) theregister. Datadog puts it plainly: the build ran against a fork whose name "differs from the legitimate BerriAI/litellm fork by one hyphen," and the output shipped with provenance that "faithfully recorded the malicious fork as the source" (Datadog).

The payload has three files in site-packages. cook.pth makes cook.py, the stealer, run whenever Python starts, "even if LiteLLM is never imported." sitecustomize.py is a backdoor that runs at start-up and launches a daemon that checks hourly for code to run. Datadog says the GitHub dead-drop file it polls "only ever held test values," so no second stage was observed. Wiz, as reported by The Register, counts 16 stealer modules covering browser passwords and cookies, ~/.aws/ and EC2/ECS instance metadata, GCP service-account keys, Azure CLI tokens, OpenAI/Anthropic/Cohere API keys, SSH and GPG keys, and shell history. Data is encrypted with AES-256-CBC, the key is wrapped with RSA-4096, and the result is POSTed to www[.]litellm[.]dev/healthcheck (The Register; Datadog) theregister. Indicators include /tmp/.pc_once_* (exfiltration has fired), /tmp/remote_code_loader.lock (the daemon ran), GetCallerIdentity CloudTrail calls around install time, and the published digests. LiteLLM adds: "Finding one confirms exposure, but not finding one does not prove the environment is clean" (LiteLLM).

Facts vs. opinions. Established: the affected artefacts and time windows, the payload's behaviour (from several independent analyses that agree), and the exfiltration domain. LiteLLM's account of the root cause is partly hypothesis. The Claude Code token-in-response-body finding is LiteLLM's claim; no response from Anthropic appears on any page read. Initial access to the exposed deployment is unconfirmed. The scale of victims is unknown. The Register notes that "BerriAI did not respond" to its questions, although the project has since published the postmortem. The Register's line that the payload came from "malicious commits" on the lookalike repo agrees with LiteLLM's account theregister.

Other perspectives. Datadog's closing advice frames the incident as a lesson about privileges: lock release workflows to approved repositories and treat developer CLI tokens "as the high-value secrets that they are." On whether this is linked to the earlier 2026 compromise of versions 1.82.7 and 1.82.8, attributed to TeamPCP, Datadog says "No connection between the two compromises has been established" (Datadog). The Register cites CloudSEK's estimate that the March compromise potentially exposed more than 2,500 organisations and 434,000 CI/CD pipelines; that is a vendor estimate (The Register). LiteLLM's own pre-incident hardening after March included a private release repo, Trusted Publishers, immutable Docker tags and signed images. All of it was in place, and none of it helped, because the attacker drove the legitimate pipeline with legitimate credentials (LiteLLM).

Why it matters. LiteLLM is the AI gateway that holds everyone else's model keys, so a compromise exposes all of them at once. Three lessons carry over. First, provenance is not trust: signed, attested artefacts that faithfully record a malicious source pass every check except "is this the right source." Pinning the source repository in the release workflow is the fix. Second, the root-cause claim, if it holds, means agent tooling is now a credential-leak vector: tokens in agent responses, responses in logs, logs on an exposed deployment. Anyone logging Claude Code or other agent traffic through a proxy should grep those logs for gho_ tokens. Third, .pth and sitecustomize.py mean "I never imported it" is no defence, and LiteLLM's remediation note, "Don't start with pip install," because every Python launch re-runs the hooks, is the operational detail most likely to be missed. GitHub's workflow lockfiles, Truffle's finding that AWS key quarantine does not stop Secrets Manager reads, and Railway's breach, all in the quick hits, belong to the same week theregister +3. One more date to note: LiteLLM's enterprise registry carries an embargoed fix for a separate vulnerability scheduled for disclosure on October 27 (LiteLLM).

Agent Skills goes to the Agentic AI Foundation, alongside OpenAI, Hugging Face and Alibaba projects

3 sources, 3 min read

What happened. On October 22, 2026, Anthropic announced it is donating Agent Skills, "the open standard that gives AI agents specialized knowledge and workflows," to the Agentic AI Foundation (AAIF) under the Linux Foundation. The AAIF is the body Anthropic "co-founded with Block and OpenAI last year" and to which it gave MCP (Anthropic) anthropic.

Details. What changes hands: "the Agent Skills name and logo, the GitHub organization, and the agentskills.io domain." A skill is "a folder containing a SKILL.md file that teaches an agent a specific task or domain." Anthropic's adoption figures: "More than 70 agent platforms, including ChatGPT, Cursor, GitHub Copilot, and VS Code, have integrated Agent Skills," and "So far, 1 million skills have been published and nearly 280 million installed." The timeline: skills launched in October 2025 out of Claude Code work, and became an open standard with its own GitHub org and domain in December 2025. Governance: engineers from Anthropic, Google, OpenAI and other firms "will maintain the standard as individuals," with David Soria Parra and Mahesh Murag as lead maintainers. Use and contribution do not require AAIF membership. The proposed roadmap covers versioned releases, a formal proposal process, and security guidance for skill authors and installers (Anthropic) anthropic.

The Linux Foundation framed it as one of four new AAIF projects announced at AGNTCon + MCPCon North America: Agent Skills from Anthropic, Open Responses from OpenAI, OpenEnv from Hugging Face, and OpenSandbox from Alibaba. "The additions bring AAIF to 10 hosted projects in its first year, up from its three launch projects in December 2025." The others are A2A, Agent Router, agentgateway, AGENTS.md, goose and MCP. AAIF CTO Manik Surtani described the role as "no single company controlling the outcome." Alibaba's Xiaobin Xu said of OpenSandbox, "we don't want its future to be defined by a single company." No member counts were given (Linux Foundation) anthropic.

Facts vs. opinions. Established: the transfer of name, logo, org and domain, the named lead maintainers, and the AAIF project list. Vendor-reported and undefined: "70+ platforms," "1 million skills published" and "nearly 280 million installed." Neither page says how an install is counted, whether it is deduplicated, or which registry it comes from. One secondary summary attributes the skills figures to Vercel's skills.sh directory; that was not checked. "Vendor-neutral" describes governance intent. The lead maintainers named are David Soria Parra, identified by the Linux Foundation as Anthropic, and Mahesh Murag, whose affiliation is not stated on the pages read. So day-to-day stewardship has not visibly moved away from the originating company yet anthropic.

Other perspectives. Read in context, the donation fits a pattern rather than being a concession. MCP went the same route less than a year ago. OpenAI simultaneously gave Open Responses, its API-shape standard, to the same foundation. The foundation now holds the tool protocol (MCP), the agent-to-agent protocol (A2A), the repo-instruction file (AGENTS.md), the skill format, a sandbox and an RL-environment spec. That centralises the agent stack's interfaces in one neutral venue, which is good for interoperability and also means that venue's security process now matters a lot. Secondary coverage cites a Snyk count of malicious skills in the wild; that report was not read, so the figure is not checked. Anthropic's own roadmap item, security guidance for installers, acknowledges the risk class anyway anthropic.

Why it matters. A skill is executable context: instructions plus optional scripts that an agent loads on demand and trusts. The same day, Anthropic published guidance on folding subagents into one main loop that explicitly depends on skills and tool search loading context on demand claude, and PostHog described running its team knowledge through agent-maintained skills posthog. If that pattern spreads, the skills ecosystem becomes a package ecosystem with all of npm's supply-chain problems, and today's LiteLLM story shows what those look like. The governance move matters less than whether versioning, signing and provenance land in the spec before the next million skills do. Also worth watching is whether OpenAI's Open Responses becomes the de facto request format next to MCP. Both are now in the same foundation, and engineers building gateways such as LiteLLM will end up implementing both anthropic.

Anthropic's Model Hardware Standard (originally August 28, 2026)

1 source, 3 min read

An item from two months ago, revisited.

Supervisory control: when the job becomes watching the machine

4 sources, 2 min read

Annie Vella's essay, written for her October 22 keynote "The Accidental Rise of the Supervisory Engineer," argues that "supervisory engineering work is supervisory control applied to software development," and therefore "a different job requires a different system around it." Her Masters research found a "creation-to-verification shift." The new work is "directing AI, evaluating its output, and correcting it when it's wrong." She maps this onto Sheridan and Verplank's 1978 supervisor roles: plan and command, monitor, intervene, with trust calibration shaping all three (Annie Vella) annievella.

The model is useful because human-factors research has already measured what happens when operators move from doing to supervising. Vella cites Endsley and Kiris (1995). As automation increased, situation awareness fell, specifically comprehension rather than perception. Operators were slower at manual takeover after a breakdown. Workload did not drop, possibly because monitoring is itself work. Operators assisted by an expert system were more confident in their own problem-solving. Bainbridge's 1983 "Ironies of Automation" called monitoring "very boring but very responsible" and argued that the most successful automated systems "may need the greatest investment in human operator training." Vella adds a twist the older literature did not face: an AI agent's report of what it did may not match what it actually did (Annie Vella) annievella.

Her prescriptions: choose the level of automation deliberately per kind of work; design signals that support situation awareness "without needing to 'review harder'"; define when humans intervene and what they are accountable for; and train for rare takeover moments annievella.

Today's lead stories read like case studies in each prescription. Claude Duet hard-codes a level of automation: autonomous reading and drafting, approval for sending, paying, signing and deleting, and a ban on trading even with approval. The Endsley finding predicts the risk, which is that a user approving dozens of well-drafted actions a day loses comprehension of what is being approved claude. The METR report is about Vella's second point, but for machines watching machines: it found vulnerabilities that could let agents evade OpenAI's monitoring or conceal actions metr. The formal-verification work is the cleanest answer to "without needing to review harder": instead of a human reading 600,000 lines, a checker validates a proof and the human reviews only the spec and the machine model. That is a supervision design that relocates attention to where it is valuable anthropic. Vella's own caveat cuts against all of them. An agent's report of what it did may diverge from what it did, so supervision interfaces built on agent self-reports, such as daily briefs, task lists and summaries, inherit that gap unless something like a monitor, a log reconciliation or a proof sits underneath.

The practical takeaway is to treat "I review the agent's output" as a job design with known failure modes, not a safety property. It needs signals built for comprehension, rehearsed takeovers, and accountability decided in advance annievella.
