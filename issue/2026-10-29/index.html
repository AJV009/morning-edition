Claude refutes the Exponential Time Hypothesis, according to Anthropic, with a Lean proof whose statement still needs human checking

2 sources, 5 min read

What happened. Anthropic has published a folder in its formal-math repository. It contains a paper and a Lean 4 formalization claiming that "A deterministic algorithm solves 3-SAT on n variables in time 2^o(n), which refutes the Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH)." The README says the algorithm in fact solves the harder counting problem, #3-SAT, "in time 2^O(n log log n / √log n)," and that "The result was discovered by an internal research Claude model" (GitHub: anthropics/formal-math/eth). Scientific American reports that this came "Mere hours after" OpenAI announced three claimed proofs, one of them its own refutation of ETH (Scientific American) github.

Details. ETH, proposed in 1999 according to Scientific American, conjectures that 3-SAT cannot be solved in time 2^o(n). A 2^o(n) algorithm would therefore make it false. The README is clear about scope: "The result is purely asymptotic and says nothing about solving formulas in practice." CellCog, a startup that discloses its agents run on Claude Opus 5.5, quotes the paper as saying that "the bound does not improve on exhaustive search until n is beyond 10^108 for any formula." By that account it is a 49-page paper "written by Claude with Anthropic researchers improving the presentation." CellCog also quotes the line "The core algorithm was found on October 16 by an internal Claude research model with 14M output tokens and no human input" (CellCog). The paper PDF itself was not read for this issue, so those details rest on CellCog's quotations. The README includes a "condensed key idea" that, it says, Claude Opus 5.5 at high effort can rederive the refutation from. The technique is recursive tensor-network contraction. Let e(K) be the exponent constant for contracting tensor networks on the complete graph K_K with λ bits per edge. The key lemma is "e(9K^16) ≤ 0.9·e(K) + O(1/K)." The trick writes each bit of a SWAP as "I − 2·singlet" and groups terms by how many singlets they contain, which gives "λ+1 operators with (row rank)·(column rank) ≤ 12^λ." The README adds that this condensed version yields only "that 3-SAT can be decided in time O(2^δn) for every δ > 0, not the sharper bounds at the top of this page." github

On the formal side, MainResults.lean states ETHIsFalse and the paper's Theorem 1 "on Mathlib's Turing machines." The README says the file "contains no proofs and imports nothing but Mathlib." It also says: "Lean checks the proofs, but no machine checks that this file says what the paper says; the file is kept short so that a reader can check this." The build "took about 8 minutes on 24 virtual CPUs," its largest process used "about 7 GB of memory," and it prints two sorry warnings, both from Challenge.lean, which states the theorems without proof for a comparison check. The expected axiom printout lists only propext, Classical.choice and Quot.sound, which the README calls "Lean's three standard axioms," with Lean and Mathlib pinned at v4.33.1. A fuller CI check uses a "Comparator" that matches the theorem statements against Challenge.lean and re-checks the proofs with "a second, independently written kernel, nanoda." github

Facts vs. opinions. Established: the repository exists, it builds as described (by Anthropic's account), and its axiom list is standard. Claimed but not independently verified: that the Lean statement of ETH faithfully captures ETH, that the paper's proof is correct, and that the discovery involved "no human input." That last point is Anthropic's characterisation of its own process, and the prompts and scaffolding behind the 14M output tokens are not described in what was read. Jelani Nelson, as quoted by CellCog, says he and others reviewed Claude's proof the week before release to improve its exposition. He also says "it seems both proofs ultimately get roughly exp(O(n / sqrt(log n))) time with lower order terms in the exponent" and that "it seems indeed the models even obtained the results on the same day." CellCog states plainly: "We have not checked the proof." github

Other perspectives. Scientific American reports that Russell Impagliazzo and Ramamohan Paturi, who proposed ETH, are working with Ryan Williams of MIT "to perform an independent investigation" of OpenAI's version, and that Williams says OpenAI will sponsor a conference on it. The same article says OpenAI's model "remains proprietary," with prompts, compute and "chain of thought outputs" unpublished. It also reports friction with AGMAI, a mathematics-and-AI advisory group, over OpenAI's release choices on the separate Yang-Mills claim. CellCog quotes Williams putting OpenAI's bound at "About exp(n / (log n)^(1/2+o(1)))." That would put the two bounds in the same family, consistent with Nelson's reading. OpenAI's own announcement page returned a 403 and could not be read, so OpenAI's claims here come only through Scientific American and CellCog. In what was read, no expert has said either proof is wrong, and none has said either is right (Scientific American; CellCog) github.

The sceptical points come from Anthropic's own README. First, the trusted surface is the statement file, not the 8-minute build. Formalizing "ETH is false" on Mathlib's Turing machines means encoding a time-complexity claim about a machine model. Subtle mismatches are possible in input encoding, the cost model, uniformity, or how "2^o(n)" is quantified, and the type-checker cannot catch them. Anthropic says Appendix E of the paper "prints it and lists how it differs from the printed statements." That is the right disclosure, and it is also an admission that differences exist. Second, two labs reaching structurally similar bounds on the same day is either strong corroboration, because independent derivations agree, or a sign of a shared, recently available ingredient that made the result reachable. The material read does not settle which. Third, the result has no practical effect, because the crossover is beyond n = 10^108 by the paper's own account. github

Why it matters. If it holds, this is the first refutation of a major complexity-theory conjecture credited to a machine. Hardness results conditioned on ETH would need to be re-derived under other assumptions. That last point is an inference about the field, not checked against a survey. For an AI engineer the more durable lesson is about verification. The artifact is honest about which part is machine-checked (the proofs) and which needs human eyes (a deliberately short statement file). Formal-math claims from labs should be read that way from now on: the build and the axiom printout are table stakes, and the review that matters is of the statement file. Separately, Axiom Math released the first complete computer-checked proof of the classification of finite simple groups (see Quick hits), which suggests agent-written Lean at scale is becoming routine. Watch for the Impagliazzo–Paturi–Williams readout. CellCog describes their verdict on whether both formal statements capture ETH as "the next real signal." github +1

Anthropic's drug-development report: six partners, one acquired biotech, all early-stage

3 sources, 4 min read

What happened. On October 27, Anthropic's science team published a progress report on using Claude across drug development. It covers six external partners, from modality engineering to clinical-trial planning, and an internal rare-disease programme built on the acquisition of Mithril Medicine, a one-person company working on two rare childhood forms of muscular dystrophy (Anthropic) anthropic +1.

Details. The partner results, as Anthropic reports them:

  • Generate Biomedicines had six scientists use Claude Code to propose redesigns of a CAR T-cell construct for solid tumours. The best design was about 70% shorter than the standard one. In lab tests, immune cells produced roughly 10× more receptors, killed tumour cells more efficiently and stayed active longer.
  • Divergence Labs targets food allergy by stripping IgE off immune cells. Claude turned the company's goals into a design plan and "largely autonomously" produced about 35,000 antibody designs in about a month. In early tests 3 designs bound IgE and 20 dislodged it, and 3 of those 20 appeared to dislodge more than the standard medication.
  • BigHat Biosciences connected Claude to its lab data system, Reccy. About 1,010 variants were made and tested in about 10 weeks. Midway, Claude and an Anthropic researcher proposed new stability metrics and a protocol change, and Anthropic says "the main predictions held." Against the starting leads, binding was more than 10× tighter, the cancer lead stayed in solution at temperatures 18°C higher, and the longer-lasting lead clumped more than 100× more slowly.
  • Enveda used Claude for manufacturing paperwork. Across 1,061 pages, "Claude caught all 11 issues that human reviewers had spotted—and flagged seven they'd missed," and human review time fell 89%, from more than 25 hours to under 3. One regulatory draft took 4 hours against about 3 months by hand. In another, Enveda's standard review found one minor error.
  • The Column Group is launching Pleiades, a company meant to use Claude to run more programmes than a small team could. Its first Claude skill, which assesses whether drug targets are worth pursuing, was evaluated on 45 target–disease pairs.
  • Bristol Myers Squibb uses Claude to review plans for an early-stage clinical drug and estimate trial success odds, with expansion planned for 2027. BMS executive Michael Pourdehnad is quoted: "From a single prompt, Claude generated a robust initial analysis."

anthropic

On the internal side, Anthropic says Mithril's founder, Cody Tranbarger, used Claude to coordinate contracts, logistics and experiments across 11 external labs. One candidate went from idea to promising results in mice in under 18 months, and Anthropic writes that "we expect a drug to be ready to start clinical trials in humans as soon as late 2028," conditional on the candidates holding up. The thread announcing the acquisition says the deal lets Mithril keep running its programmes with more resources, lets Tranbarger start several more, and aims to put the way of working "in the hands of all biopharma" anthropic +1.

Facts vs. opinions. Every number above is reported by Anthropic or attributed by Anthropic to a partner. None comes with a paper, a protocol or a control arm in the post. Several results are measured against the partner's own baseline ("the original leads", "the standard version") with no description of what a non-Claude team would have done in the same time. Anthropic's own caveats are explicit: "All of this research is still at an early stage," the work "so far it is mostly preclinical," and Divergence still has to combine its two arms into a single drug. The Enveda figure, all 11 human-found issues plus seven missed ones, is a single document set. The post does not say how the seven were confirmed as real issues. The "as soon as late 2028" date is a projection. anthropic

Other perspectives. Read with the S-1 (below), this is also positioning. Anthropic's prospectus, per The Next Web, reports first-half 2026 revenue of "$16.3bn" and top-five-customer concentration of about 25% (The Next Web). A credible life-sciences line diversifies a story that is otherwise mostly coding. The post also mentions a tension: Anthropic says its safeguards can hinder legitimate biology research, and a Life Sciences Verification Program gives vetted organisations "more permissive versions" of them. Anthropic built strict bio filtering as a safety commitment, and Zvi lists it among the Dario-driven sacrifices (next story). It is now tiering access to that filtering for commercial partners. No independent reaction from drug-discovery researchers was found in the time available. anthropic +1

Why it matters. The useful signal is where in the pipeline Claude is said to help. It is not mainly molecule generation, where specialised models already exist. It is operations: coordinating 11 labs, review paperwork, protocol critique, metric redesign mid-campaign. The post's own thesis is that "the bottlenecks are distributed all throughout the drug development process." That is a claim about agents as project managers for wet-lab work, and it can only be tested over years, by whether Mithril's candidate actually enters trials. For the reader's agent-design interests, BigHat's Claude-to-LIMS integration (Reccy) is the most concrete pattern: an agent with read and write access to the lab's system of record, proposing changes to the measurement protocol itself. That is powerful, and it is also how you get a model optimising the metric it chose. anthropic +1

The same week Anthropic filed its S-1 for a Nasdaq listing under the ticker ANT (The Next Web). Zvi explicitly defers the S-1 to a later post so he can "actually Do the Reading" wordpress.

Liquid AI's LFM3: a 1.7B tri-hybrid backbone and a benchmark table worth reading line by line

2 sources, 3 min read

What happened. On October 27, Liquid AI released LFM3, six models of about 2B parameters each, all derived from one backbone, LFM3-1.7B-Base. They cover text, vision, audio, omni and two "decision" models derived from the vision and omni variants (Liquid AI). Weights are on Hugging Face under the "LFM Open License v1.0," not Apache or MIT (Hugging Face: LFM3-1.7B) liquid.

Details. Architecture: "Of its 24 layers, 13 are convolutions, 5 are SWA with a 256-token window, and 6 are global attention." That is gated short convolutions with kernel size 7 (up from 3 in LFM2), sliding-window attention and global attention. Other components are per-head sigmoid gating on attention, partial RoPE (only the high-frequency half of each head is rotated in global layers), sandwich norm, and a 128K vocabulary. Liquid says it chose the design by hardware-in-the-loop search across 14 device and backend combinations, from Raspberry Pi 5 to Hexagon NPU. Pre-training used 22T tokens, with the final 3.5T as a mid-training stage that extends context to 256K. Post-training: SFT on 1.75T tokens, mixed RL with a length penalty, on-policy distillation from six specialist teachers, then agentic RL. Liquid reports a KV cache at 128K context of 1.6 GB, against 4.3 GB for Qwen3.5-4B and 5.6 GB for MiniCPM5-2B. It says that in llama.cpp at Q4_0 and 32K context the model decoded 1.8 to 2.8 times faster than the runner-up on all three tested devices, and it reports server throughput of 3.6B tokens per day on one NVIDIA B300 at 1,024 concurrent requests. The text model "fits in about 1 GB at 4-bit precision" liquid.

Facts vs. opinions. All the benchmarks are vendor-run. In Liquid's table, LFM3-1.7B averages 49.37 against 47.24 for Qwen3.5-9B and 39.27 for Qwen3.5-4B. The agentic numbers are the headline: WebArena-Verified 38.42 against 21.92 for Qwen3.5-4B, AutomationBench (public) 41.29 against 8.67, and LiveClaw 45.60 against 19.94. Four details in the same table temper this:

  • Knowledge is weaker. GPQA Diamond is 63.37 against 75.76 for Qwen3.5-4B, and Liquid acknowledges larger models still lead on knowledge and agentic coding.
  • Long-context retrieval is weaker than the 256K window suggests. RULER at 128K is 65.56 against 84.63 for Qwen3.5-4B, though AA-LCR is roughly at parity (52.83 against 52.67).
  • The AA-Omniscience score of 0.50, against -57.17 for Qwen3.5-4B, comes from abstention, not knowledge. Accuracy is 7.07, the lowest in the row, while the non-hallucination rate is 92.93. The model mostly declines to answer, which is a reasonable behaviour for a 1.7B model and should be read as such.
  • Liquid's SWE-bench Verified score of 43.40 sits beside its own model card saying the model is "not recommended for agentic coding or complex multi-step reasoning due to its small size" (Hugging Face).

The newsletter summary's claim that LFM3-VL-2B "matches Gemma 4 12B across 62 vision benchmarks" matches Liquid's blog, which says that across 62 vision benchmarks it "matches the performance of Gemma 4 12B" (an average of 71.4 against 71.7) (Liquid AI) liquid.

Other perspectives. On r/LocalLLaMA, the Hugging Face release thread drew 387 points at the time of reading. One commenter wrote "I hope real and not benchmaxxed," and another replied that it is "Definitely not better in general usage from their own benchmark" but good at what it targets. Another asked how much worse the VL and Omni variants are at text-only tasks, which the release does not say. These are opinions, not measurements. The model card shows 8,892 downloads last month at the time of reading, and a separate thread credits a zero-day llama.cpp release for the omni model liquid.

Why it matters. LFM3 is a concrete bet on the architecture Stratechery's archived "Inference Shift" essay (below) did not consider: agentic inference that runs on the device, where KV-cache memory is the binding constraint. A 1.6 GB cache at 128K is the number that matters for phones, more than any leaderboard average. The model is also post-trained for specific agent harnesses (Hermes Agent, Pi, OpenClaw), which is a sign that small open models are now tuned to particular scaffolds rather than to chat. That makes "fine-tunes on a single GPU" the real offer. The custom licence should be read before shipping liquid +1.

Google's "sleep" framework: continual learning via consolidation and dreaming, older than the blog post suggests

3 sources, 3 min read

What happened. On October 27, Google Research published a blog post by Ali Behrouz and Vahab Mirrokni presenting the "sleep framework." LLMs alternate between a wake phase, holding new information in fast short-term memory, and an offline sleep phase that consolidates it into parameters and then "dreams" to improve itself (Google Research). The underlying paper, "Language Models Need Sleep: Learning to Self-Modify and Consolidate Memories" (arXiv 2606.03979), was first posted on June 2, 2026, revised on July 10, and notes that "A version of this work has been publicly available from September 2025 on OpenReview" (arXiv) research.

Details. Stage 1, consolidation, works as follows. The model adds new low-rank experts into slower-updating memory blocks built on MoE plus Google's Hope architecture, leaving old parameters intact. It distils short-term content into them through Generalized Knowledge Distillation combined with RL-based imitation, where the student completes prefixes of teacher text and is rewarded for semantic similarity and Levenshtein closeness. It then resets the fast parameters ("synaptic pruning"). Stage 2, dreaming, deliberately routes generation through non-standard experts to mix domains, keeps the synthetic "dreams" most likely to help via gradient-based selection, and fine-tunes on the top-rated ones. The blog insists dreaming "is not simply self-generated data augmentation" and that the framework "operates entirely within a fixed model architecture and human-defined training rules" research.

Paper facts. Status: arXiv preprint (v1 June 2, 2026; v2 July 10, 2026), with an earlier OpenReview version from September 2025. No venue acceptance is listed on the abstract page. Authors: Ali Behrouz, Farnoosh Hashemi, Adel Javanmard, Vahab Mirrokni (Google, Cornell, USC). Models: Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-8B for maths, Llama-3B and Llama3-8B for class-incremental learning, Llama-3.2-1B for few-shot ARC. The BABILong and translation backbones are not stated in the portion read. Headline numbers (arXiv HTML):

  • Maths, Qwen3-8B, average@16. Sleep scores 79.2 on AIME-24, 69.0 on AIME-25 and 46.1 on HMMT-25, against GRPO at 76.4, 68.1 and 44.9. For Qwen3-1.7B, Sleep scores 53.2, 40.2 and 29.3 against OPSD at 51.6, 40.0 and 28.1.
  • SQuAD knowledge incorporation, no-context accuracy. Single passage, then continued pretraining on 200 passages and 974 questions: Sleep (four-level) 48.9 and 46.2, against SEAL at 46.7 and 43.2 and the base model at 31.9. The "without dreaming" ablation falls to 35.7 and 36.2.
  • ARC few-shot, 11 training and 8 held-out tasks: Sleep at 80% against SEAL at 72.5%, TTT at 10% and ICL at 0%.
  • BABILong appears only as a figure. The paper's text attributes the "almost perfect score in scaling to 10M of tokens" to Hope, and the exact numbers sit in a truncated appendix.

The portion read contains no general limitations section and no compute cost for the sleep phase; the efficiency appendix was truncated. No code release is mentioned research.

Facts vs. opinions. The blog's phrase "retain long-context knowledge up to 10M tokens" is, in the paper's own text, a result for the Hope architecture on BABILong, compared against GPT-4, Titans and others. It is not an isolated effect of sleep. The blog names no underlying model. It also links "continual translation" to arXiv 2512.24695, the Hope paper, which makes it harder to see which result belongs to which contribution. The maths gains over GRPO are 0.9 to 2.8 points on single benchmarks with average@16. The large effect is on SQuAD knowledge incorporation, and the ablation shows dreaming is doing most of that work (48.9 with it, 35.7 without). The neuroscience vocabulary (NREM, REM, synaptic pruning) is metaphor, and the blog itself treats the terms as borrowed research.

Other perspectives. Today's brain story (below) offers an instructive contrast. A new modeling study suggests dendrite-specific inhibition lets overlapping neuronal assemblies form across brain areas without erasing existing memories. Google's approach is architecturally similar in spirit: it adds new low-rank experts rather than overwriting old ones. Both are parameter-isolation strategies. Neither shows how to make shared weights learn without interference, and Google's version grows the model with every consolidation, which the read portion does not cost out. No independent replication or critique was found research +1.

Why it matters. For agent builders the practical question is whether "consolidate session memory into weights overnight" beats retrieval plus a growing context, the approach LangChain still calls memory's least-solved piece (Quick hits). This paper does not answer that. Its baselines are in-context learning, Cartridges, SFT and SEAL, not a strong RAG-plus-summary agent memory, and it has no compute or latency budget in what was read. It is a credible research direction from the Titans and Hope line, presented this week as news although it was first posted in June research +1.

Molt, NVIDIA's lean PyTorch-native agentic RL framework (Hugging Face Papers, 2026-07-28)

3 sources, 2 min read

What it said. Molt pitched itself as an RL framework small enough for a researcher, or a coding assistant, to read in full. Agents are ordinary Python programs, and a single asynchronous loop trains multimodal and MoE policies, "never training on a token it did not generate." It is built on Ray, vLLM and NeMo AutoModel (FSDP2) without forking any of them (Hugging Face Papers) huggingface.

Paper facts. arXiv 2607.21653, July 2026, technical report. All authors are at NVIDIA; the visible names include Huiying Li, Hao Zhang, Binfeng Xu, Pavlo Molchanov, Jan Kautz and Yi Dong, and the author list appears truncated. Code size for the RL path, in the authors' count: Molt about 8.6K lines, against OpenRLHF about 7.2K, slime about 25K and verl about 62K. It ran end to end on a 700B MoE at expert parallelism 256. In a head-to-head with slime on Qwen3-30B-A3B over 2×8 H100 and 3 runs each, Molt's step time was 119.4 ± 2.3 s against slime's 109.5 ± 10.3 s, and throughput was 461 against 502 tokens per GPU per second. The authors treat the gap as run-to-run noise and claim no superiority. They also disclose that an upstream MoE log-prob mismatch of about 1 nat caused their [0.99, 1.01] gate to reject batches, so the comparison "measure[s] throughput only, not training quality." Speculative decoding (MTP) cut generation per step from 329 s to 64 s in their Qwen3.6-35B-A3B recipe. No throughput comparison with verl is given. Code: Apache-2.0 huggingface.

How it aged. As of October 29, the GitHub repository has about 1.2k stars, 121 forks and 327 commits on main, with 9 open issues and 15 open pull requests. How brains might avoid catastrophic forgetting: dendrite-specific inhibition

2 sources, 2 min read

The Transmitter's Natalia Mesa reports on a modelling study in Neuron (August 2026) by groups including Julijana Gjorgjieva (Technical University of Munich), Christoph Miehl (Tim Vogels's lab, IST Austria) and Sebastian Onasch. In the model, each neuron has a soma and several dendritic compartments that can fire NMDA-driven spikes and strengthen their own synapses independently. Inhibitory cells silence individual compartments, which blocks plasticity there. Context signals switch off subsets of inhibitory neurons, disinhibiting particular compartments so learning lands only there (The Transmitter) thetransmitter.

Paper facts. Status: peer-reviewed in Neuron, August 2026 (DOI 10.1016/j.neuron.2026.07.028, per The Transmitter). The paper itself was not read; everything here comes from The Transmitter. Authors: the three named above, with no full list given. Method: a computational model of recurrent excitatory and inhibitory networks with dendritic compartments, with brain areas linked by feedforward connections. Results (no numbers are reported in the article): a neuron can join multiple assemblies without erasing earlier ones, the same input in a different context forms a separate assembly, partial cues reactivate the whole assembly only in the learned context, and assemblies can be copied and merged across areas. In a simulated task, look-alike characters such as "0" and "O" overlapped in the visual area but separated in a downstream "concept" area given the right context. Limitations as reported: the model is computational only, it assumes inhibition targets one dendrite per neuron per context, the origin of "context" is the biggest open question, and the task was simple. Code: not stated. Independent comment: Christos Papadimitriou called it "a major advance," and Attila Losonczy said it "provides a really strong testable hypothesis." Losonczy's testable predictions are that subgroups of one inhibitory class respond to different contexts, preferentially target specific branches, and that dendritic activity shifts systematically with context thetransmitter.

For the reader, this sits next to Google's sleep paper. Both avoid forgetting by isolating where new learning can go: the brain model gates per-branch plasticity with context, and Google adds fresh low-rank experts. The neural version has a feature the LLM version lacks: learning is routed by context at write time, at no parameter cost. For whole-brain emulation it is a reminder that dendritic compartmentalisation and inhibitory cell-class targeting may be functionally necessary detail, not something a point-neuron emulation can drop thetransmitter +1.
