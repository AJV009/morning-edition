Mistral releases the weights of Mistral Large 4, a 1T-parameter MoE

2 sources, 5 min read

What happened. Mistral has published the weights of Mistral Large 4, a model it puts at one trillion parameters. It is pitched at self-hosted deployment and cybersecurity work, and Mistral claims it leads open-weight models on agentic coding. The newsletter summary that brought it in adds that Mistral claims cybersecurity capability comparable to GLM-5.3 mistral. Mistral's own announcement (Mistral: Mistral Large 4 open weights) calls it an "Open-weight hybrid instruct-and-reasoning MoE with multimodal input", trained from scratch on European infrastructure, covering 160+ languages, with three reasoning modes (Low, Medium and a default High). It says the model is the "best open-weight model for coding" and that "a full technical report is coming soon". There is no technical report yet, so the architecture, training data and context length cannot be checked from a primary source as of 31 October mistral.

Details. The announcement does not give the active parameter count. The Hugging Face repository name, Mistral-Large-4-1T-A52B, implies about 52B active, and Hacker News commenters work from the same 1T total and 52B active (Hacker News: weights thread). The announcement does not state the context length or hardware requirements either. It lists vLLM, SGLang, an NVIDIA NIM container and TensorRT LLM as serving paths. API pricing is $0.70 per million input tokens, $0.07 per million cached input tokens and $2.10 per million output tokens. The licence is called the Mistral Ecosystem Open-Weight license, a modified MIT licence under which the model is "free to use, modify, fine-tune, and commercialize, with a single condition". That condition is that companies offering it as a Model-as-a-Service need a separate commercial licence from Mistral before commercial use (Mistral) mistral.

The benchmarks are all published by Mistral. Some come from third parties, but Mistral chose which ones to show. The chart values below were extracted from the page's chart text in order of appearance, so individual pairings could be wrong. On Cognition's private FrontierCode 2.0, which scores end-to-end code quality and whether a maintainer would merge the change, Large 4 scores 19.7% against 19.3% for DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, 17.8% for GLM-5.3 and 17.3% for Kimi K3. That is a lead of 0.4 points on a private benchmark. On the Artificial Analysis Coding Agent Index, Mistral itself describes 48% as effectively tied with GLM-5.3, while using 47% fewer tokens per task on average. On Terminal-Bench 4.0 it reaches 41%, up 13 points from the preview's self-reported 28%, against 40% for GLM-5.3, 31% for DeepSeek V4.1 Flash and 21% for Kimi K3. Mistral credits three extra weeks of reinforcement learning after the preview for the coding gains and writes that "Gains continue to accelerate, with no signs of saturation." On Surge's blind pairwise human-preference test for code, Large 4 wins 64% against GLM-5.3 and against Kimi K3, 56% against DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, and 22% against Claude Opus 5.5 (Mistral) mistral.

The non-coding numbers are mixed. On FinWorkBench (Finch) it scores 73.3% against 77.3% for Kimi K3. On BrowseComp it scores 84.5 against 91.2 for Kimi K3 and 82.5 for GLM-5.3. On the Artificial Analysis Cyber Index the page shows Large 4 at 51%, below DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash 1017 at 57% and MiMo-V2.6-Pro at 56%, and above GLM-5.3 Flash at 50% and GLM-5.3 at 36%. Mistral also reports an average refusal rate of 96.6% on the harmful-cyber prompts in JailbreakBench, StrongREJECT and AgentHarm, and 97.1% attack resistance on the B3 Agent Security Benchmark, up from 93.3% for the preview. It claims that "anyone hosting the model can run a safe agent without additional tuning", and also says "we see a potential for uplift to conduct real attacks" (Mistral) mistral.

Facts vs. opinions. The weights, the price list, the licence condition and the existence of the benchmark charts are facts. "Best open-weight model for coding" is a vendor claim resting on a 0.4-point lead on a private benchmark, a one-point lead on Terminal-Bench and a tie on the Artificial Analysis coding index. The newsletter line about cyber capability "comparable to GLM-5.3" does not match Mistral's own chart as extracted. That chart puts Large 4 at 51% and GLM-5.3 at 36%, though GLM-5.3 Flash is at 50%. Either the summary means the Flash variant or the extraction is wrong, and the primary page does not settle which. The refusal figure is incomplete as a safety claim: the page gives no false-refusal rate on benign security work, although it asserts the model handles "legitimate defensive security tasks without unnecessary refusals" mistral.

Other perspectives. The Hacker News thread on the weights release had 1,670 points and 433 comments when read. Its longest argument is about the licence. Critics, including the user maxloh ("Calling this "open weight" is misleading."), compare the MaaS carve-out to the Redis, Elasticsearch and Terraform relicensings. Defenders point out that Mistral never said "open source". On hardware, one commenter notes that "The lowest quant will still require 256GB VRAM to run inferences", even with day-zero Unsloth quantisations and an NVFP4 release. The suggested alternatives were a roughly $30k Apple machine with 512GB or rented cloud capacity. Another commenter flagged the missing benign-refusal statistic ("Where is the statistic about not refusing the non malicious requests?"). Others said Opus 5.5 remains well ahead, or that cheaper cached input from DeepSeek matters more than the headline price. EU data sovereignty came up repeatedly as Mistral's real selling point (Hacker News). These are forum opinions, not measurements. The thread also shows that Mistral now hosts GLM-5.3, so the company it benchmarks against is also a model it sells mistral.

Why it matters. For self-hosting, the operating question is whether the 1T weights change anything at your scale. At 256GB for the smallest quant, this is a datacentre model whose "open" status matters to sovereign and regulated buyers, not to someone with a workstation GPU. The cyber framing also cuts both ways. In the same week, Epoch showed that GLM 5.3 can turn shipped-but-unreleased fixes into exploits (next story), and Mistral's own chart places Large 4 above GLM-5.3 on a cyber index. Nothing here shows that index scores transfer to Epoch-style tasks. Still, a vendor saying it sees "potential for uplift to conduct real attacks" while releasing weights with refusal tuning as the main safeguard is the trade-off the patch-gap story is about. The Vulnerator AI pentest harness in today's quick hits already uses Large 4 as its default driver github. Wait for the technical report, independent Terminal-Bench reruns, and the false-refusal numbers before treating any of the coding lead as settled mistral.

DeepMind: "competence reshapes the economy gradually, whereas coordination abruptly transforms it"

5 sources, 5 min read

What happened. The Google DeepMind Institute published an essay titled "The Economics of Intelligence", dated 29 October 2026, by Alex Imas (Director of AGI Economics), Andrew Koh (Research Scientist) and Shane Legg (Co-founder and Chief AGI Scientist). It summarises a longer paper, Organizing Intelligence, that contains the formal model (Google DeepMind Institute: The Economics of Intelligence). The newsletter summary puts the thesis this way: models are improving fast in individual competence and lagging in coordination, they already match or beat experienced specialists on self-contained deliverables, and wages and job roles will be the first signals of change deepmind.

Details. The core move is to say that "the individual AI agent is the wrong economic object". Benchmarks test lone agents, but economic output comes from organisations competing in markets. Human teams are mosaics: people are strong in different places, so a team of complementary specialists can beat an AI that outscores every individual member. In the authors' words, "a model can exceed the performance of every person one-on-one and still lose to a well-structured team". The essay says AI is jagged in similar ways across models (citing arXiv 2512.15567), so adding another copy of a model adds capacity, not breadth. AI differs from human labour in two ways: it can be duplicated at the cost of compute, so "whenever a model can match human capability, human wages become tied to the cost of compute" (citing Pascual Restrepo, NBER w34423); and in principle it could escape human coordination limits such as span of control, lossy handoffs and misaligned incentives (DeepMind Institute) deepmind.

From there the authors build "organizational scaling laws" with three phases. In solo work, working alone is best because coordination is too weak. In bounded scale, which they say is today's world, productivity gains level off and then fall as firms grow. In unbounded scale, past a "supercooperative" threshold, each added member raises productivity, which "breaks the Coasean ceiling" so that market size is the only limit on firm size. Their summary line is that "competence sets the potential productivity level; coordination determines how big an organization can get". They then describe four regimes. Under minimal AGI, AI matches average human ability, industry structure mostly holds, wages for matched skills fall toward compute cost, and specialists and coordinators earn more. Under supercompetent AI, AI beats every expert except at coordination, and the result is lean, highly productive firms and "abundance without concentration". Under supercooperative AI, average competence combines with coordination past the threshold, producing an abrupt switch from concave to convex returns to scale, consolidation of deep markets into a few AI-only firms, and a sharp drop in wages. Under ASI, both thresholds are crossed and spending may shift toward a relational sector such as healthcare and education (DeepMind Institute) deepmind.

The essay's diagnosis of the present is that competence is surging and coordination is lagging. Its recommendations are benchmarks for "organizations of agents", tracking compute spending alongside payroll, and watching wages and job roles as early signals, "because wage effects arrive before organizational restructuring". Its evidence that agents coordinate poorly today is CooperBench plus a quote from OpenAI's Noam Brown on the Dwarkesh podcast: "it's actually very difficult to get these agents to coordinate in a productive way". Brown also said he "wouldn't even attribute 10% of the credit to multi-agent" capabilities in running thousands of agents on math problems (DeepMind Institute) deepmind.

Facts vs. opinions. There is less empirical content than the confident tone suggests. The essay's own data is a quiz given to 100 people across six domains. Performance in one domain predicted little about another, and complementary pairs beat random pairs by 32%. That is a small lab exercise, not a labour-market study. Several load-bearing claims are made without citation, according to the page as read: that frontier models solve "Millennium Prize problems in math" and write production software, and that headcounts are similar to a few years ago while productivity has "barely budged". The present-day labour-market description (leaner startups, flatter org charts, weaker entry-level hiring in roles closest to benchmark tasks) is also uncited. The four regimes and the threshold are model outputs. They are predictions conditional on the model's assumptions, not observations (DeepMind Institute) deepmind.

Other perspectives. A search on X for reactions to Organizing Intelligence returned nothing as of 31 October, so the independent reception is not established. Some of today's other items bear on it directly. Sam Altman argues for "a trillion AIs of slightly superhuman intelligence" over one superintelligence, on the grounds that swarms of agents keep each other in check, much like a resilient society substack. That is the coordination question with the opposite valence: DeepMind treats crossing the coordination threshold as the moment wages collapse, while Altman treats many coordinating agents as the safety architecture. A swarm experiment by researchers, with eight agents from four models resolving simulated outages, found that leadership emerged from the underlying model and from the order names appeared in the prompt raindrop. That suggests leaderless coordination today depends on arbitrary configuration details, which is consistent with DeepMind's "coordination lags" diagnosis. Spawn's claim of 77,000 substantial changes in 30 days with ten people and $3.1 million in tokens is an unverified self-report, but it shows the "track compute alongside payroll" ratio in a founder's own accounting x. On the same point, the Pragmatic Engineer reports that incident.io cut AI spend from $4,500 per developer per month in August to $1,600 now source.

Why it matters. The useful part for an agent engineer is the framing, not the forecasts. If the binding constraint is coordination (handoffs, shared state, credit assignment across agents), then harness work on subagent orchestration, memory curation and shared permissioned state is the work that would move an organisation from the bounded regime toward the unbounded one. The essay explicitly asks for benchmarks of "organizations of agents", which barely exist. The economic claim worth carrying is the ordering: wage effects before restructuring, so employment statistics alone are weak evidence that AI is doing nothing. That claim is testable, and it is the one to check against real wage data over the next year, not against the essay's own toy quiz deepmind.

Epoch: an open-weight model turned unshipped Firefox fixes into exploits for about $1,000 per version

2 sources, 4 min read

What happened. Epoch AI published research on 29 October showing that GLM 5.3, Z.ai's open-weight model released in August 2026, compromised six of six Firefox releases between 156.0 and 158.0.1 (released 17 September to 26 October). It did this by using security fixes that were published in Firefox's code but not yet shipped to users (Epoch AI: open-weight models and the patch gap). The authors are Aiden Ament, Yafah Edelman, Alexander Barry, Timothée Chauvin, Lee Bousfield and Jaeho Lee. The newsletter summary frames the conclusion as open-source projects being especially exposed because their fixes are public, with the threat likely to worsen as open-weight cyber capability improves epoch.

Details. The goal was a webpage that, when a Firefox user on Ubuntu 26.04 with default security settings and the official build visits it, reads a local file and sends it to the attacker with no further interaction. GLM 5.3 was accessed through Z.ai's first-party API and through third-party APIs and was told the task was a security exercise. It had basic tools and no internet access. The researchers supplied the public information it would need, limited to what existed at each version's release. Beyond basic instructions to inspect fixes not yet in the stable release, the model picked the security fixes, chose the vulnerabilities and built the chain itself. Each successful chain combined two vulnerabilities. A patch-gap memory-safety flaw gave code execution inside the content process, and a separate, unpublished sandbox-bypass bug, which the model rediscovered on every attempt, broke out of the sandbox. Disguising fixes as ordinary improvements did not help, because the model identified which changes were security-related (Epoch AI) epoch.

On cost and time, Epoch reports: "Our marginal cost of compromising a new version of Firefox was about $1,000 at API pricing", once the harness existed. Total Firefox research spend, including environment-building, was over $100,000. Each attempt was allowed up to 48 hours: "We gave GLM 5.3 up to 48 hours to find an exploit, but its fastest attempt needed only 12 hours." Another roughly $100,000 went on Chrome attempts, with no success. And: "We did not trigger any safeguards or refusals from any provider, including Z.ai, when using GLM 5.3 in this way." (Epoch AI) epoch.

Facts vs. opinions. The authors state their limits clearly. Every success depended on the one unpublished sandbox escape, which is now fixed in Firefox 159.0 (27 October, advisory mfsa2026-118). They have not tested 159.0 but expect it to be "at least substantially more difficult". Only file reads worked: "We do not have any code execution successes on recent Firefox releases as of writing." Releases before 17 September were not systematically tested, nor was closed-source software or Kimi K3.1 Pro. They note that costs might be lower on subscription accounts, and that simply giving the model internet access would make the attack cheaper. They also explicitly decline to make claims about the offence-defence balance. The report names no specific bugs or CVEs and gives no per-attempt success rate, so "cheap and reliable" cannot be checked from the page. One operational note: in one incident, broken environments let agents act outside the sandbox, with no network access and no malicious activity, and all runs were paused (Epoch AI) epoch.

Other perspectives. Mozilla published "defending the patch gap" the same day, under Bobby Holley's name (Mozilla: defending the patch gap). It does not mention Epoch. It says Firefox halved its release cycle earlier this year to weekly builds, is again keeping regression tests confidential until a fix ships because GLM 5.3 struggles to exploit patches that come without a test, scans all new commits, and will roll out "staggered patch gaps": grouping fix classes such as renderer bugs and sandbox escapes so their exposure windows never overlap and only one link of a chain is exposed at a time. Mozilla says it has made more than 7x the security fixes since February compared with the same period in 2025, using early access to frontier models from Anthropic and OpenAI. It also says the models available to attackers are "several generations behind". The tension with Epoch is real, since Epoch's attacker model is exactly such a widely available model and it still worked. Mozilla's line is: "The patch gap threat has increased, but we have the tools to structurally address it." epoch

On Reddit's r/firefox (533 points when read), the top comment predicted "almost-daily patch releases for majorly important software". Another commenter said Microsoft patched over 1,400 vulnerabilities in October alone, against about 1,100 in all of 2025. That is unverified forum data. A pointed reply read the piece as "open models 'bad', closed models with safeguards 'good'", and someone answered "the 'Trust us' approach". Epoch itself avoids the offence-defence claim, but its recommendation, "Defenders should avoid patching multiple vulnerabilities simultaneously if they can be chained together", amounts to the same staggering strategy Mozilla announced epoch.

Why it matters. This is the quantitative version of what the Pragmatic Engineer describes anecdotally (next story). The window between a fix landing in a public repo and the update reaching users is now exploitable by a widely available model for about $1,000 and 12 to 48 hours. Epoch's PHP data point fits: a vendor published patched PHP builds more than six hours before coordinated release, and exploit attempts went from thousands before public release to millions immediately after (Epoch AI) epoch +1. For anyone running infrastructure, this changes the cost of every day an update is deferred. For open-source maintainers, it means the commit itself is the disclosure. The countermeasures now in use are withholding tests, staggering fix classes and shortening release cadence, and none of them depends on the model provider refusing anything epoch.

The Pulse: a month's worth of breaches in a day

3 sources, 4 min read

What happened. Gergely Orosz's Pragmatic Engineer released a free section of The Pulse (sent to paying subscribers on 22 October, republished on 30 October) on a cluster of security incidents mostly over one Tuesday and Wednesday. The list: eight eMudhra intermediate CA private keys leaked; two critical PHP patches for remote code execution; Railway customer secrets exposed and some decrypted; a critical GitLab RCE patch; a Node.js release fixing 10 critical CVEs, including one RCE, and revoking the eMudhra intermediate; a Go release adding an eMudhra mitigation; and PingCAP's managed TiDB, where attackers had near-total control-plane access from 9 September to 7 October and released 10TB of customer-sensitive data. PingCAP claims no customer data stored inside TiDB was accessed source.

Details. On eMudhra, Orosz reports that one 384-bit private key "turned out to be the number '4'", with others at 56, 66, 115, 156, 230 and 237. That is seven values listed for eight keys, and the piece does not say whether one repeats. He quotes eMudhra's preliminary explanation: an approved deviation from its usual Key Generation Ceremony to test wrap/unwrap for export introduced a code flaw that created a second, weaker key pair, and those keys "were used by mistake to create the CSR (because the key labels were similar)", which was then signed by eMudhra's root and sent to Sectigo for cross-signing. The certificates were generated on 30 April 2025. Orosz writes that eMudhra, listed in India at close to $500M market cap two days before disclosure, lost 40% of its value in two days source. Google's own post (Google: protecting users from compromised eMudhra intermediates) is more restrained. It says only that the keys were "generated with insufficient randomness (i.e., weak cryptographic entropy)". It dates the report to Saturday 17 October at 18:05 UTC, followed by a CRLSet block at 19:45 UTC, and says no certificates chaining to the affected CAs had been logged to Certificate Transparency before the block, so CT-enforcing clients such as Chrome could not have been targeted. It removed "emSign ECC Root CA - G3" from the Chrome Root Store, and "the Chrome Root Program is closely investigating the ongoing inclusion of eMudhra's remaining CA certificates". Google's post is internally inconsistent: it refers to "eight" subordinate CA certificates and later to "these six intermediate CAs". The likely explanation is a split between TLS and code-signing intermediates, but that was seen only in search summaries and is not checked here source.

On PHP, Orosz quotes OpenAI: Calif found a critical flaw using GPT-5.6 Sol under OpenAI's "Patch the Planet" initiative, and Wiz independently found both vulnerabilities. Amazon Threat Intelligence says it saw a PHP2Shell attempt in MadPot honeypot telemetry on 17 October, four days before public disclosure of CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619, from an anonymisation network, and that "it remains unclear how the actor acquired exploit information prior to disclosure". Orosz adds that the NSA urged patching on release or taking applications offline, and that Cloudflare saw millions of exploit attempts within hours of the patch. Railway said an unauthorised party "may have accessed encrypted environment variables and, for some of them, obtained the decrypted contents", told customers to rotate every variable, and paused all deployments for close to 20 hours. Since rotating a secret requires a redeploy, customers were left choosing between taking services down and leaving possibly leaked keys live source.

Facts vs. opinions. The incident list, vendor quotes and dates come from the companies involved as relayed by Orosz. "Around 70% of all websites run on PHP" is his figure and is not sourced in the piece. His diagnosis is opinion, flagged as such ("I sense the balance of power... is shifting"). He also cites a CNBC report from May in which Dario Amodei warned of a "narrow window" because Chinese models are "maybe six to 12 months" behind Mythos, and says that prediction "has come to fruition". Amodei's "six to 12 months" was an estimate, not a measurement. The breaches Orosz lists were not shown to be AI-assisted, with the exception of the PHP discovery side. The claim that attackers can now "spin up agents" to exploit unpatched servers is a plausible explanation of Cloudflare's numbers, not a demonstrated one source.

Other perspectives. Epoch's report is the closest thing to an independent measurement of Orosz's thesis. It shows a widely available model doing the patch-gap work for about $1,000, and it independently records the PHP early-build leak (Epoch AI) epoch. Mozilla's argument that the patch gap can be "structurally" addressed is the counterweight to Orosz's "attackers gain advantage" framing (Mozilla). On the CA side, Google's account takes some of the drama out of the MITM risk for browsers: no CT-logged certificates, a block within two hours of the report, and root removal reaching all Chrome Root Store devices on 27 October. Orosz's warning holds for non-browser, server-to-server clients that do not use CRLSets (Google) source. The Dark Reading item in quick hits, a scanner impersonating 32 AI crawlers to harvest credentials, is another instance of the commodity attack automation Orosz describes darkreading.

Why it matters. Orosz's recommendations are automated patching, assuming attackers hold zero-days, mandatory automated monitoring (the TiDB month-long dwell is his example), defenders adopting AI, and accepting that security will cost more. All are plausible, and none is new. What is new is the timeline compression, which Epoch and Mozilla now quantify. The Railway episode is the design lesson for platform builders: a breach response that pauses deploys while asking customers to rotate secrets turns a confidentiality incident into an availability incident. Secret rotation that does not require a redeploy, through runtime secret fetch or short-lived credentials, now counts as incident-response infrastructure. Orosz's list of follow-on CVEs after 22 October (a Brotli RCE, a second PHP RCE, Linux kernel bugs accidentally posted to a public mailing list, and a Pillow OS-command flaw) suggests the volume has not dropped source.

Anthropic-found Linux TCP use-after-free: 9.8 or 5.9, depending on who is counting

3 sources, 4 min read

What happened. The Hacker News (the security site) reports CVE-2026-98432, a use-after-free in Linux IPv4 TCP connection setup, reported by Anthropic researcher Xinyang Ge (The Hacker News: Linux kernel TCP flaw). The newsletter summary says it could lead to memory corruption, kernel memory reads or crashes, and that Red Hat and others rate it lower because the race is complex to exploit thehackernews.

Details. According to the report, when the TCP handshake completes, the IP options stored with the pending request are handed to the new socket without being duplicated. If that socket is torn down while another CPU is still using the options to answer a retransmitted connection request, freed memory is read. IPv6 is not affected because it copies the options. The fix, commit e3f33b0a1d89d738e2b913dab519226eaf76e15b applied on 5 October, delays freeing the options until other CPUs have finished reading them. The CVE was published on 15 October. The bug was introduced in Linux 4.4 and is fixed upstream in 5.10.272, 5.15.223, 6.1.190, 6.6.159, 6.12.113, 6.18.56, 7.2.10 and 7.3. RHEL 7 is not affected. Debian, Oracle UEK, some SUSE releases and some Amazon Linux 2023 kernels had shipped fixes. As of 29 October, Ubuntu and RHEL 8, 9 and 10 and OpenShift 4 had not (The Hacker News) thehackernews.

Anthropic's 27 October post, as relayed by The Hacker News, says Claude wrote an exploit after "initial prompting" that gives an unauthenticated remote attacker root command execution on servers with a multipath TCP (MPTCP) listener. It worked in two stages: a memory leak to defeat KASLR (48 minutes in testing), then a buffer overflow overwriting a callback pointer (5 more minutes). A lost race only kills that one connection, so the attacker can keep retrying. Anthropic did not publish the exploit, the kernel version or the configuration it tested. The article does not name a model version, though a linked headline calls it "unreleased". Go 1.24+ requests MPTCP by default on listening sockets. Setting GODEBUG=multipathtcp=0 disables that, but the Go team calls it a fragile mitigation and says the bug is not specific to MPTCP. No in-the-wild exploitation was reported, and the flaw is not in CISA's KEV catalogue (The Hacker News) thehackernews. Anthropic's original post was not read for this issue, so its claims appear here only as The Hacker News reports them.

Facts vs. opinions. The scoring spread is the story. The kernel CVE team gives 9.8 with low attack complexity, and NVD (as of 28 October) displays that 9.8 rather than its own score. Ubuntu lists it as Priority High and shows 9.8. Red Hat's CVE page says Moderate, 5.9. Red Hat's VEX file (29 October) says Important, 8.1. Oracle and SUSE say Important, 8.1. Amazon says Important, 7.0. Debian gives no score. The 8.1 scores match the kernel team on every metric except attack complexity, which the vendors rate high; the kernel team calls both sides of the race "attacker-induced". Red Hat says "no confidentiality or integrity impact has been demonstrated", while Anthropic says it demonstrated root. Both statements can be true from where each party stands, because Anthropic did not publish the exploit or its configuration, so Red Hat has nothing to reproduce. Red Hat first said fixes were "currently deferred to a future update", then changed the status to Affected (The Hacker News) thehackernews.

Other perspectives. This sits uneasily next to the Pragmatic Engineer's point that publishing a fix is itself disclosure, and next to Epoch's finding that an open-weight model can exploit published fixes source +1. The fix landed upstream on 5 October and the CVE was published on 15 October, yet two of the largest enterprise distributions had no fixed kernel by 29 October. That is two weeks of public patch gap for a bug a frontier lab says is remotely exploitable to root. Withholding the exploit is responsible practice, but it also lets distributions treat the difficulty as theoretical. Orosz separately notes "several unpatched Linux kernel vulnerabilities were accidentally posted on the public kernel mailing list" in the same period, which is a different incident source.

Why it matters. Operationally: if you run Go services on Ubuntu or RHEL 8 to 10, you are potentially exposed through MPTCP-by-default listeners until fixed kernels ship. The GODEBUG switch is a stopgap the Go team itself calls fragile. More broadly, this is now a recurring pattern. A frontier lab's model finds a long-lived memory-safety bug in critical infrastructure (here, present since 4.4), the lab claims a working exploit, the downstream severity process cannot verify it, and scores diverge by almost four CVSS points. Severity scoring was built for human researchers who publish proofs of concept. If AI-found bugs keep arriving with private exploits, vendors will need either an escrow mechanism for verification or a default of trusting the finder's complexity assessment thehackernews.

Tokenomics: the 62.5-minute rule for Claude's cache (originally 18 May 2026)

1 source, 3 min read

What it said. Ryan Skidmore's post, as summarised in May, argued that when a 5-minute prompt cache is about to expire and you have no real request to send, you should refresh it if you expect to need it again within 62.5 minutes and otherwise let it expire. The number was said to be the same across models and cache sizes, with only the dollar amounts changing skids. This is an old item revisited, not news.

How it aged. The derivation is simple and still correct for the prices it assumed. The post (skids.dev: the 62.5-minute rule) uses the 5-minute cache write, W, at 1.25x base input and the cache read, R, at 0.10x, noting that a read also renews the cache's lifetime ("cache hit = cache refresh"). OmniRetrieval (arXiv 2605.29250, v1 on 28 May 2026, no venue listed) argues that retrievers "operate over one source at a time under a fixed query language", and that flattening everything into one embedding space "erases the structural affordances (such as schemas, ontologies, compositional operators)" (arXiv: OmniRetrieval) huggingface. Its alternative is an "overarching layer" that "takes any natural-language query, identifies appropriate knowledge sources, and dispatches source-native queries": SQL to databases, SPARQL to Wikidata, Cypher to Neo4j graphs, and document search to text.

Paper facts. Authors are Jinheon Baek, Soyeong Jeong, Sangwoo Park, Woongyeong Yeo and Minki Kang (KAIST), Patara Trirat and Heejun Lee (DeepAuto.ai), and corresponding author Sung Ju Hwang. Status: arXiv preprint (arXiv HTML: OmniRetrieval). The benchmark spans 13 datasets and 309 knowledge bases: 7 BEIR sets, Spider and BIRD for SQL, SimpleQuestions, QALD-10 and LC-QuAD 2.0 for SPARQL, and Text2Cypher over 15 Neo4j graphs, with 300 sampled questions per dataset. Scores are macro-averaged over the four paradigms and five backbones (GPT-5.4, Gemini-3.1 Pro, Sonnet-4.6, and locally on one H200 via vLLM, Qwen-3.5 27B and Gemma-4 31B). OmniRetrieval reports source-selection accuracy of 65.71, retrieval accuracy of 44.34 and LLM-as-judge 65.88, against 61.65, 39.98 and 57.99 for a KB-routing baseline, and against an oracle upper bound of 100.00, 61.85 and 74.55. Single-paradigm baselines score far lower (document search, for example, 21.78, 13.69 and 39.49), which is partly by construction since each covers only a quarter of the queries. By backbone, retrieval accuracy runs from 38.34 (Qwen-3.5 27B) to 52.69 (Gemini-3.1 Pro). The stated limitations are that evidence selection could be strengthened "through supervised fine-tuning on labeled cross-source selections or reinforcement learning", and that the setup "uses a single shared LLM across source selection, native query formulation, and evidence selection". Code: "Our code is available at https://github.com/JinheonBaek/OmniRetrieval". The judge is GPT-5.4-mini, so the LLM-as-judge column is an LLM grading LLMs huggingface.

Why care: agent memory and tool use increasingly mean querying structured stores, and the JitMem and JAM line on read-time curation is the same move applied to trajectories huggingface. The fair comparison is against KB Routing, not the single-source strawmen, and there the gain is about 4 points on source selection and retrieval. The paper is real and useful, but incremental over a sensible router, and the gap to the oracle (44.34 against 61.85 on retrieval) says native query formulation is still the weak link huggingface.
