Mistral's Timestral makes the case for small specialist models; also: Claude Mythos seven months on, Anthropic's Claude Code postmortem, Databricks' "80%" claim and GitLab's agentic restructuring

Today: The lead is Mistral's Timestral. It is a family of time-series forecasting models running from 5 million to 209 million parameters. An AlphaSignal essay uses it to argue that small specialised foundation models should take over parts of the AI stack from LLM pipelines. We check Mistral's benchmark tables, the leakage problem in time-series benchmarks, NVIDIA's Kumo Relational claims and a new preprint on Jev-style decision models. Six archive items follow, each checked for how it aged. Claude Mythos, gated in April for its cyber capabilities, has since gone through a Commerce Department suspension and a staged release. The reported 67% drop in Claude Code thinking depth ended in an Anthropic postmortem that named three product-layer causes. Claude Code Routines has been renamed "scheduled tasks" and is still a research preview. Managed Agents Memory is still in beta and has a new header. Cameron Wolfe's nanoMoE tutorial now reads as a primer for the MoE models that dominate local inference. Databricks' 80% GPU saving turns out to be measured against provisioning for peak load. Ways of thinking takes Shane Parrish's line that action produces the information inaction waits for. The wildcards are GitLab's "agentic era" restructuring and what its numbers did afterwards, and TurboQuant KV-cache compression for self-hosted Gemma 4. Quick hits cover Xiaomi's MiMo RL report, coding-agent harnesses, 4 GB local agents, dynamic workflows and the hidden cost of open weights.

Lead stories

Mistral's Timestral and the argument for a multi-model stack

1 source, 7 min read

What happened. On Sunday AlphaSignal published a deep dive by Ben Dickson. It uses Mistral's Timestral to argue that the default habit of reaching for a more powerful LLM is wrong for tasks that do not need language generation. Its central claim is that small, task-specific foundation models (forecasting, tabular, relational and decision models) can replace expensive LLM pipelines for individual steps, with a reasoning model orchestrating them source. The model underneath the essay is real and recent. Mistral announced Timestral on 15 October 2026 as "our first family" of time-series foundation models, in four sizes of 5M, 28M, 123M and 209M parameters, under a Modified MIT licence, with weights on Hugging Face and a GitHub repository for running them on your own data (Mistral).

Details. Mistral's headline sentence is that "Our largest model, at just 209M parameters, ranks first on all three of the field's main public benchmarks," those being GIFT-Eval, TIME and fev-bench. It also says the model beats the prior state of the art "with only two-thirds of the model parameters." The comparison covers pretrained, zero-shot models only, using a single checkpoint without ensembling (Mistral). The margins are thin. On GIFT-Eval's geometric-mean weighted quantile loss (lower is better), Timestral-209M scores 0.4460, against 0.4520 for a model listed as t0-gamma and 0.4557 for TimesFM-3. On GIFT-Eval geometric-mean MASE it scores 0.6575, against 0.6608 for t0-gamma and 0.6660 for Falcon-2.0. On TIME's geometric-mean CRPS it scores 0.5223, against 0.5340 for t0-gamma and 0.5363 for TimesFM-3. On fev-bench's scaled quantile loss skill score (higher is better) it reaches 50.39%, against 49.42% for t0-gamma, 48.99% for TimesFM-3 and 47.28% for Chronos-2. The smaller sizes matter most for cost. Mistral says "Our 28M model beats Chronos-2, about four times larger, on GIFT-Eval and TIME." The 28M model scores 0.5344 CRPS on TIME, level with t0-gamma's 0.5340, and Mistral claims "Every size sits on the Pareto front of accuracy versus model size" (Mistral). The interface works as the essay describes: numerical arrays in, numerical quantiles out. Mistral's page specifies a 21-quantile output, a context of 32,768 time steps, multivariate targets, and past-only and known-future covariates, including categorical ones (Mistral) source.

The architectural novelty is what Mistral calls Decoupled Horizon Forecasting. Mistral contrasts it with the contiguous patch masking used by Toto 2.0 and TimesFM-3, where forecast targets are masked as if missing and so lost as context. In DHF, in Mistral's words, "the full history stays in the sequence, and learned forecast tokens read the history before them." Covariate-aware attention gives each series a role. Known-future covariates are visible across the whole window, targets stay causal, and information flows only from covariates to targets, which Mistral says prevents leakage of a target's own future. Training ran in two stages: pretraining on purely synthetic data at context 8,192, then post-training on mixed real and synthetic data at 32,768, with "a pinball loss over 21 quantiles, a discrete approximation of CRPS." Mistral states that "The majority of the training data was synthetic" (Mistral). AlphaSignal says inputs are patches of 32 observations source. Mistral's announcement does not state a patch size, and the figure was not checked against the repository.

On latency the two sources describe the same test differently. AlphaSignal says the smallest model forecast 96 steps from 2,048 observations in 17 milliseconds on eight CPU threads source. Mistral describes it as forecasting one day ahead from three weeks of 15-minute history on a standard 8-thread server CPU: 17 ms for the 5M model and 113 ms for the 209M model. It adds that the 28M model is 3.6 times faster than the 209M, and that the 123M "comes within 0.2% of the 209M score at three quarters of its latency" (Mistral). One day at 15-minute resolution is 96 steps. Three weeks of 15-minute data is 3 × 7 × 96 = 2,016 observations, not 2,048, so AlphaSignal's figure appears to round to a power of two or reflect a different configuration. Neither source gives the CPU model.

Facts vs. opinions. The facts are these. Timestral exists in four sizes, the weights and code are public, and Mistral's own tables put it first on the three benchmarks among the zero-shot models it lists. Each of those statements carries a qualifier. The rankings are vendor-run. Mistral says results "have been submitted to the GIFT-Eval and TIME leaderboards," not that they have been accepted. Its charts use truncated axes, and "A technical report with the full results will follow," so no paper exists yet to check the method against (Mistral). The essay's larger claims are opinion and are mostly hedged as such. Specialised models "could" reshape the stack, and an application's value "will increasingly come from the systems built around" the language model source. To its credit, the essay also says these models still need clean data, application-specific evaluation and monitoring, and "are not always faster or cheaper than traditional models such as XGBoost or CatBoost," with their advantage "mostly in reducing development costs" source. That last concession matters more than the headline. The case is less that these models predict better and more that they let teams skip building a bespoke ML pipeline.

Other perspectives. First, the benchmarks themselves are contested. A preprint by Meyer, Kaltenpoth, Zalipski and Müller (arXiv 2510.13654, revised February 2026, no venue listed) argues that as pretraining corpora grow, "it becomes difficult to ensure the integrity of the test sets used for benchmarking." It names two leakage routes, "(1) train-test sample overlaps arising from the multi-purpose reuse of datasets" and "(2) temporal overlap of correlated train and test series." It warns these risk "overly optimistic performance estimates that fail to generalize to real-world settings" (arXiv). Mistral trained mostly on synthetic data, which plausibly lowers this risk. But its post-training mixed in real data whose sources are not stated, and a gap of 0.006 in weighted quantile loss is easily inside the range leakage could plausibly explain. That will stay unknowable until the technical report lists the data. Second, the essay's other examples hold up partly. NVIDIA's Kumo Relational post (21 October 2026) does claim that "Across 41 tasks on 15 databases, it beats supervised relational deep learning models," with "None of these datasets were seen in pre-training." It reports an 8% MRR lead over large AutoGluon ensembles on SAP SALT, rising to 16% after fine-tuning. NVIDIA itself concedes that "On tasks with very large training sets, task-specific supervised models may still edge out in-context predictions" and that "Predictive signals must be reachable within a limited number of hops" (Hugging Face / NVIDIA). A commenter from Leeroo claims their system beats it on the RelBench leaderboard, a claim unverified as of today (Hugging Face / NVIDIA). The essay's TabPFN description was not checked for this issue.

Third, the "decision model" category the essay names, with Jev as its example, now has a direct critique. A preprint by Yu Sun, Junhao Xu, Jiajia Shi and Zijin Yang (arXiv 2609.26758, v3 of 8 October 2026, no venue) tested Jev and two open-weight lookalikes on 1,200 decision tasks. It swapped only which definition was bound to which option name. Type-error rates stayed at 0% in every condition. But with yes/no option names, flip rates ran up to 70.4 percentage points higher than with a 0/1 control, and mean AUC fell from 93.8% to 23.2%, below chance, after reassignment. The authors conclude that "type-correct outputs can still fail to follow explicit option definitions" (arXiv). That undercuts a quiet assumption in the essay's inventory-agent example, where Jev "classifies findings into predefined categories" source. A typed output contract guarantees the shape of the answer. Per this preprint, it does not guarantee that the model followed the rubric.

Fourth, builders have started experimenting. Builders have exported the 5M and 28M models to ONNX for in-browser use, reporting sizes of 13 MB and 59 MB, according to a post on r/MistralAI. A Brazilian data scientist posted on r/datasciencebr a zero-shot day-ahead test on the national grid operator ONS's hourly load data over 28 days, saying it was prompted by Hacker News scepticism about whether such models work on real data. The results were not checked here.

Why it matters. For an agent builder the practical idea is sound and old: break the workflow into steps and give each step the cheapest component that can be measured. The essay's own three-way split is reasoning model, specialised predictor, deterministic code source. What is new is that the specialised predictor now often comes as a free, CPU-sized, zero-shot checkpoint. That changes the build-or-skip calculation for forecasting features that never justified a bespoke pipeline. What is not established is whether the benchmark margins carry over to your data, whether typed decision heads follow your rubric, and whether these models beat a tuned gradient-boosted baseline. The essay concedes they may not. The right test is cheap: run the 28M model against your current baseline on a held-out window of your own series before believing anyone's leaderboard.

Anthropic gates Claude Mythos over cyber capabilities (AgentBrief, 9 April 2026)

1 source, 3 min read

What it said. AgentBrief, aggregating X posts, reported that Anthropic had withheld its most capable model, Claude Mythos, after a system card described "unprecedented" hacking capability. The figures came via social accounts, not Anthropic: a "90x improvement" over Opus 4.6, 100% on Cybench, 181 working shell exploits, and autonomous discovery of a 27-year-old OpenBSD TCP SACK denial-of-service bug and a 17-year-old FreeBSD remote code execution bug at $50 to $2,000 of compute. It also claimed cybersecurity firms "reportedly lost billions in valuation," and described Project Glasswing as a $100M defensive coalition with Apple, Google and Microsoft x.

How it aged. First, a sourcing note. Anthropic's own technical write-up could not be read for this issue (the fetch did not complete), so the specific figures above, the 90x, the 100% Cybench, the 181 exploits and the dollar range, are not checked against the primary source here. They stay as social-media claims. The release history can be checked, mainly through the Wikipedia article on Claude Mythos (last edited 22 October 2026), which is itself secondary (Wikipedia). By its account, draft blog posts leaked on 26 March 2026, and Anthropic confirmed the model to Fortune. The public announcement and Glasswing came on 7 April, with more than forty firms getting access, including Microsoft, Apple, Google, AWS, the Linux Foundation, Cisco, Nvidia and Broadcom. So the coalition was wider than the three names AgentBrief led with. Wikipedia does not mention the $100M figure. On the same day, members of a private Discord group reportedly gained unauthorised access using data from the Mercor breach. For a model withheld on security grounds, that sits awkwardly (Wikipedia).

The rollout then went through stages. In May, Glasswing reportedly grew from 50 to 200 organisations, and Anthropic said the first 50 partners had found more than 10,000 serious vulnerabilities, a vendor figure. The same article also says access widened to 150 organisations in over 15 countries on 2 June, which does not sit cleanly with the 200 figure; the discrepancy is in the source. On 28 May the Opus 4.8 announcement promised "Mythos-class" models for all customers within weeks. On 9 June, Claude Mythos 5 launched as a Glasswing preview alongside Fable 5, a version with added safeguards. On 12 June the US Commerce Department sent a letter "prohibiting access to both Mythos 5 and Fable 5 to any non-U.S. national whatsoever, regardless of their location," and Anthropic cut off all customers. Restrictions were lifted on 30 June and access resumed on 1 July. Mythos 5.1 and Fable 5.1 shipped on 1 September. Users approved under an updated Cyber Verification Program got Mythos 5.1 access on 6 October, and Anthropic launched a free "OSS Scanner" for qualifying open-source projects on 8 October (Wikipedia). Anthropic's current Managed Agents documentation lists claude-mythos-5-1, claude-mythos-5 and claude-mythos-preview among supported model IDs, so the model family is on the platform for permitted users (Claude Platform Docs) x.

The capability claims drew pushback. Wikipedia records an independent researcher saying Opus 4.6 had actually found the bugs, and Anthropic itself acknowledging that "almost every successful run relies on the same two now-patched bugs." The security firm AISLE reported that all 8 open-source models it tested caught one of the headline flaws. Most tellingly for the "zero-day collapse" story, Patrick Garrity said that as of September only about 10% of findings had been disclosed and under 1% fixed (Wikipedia). On markets, the article contains no reports of cybersecurity stock losses. It cites only a CNBC headline about the private rollout having "rocked Wall Street," and warnings from Bessent and Powell to bank executives. AgentBrief's "billions in valuation" stays unverified here. Regulators in Canada, the UK, India, Japan and Australia held meetings on the risks, and the article reports that Mistral began building a rival model for European banks that lacked access (Wikipedia) x.

Why it matters now. The gating decision held for seven months, but its form changed. It moved from a closed coalition to tiered verification plus a safeguarded general model, Fable. Meanwhile a government directive showed that access can be cut overnight on nationality grounds. For anyone building on frontier models, that is a supply-chain risk, not a footnote. The figure from this story that has aged worst is the implied speed of remediation. If the reported 10% disclosed and under 1% fixed is right, finding bugs has outrun the human patch pipeline, which is where defensive effort is now bottlenecked x.

"Claude's thinking depth dropped 67%" and Anthropic's response (AINews, 8 April 2026)

1 source, 3 min read

What it said. AINews summarised Reddit threads about a GitHub issue claiming Claude Code's "thinking depth" had fallen 67% by late February. Some users suspected deliberate downgrades to free compute for Mythos. Others questioned the method: the 67% was estimated by correlating signature-field length with thinking length, and January logs had been deleted. Firmer signals cited included a read-to-edit ratio falling from 6.6 to 2.0 and stop-hook violations rising from zero to 173 after 8 March. The Claude Code creator, named as "Boris Charny" in the summary, first pointed to a beta header (redact-thinking-2026-02-12) that hides thinking from the UI. He suggested /effort high and CLAUDE_CODE_DISABLE_ADAPTIVE_THINKING=1, and later acknowledged that adaptive thinking was under-allocating reasoning on some turns reddit.

How it aged. The complainants were largely vindicated, though not on the mechanism most of them guessed. InfoQ, reporting on 14 May 2026 on Anthropic's postmortem, says three unrelated product-layer changes between March and April caused about six weeks of quality complaints, and that "the API and model weights were not affected" (InfoQ). The first cause: from 4 March to 7 April, Claude Code's default reasoning effort was lowered from high to medium to fix a UI that looked frozen during long thinking. Anthropic called this "the wrong tradeoff," and most users never changed the default. The second: from 26 March to 10 April, an optimisation meant to drop old thinking sections once, after an hour of idleness, fired on every turn for the rest of the session. Claude kept working but gradually lost track of why it had chosen its approach. Cherny (InfoQ's spelling) described a user with 900K tokens of context hitting a full cache miss after idling, burning a large share of rate limits. The third: from 16 to 20 April, a system prompt shipped with Opus 4.7 told the model to "keep text between tool calls to 25 words or less" and "keep final responses to 100 words or less." Broader ablations later showed a 3% quality drop for both Opus 4.6 and 4.7. Everything was fixed by 20 April in v2.1.116, and usage limits were reset for all subscribers (InfoQ) reddit.

The 8 April thread mixed together several things that the postmortem separates. The redact-thinking header the creator pointed to was a red herring for real degradation: it hid thinking without causing the regression. That explains why the 67% estimate, built on visible thinking, was methodologically shaky even though the underlying complaint was real. In InfoQ's account of the three causes, adaptive thinking is not among them, despite being the creator's first technical admission. The causes actually named, a lowered effort default and a context-clearing bug, are configuration and harness faults that look like reasoning loss from the outside. The firmer evidence came from an independent audit by Stella Laurenzo on GitHub, covering 6,852 session files, 17,871 thinking blocks and 234,760 tool calls. It found a shift from research-first to edit-first behaviour. InfoQ notes that not every claim in it is confirmed by the postmortem but that the symptoms match the three causes (InfoQ). The deliberate-downgrade-for-Mythos theory found no support in the postmortem. A Hacker News commenter quoted by InfoQ offered a milder version: that Anthropic "wanted to cost-reduce idle sessions to the bare minimum, and 'latency' is a convenient excuse." Fortune reported users feeling "gaslit," and Anthropic responded that "compute is a constraint across the entire industry" (InfoQ) reddit.

Two loose ends stay open as of the InfoQ report. Reddit users raised silent sub-agent delegation to Haiku, which the postmortem does not cover. One user said the verbosity instructions were still present after the announced revert and built a pre-tool hook to counter them. Anthropic's stated remedies were more staff on exact public builds, per-model evals for every prompt change, soak periods and gradual rollouts, and more careful versioning of system prompts (InfoQ). InfoQ also reports that in back-tests of Anthropic's Code Review tool, Opus 4.7 caught the caching bug given enough repository context, while Opus 4.6 did not. That is a vendor claim, but a pointed one reddit.

Why it matters now. The lesson for an agent engineer is that the harness is part of the model. An effort default, a cache-eviction rule and a 25-word prompt constraint each degraded output measurably, with weights unchanged. Telemetry from your own sessions, such as read-to-edit ratios, hook violations and tool-call patterns, was better evidence than estimates of hidden reasoning. The other lesson is that a vendor's system prompt is a moving dependency. The Hacker News request to "At least tell users when the system prompt has changed" is still the right demand reddit.

Claude Code Routines (AlphaSignal, 15 April 2026)

1 source, 3 min read

What it said. AlphaSignal led with Anthropic's routines for Claude Code. A routine packages a prompt, repositories and connectors into one configuration that runs on Anthropic-managed cloud infrastructure. It is triggered by a schedule, by an HTTP POST to a per-routine endpoint with a bearer token, or by GitHub events such as pull requests and releases, and each run returns a session URL. Routines could be created in the web UI or with the /schedule CLI command. The same issue noted a rebuilt Claude Code desktop app for multi-session work, OpenAI's GPT-5.4-Cyber for verified defenders, and a Cursor–NVIDIA multi-agent system reporting a 38% average CUDA kernel speedup across 235 problems source.

How it aged. The feature survived but lost its name. As of today the official documentation is titled "Automate work with scheduled tasks" and describes "scheduled tasks, formerly routines." It still carries the note "Scheduled tasks are in research preview. Behavior, limits, and the API surface may change" (Claude Code Docs). So more than six months after launch it has not left preview. The three trigger types are unchanged: schedule ("hourly, nightly, or weekly, or once at a specific future time"), API, and GitHub. A single task can combine them, and a task "doesn't need a schedule" at all, since API-only or GitHub-only tasks are allowed. Two things were added. Tasks can now execute "on your organization's self-hosted environment when routed there," not only on Anthropic's cloud. And availability now lists "Pro, Max, Ultra, Team, and Enterprise plans," with management at claude.ai/code/routines or via /schedule (Claude Code Docs). The docs keep a section explaining where the old "routines" name still appears, the usual sign of a rename partway through. The launch-day per-plan run caps reported elsewhere were not checked against a page read for this issue source.

The self-hosted routing is the most significant change. The original pitch was that workflows run "without a local machine" on Anthropic's infrastructure source. Letting enterprises route tasks to their own environments answers the obvious objection about running unattended agents with repository and connector credentials on a vendor's cloud. It also links scheduled tasks to the broader Managed Agents self-hosted-sandbox work (see the Memory item below, where self-hosted sessions have their own limitations). Adoption numbers are not public. The 17,332 likes AlphaSignal cites for the launch post are engagement, not usage source. Whether "scheduled tasks" has displaced GitHub Actions-based agent runners for teams is not established by anything read here.

On the companion items: the rebuilt desktop app source fits the multi-session direction Anthropic has kept since. The Cursor kernel result was a vendor-run, three-week study and was not followed up for this issue. The Claude Code regression covered in the previous item ran through the same weeks, from 4 March to 20 April. Routines launched inside that window, a week after the lowered effort default was reverted on 7 April and shortly before a system-prompt verbosity limit shipped with Opus 4.7 on 16 April, a reminder that an unattended scheduled agent silently inherits whatever defaults the harness ships that week (InfoQ).

Why it matters now. Scheduled, API-triggered and event-triggered agent runs are now a standard primitive, and the design stayed stable through the rename: prompt plus repos plus connectors, with three trigger types. The long research-preview status means limits and the API surface are still officially changeable. That is a reason to keep the orchestration logic, meaning what triggers what and with which credentials, in your own code or CI, and to treat the vendor's scheduler as replaceable source.

Memory for Claude Managed Agents (TLDR AI via TestingCatalog, 27 April 2026)

1 source, 3 min read

What it said. Anthropic added Memory to Claude Managed Agents. It is a filesystem-based layer that lets agents keep information across sessions without manual prompt updates, stored as files that can be exported, managed through APIs and scoped with permissions. It launched in public beta for all Managed Agents users testingcatalog.

How it aged. Anthropic's own launch post is dated 23 April 2026. It says memory "mounts directly onto a filesystem," that developers "can export them, manage them via the API, and keep full control over what agents retain," and that stores can be shared across agents with different scopes, for example "an org-wide store might be read-only, while per-user stores allow reads and writes." It adds "Multiple agents can work concurrently against the same store without overwriting each other," "All changes are tracked with a detailed audit log," and "You can roll back to an earlier version or redact content from history" (Claude blog). The customer claims are vendor-reported testimonials. Rakuten reports "97% fewer first-pass errors at 27% lower cost and 34% lower latency," and Wisedocs about 30% faster verification. No baseline or method is given for either (Claude blog) testingcatalog.

Six months later the design is intact but still not generally available. The current documentation lists memory stores with status beta and a new beta header, agent-memory-2026-07-22. It warns that sending the old managed-agents-2026-04-01 header together with the new one on a memory-store call "returns a 400 error," while session endpoints still use the old header (Claude Platform Docs). The header change in late July is a breaking change for anyone who set beta headers explicitly, which is the cost of building on a beta. Other limits are documented too. Managed Agents sessions are not eligible for zero data retention, since "transcripts persist until you delete them." On Claude Platform on AWS, and on Anthropic-managed Claude on Google Cloud, sessions on self-hosted environments cannot attach memory stores (Claude Platform Docs). The supported-models list has grown to include the 5-series models (Opus 5.5, Sonnet 5.5, Haiku 5.5, Fable 5.1, Mythos 5.1) alongside the 4.x line, so the feature has been carried through model generations rather than dropped testingcatalog.

The curator expected later memory merges to show how the design aged. On the consumer side, search results describe a 25 August 2026 unification of chat and Cowork memory, and report that Claude Code keeps separate memory. Those pages were not opened for this issue, so treat it as unverified. Nothing read here shows Managed Agents memory being folded into that consumer system. On the evidence available, Anthropic runs at least two memory designs: an explicit, file-based, versioned store for API agents, and a managed, topic-based memory for consumer products. That split is defensible. Enterprises want exportable files, audit trails and redaction, while consumers want memory that just works testingcatalog.

Why it matters now. Memory as plain files under a mount point, with versioning and scoped permissions, has aged better than vector-store-as-memory designs: it is debuggable, diffable and portable. The open risks are those the docs themselves flag, namely beta-header churn, no zero data retention, and gaps on self-hosted sessions. A memory store that persists "prior mistakes" across sessions, as the docs put it, also persists poisoned or wrong entries. The rollback and redaction features are the real product, and the audit log is what will show whether they get used (Claude Platform Docs) testingcatalog.

nanoMoE: Mixture-of-Experts LLMs from scratch in PyTorch (Cameron R. Wolfe, resurfaced on Medium September 2026)

1 source, 3 min read

What it said. The digest teaser promised "An introductory, simple, and functional implementation" of an MoE LLM in PyTorch by Cameron R. Wolfe medium. The digest presented it in September 2026, but the article is much older. The original on Wolfe's Deep (Learning) Focus is dated 10 March 2025 (Deep (Learning) Focus). So this is a 20-month-old tutorial recirculating, not new work.

Paper facts. This is a tutorial with code, not a peer-reviewed paper. Author: Cameron R. Wolfe. The repository, github.com/wolfecameron/nanoMoE, is a fork of Karpathy's nanoGPT. The configuration has 6 layers, 6 attention heads and model dimension 368, with 8 experts and top-2 routing, an MoE block every other layer, and capacity factor 1.25 for training and 2.0 for evaluation. Training used about 25B tokens of OpenWebText, roughly 10% of nanoGPT's token budget, on two RTX 3090s for about 5 days at 50,000 iterations. The losses were a Switch-style load-balancing loss scaled by 0.01 and an ST-MoE router z-loss scaled by 0.001. The stability measures were those auxiliary losses, the router in float32 with the rest in bfloat16, truncated-normal fan-in initialisation with s = 0.1, and optional noisy top-k routing (Deep (Learning) Focus). Total and active parameter counts are not stated in the visible text, nor are loss or perplexity numbers. The result reported is qualitative: each trick on its own delayed divergence, and with all of them "the model actually completes the entire training process without any issues!" One inconsistency: the loss weights used are swapped relative to the "default" values stated earlier in the same article, 0.001 for load balancing and 0.01 for z-loss (Deep (Learning) Focus) medium.

How it aged. Its central premise, that "MoE models are more difficult to train than a standard LLM" and need stabilisation, has become everyday knowledge, because MoE won the open-weights market. Evidence for that comes from today's other sources. The TurboQuant llama.cpp fork covered in the wildcards benchmarks almost entirely on MoE checkpoints: Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, Qwen3-30B-A3B, Gemma 4 26B-A4B and DeepSeek-V4-Flash (GitHub: AmesianX/TurboQuant). The fork's own notes report 5.2x KV compression for the Gemma 4 26B-A4B MoE variant against 1.8x for the dense Gemma 4 31B, and describe DeepSeek-V4-Flash decode as memory-bound medium.

The specific techniques have aged unevenly. The float32 router and the z-loss remain standard advice in the literature this tutorial draws on. The auxiliary load-balancing loss is the part most open to change, since frontier MoE designs have explored alternatives. This issue did not fetch a source establishing which current open models drop it, so that point is left at "not checked". No adoption figures for the repository were verified; a GitHub profile snippet seen in search was not opened. Several unrelated projects share the nanoMoE name, including a JAX version and a 120M-parameter model with DeepSeek-style attention, a sign that "nano" from-scratch MoE has become a teaching genre. Those pages were also not opened, so they are noted only as leads. The model itself is far too small to be useful. Its value was always pedagogical: about 25B tokens on two consumer GPUs, enough to watch routing collapse happen and then fix it medium.

Why it matters now. For someone who runs A3B-class models locally, the tutorial is still the shortest path to understanding why an MoE's memory footprint follows total parameters while its speed follows active ones, why capacity factors and token dropping exist, and why low-bit quantisation of routers is risky. It is a good weekend exercise. Read it as March 2025 material and check current router practice against recent model reports before copying its loss weights, especially given the inconsistency noted above medium.

Databricks: "Reliable LLM Inference at Scale" and model units (TLDR Dev, 28 May 2026)

1 source, 3 min read

What it said. TLDR Dev summarised a Databricks engineering post. To manage multi-tenant LLM serving, Databricks built an abstraction called "model units" that allocates and scales GPU resources by the computational cost of each workload. Wiring them into load balancing and autoscaling can "decrease GPU costs by more than 80%" while meeting latency targets during traffic spikes databricks.

How it aged. Going back to the primary post changes the meaning of the headline number. The 80% is not a saving from model units over some smarter alternative. The post says cost-aware load balancing and autoscaling built on model units "saved over 80% in GPU costs versus static provisioning while maintaining latency targets." The body specifies that "For models with bursty traffic, autoscaling kept replica counts close to actual demand," "translating to over 80% GPU savings compared to static provisioning at peak" (Databricks). The baseline is provisioning for peak demand, the most wasteful possible policy, and the condition is bursty-traffic models. Any competent autoscaler would beat that baseline by a wide margin. The claim to evaluate is model units' contribution: making the autoscaler and router measure load in cost-weighted units instead of request counts. The post gives no ablation isolating that contribution databricks.

The mechanism itself is sensible. The post describes model units as providing "a VM-like abstraction that makes it possible to allocate, route, and scale GPU resources per customer." A replica is projected to process "a fixed number of model units per minute (e.g., 100)." Requests with long inputs or outputs consume more units, with outputs weighted more heavily because decode throughput differs from prefill. Coefficients come from "automated benchmarking for each model on each hardware type," with adjustments for prefix caching and multimodality, though the formula itself was not in the extracted text (Databricks). The more concrete engineering wins are elsewhere in the post. Throughput rose more than 3x in requests per second after fixing image-processing bottlenecks, with the same replicas and load. Image resizing was 10x slower than base64 decoding. The default OMP_NUM_THREADS was a poor choice when a container saw 12 of a host's 192 vCPUs. Prioritised health checks cut false liveness failures from several a week to zero and allowed hang detection and recovery in under 5 minutes. Latency targets are named only as p95 TTFT and output tokens per second, with no numeric values (Databricks) databricks.

Reception was thin and partly machine-written. The most visible analysis, from Futurum Group (29 May 2026), carries a byline disclosing that it was written by a commercial LLM, was not reviewed by editors, and does not "perform any independent analysis." It repeats the 80% figure and asks whether "these efficiencies can be sustained as workloads diversify and scale" (Futurum). It cites "over 120 trillion tokens" served monthly, while the Databricks post as read today says more than 125T. Either the post was updated after publication or the figures were transcribed differently; the post shows no date, only "© Databricks 2026" (Databricks). "Model units" also predates this post as the provisioning unit in Databricks' provisioned-throughput product (a search lead, not opened), which suggests the abstraction was extended from billing to scheduling rather than invented for it databricks.

Why it matters now. For anyone running multi-tenant inference, the durable idea is that requests are the wrong unit of load. Cost-weighting by prefill and decode tokens is what makes routing and autoscaling stable. The 80% figure should not be repeated without its baseline. The less glamorous findings in the post, CPU-side preprocessing bottlenecks, thread-count defaults in containers and health-check starvation, are the ones most likely to apply to your own vLLM or SGLang deployment this week databricks.

Ways of thinking

Action produces the information inaction waits for

3 sources, 2 min read

Shane Parrish's Brain Food this week opens with a one-liner: "Action produces the very information inaction waits for." The next line: "Being smart helps once you start. Before that, it often just makes your excuses more convincing." He also quotes Paul Graham on problems having "a hard core at the center, surrounded by easier stuff at the edges," and on aiming "as close to the center as you can without stalling" source.

The first line is about where information comes from. Many decisions are framed as "gather more data, then act." But for a large class of decisions, the decisive data only exists downstream of a small action. Waiting does not reduce uncertainty. It just postpones the experiment that would. The model breaks down when the action is irreversible or expensive. In that case waiting is information-gathering by other means, and the cleverness Parrish warns about is legitimate caution. The skill is telling the two cases apart: reversible and cheap, act; irreversible and expensive, investigate source.

Today's lead is a clean case. The question "are time-series foundation models any good on my data?" cannot be answered by reading Mistral's leaderboard tables. They are vendor-run, submitted rather than accepted, and sit inside a benchmark ecosystem that a preprint argues "risks producing overly optimistic performance estimates" because of leakage (arXiv) source. Debating the leaderboard produces only more convincing reasons to wait. Running the 28M checkpoint on a held-out month of your own series costs an afternoon on a CPU, and produces the one number that matters. The Brazilian data scientist who answered Hacker News scepticism by testing Timestral on ONS grid load, in a post on r/datasciencebr, did exactly this. The action is reversible and cheap, so the model applies fully.

The Graham quote fits the Claude Code regression story in the archive. The edges of that problem were easy to argue about: whether hidden thinking counted, whether users had changed settings, whether Anthropic was saving compute for Mythos. The hard core was measurement. Stella Laurenzo's audit of 6,852 sessions and 234,760 tool calls aimed at the centre: what did the agent actually do, read-first or edit-first? That framing held up when the postmortem arrived (InfoQ) reddit. The 67% estimate built on hidden-token proxies was work at the edges. It was clever, but it was the shakiest part of the case reddit. Put together: when uncertain, take the cheapest action that produces direct evidence about the core question, not the most sophisticated argument about the peripheral ones.

Gemma 4 + TurboQuant + RAG for self-hosted OCR (Medium, 13 April 2026)

1 source, 2 min read

Why this is here: exploring Inference infrastructure, kernels, and model tooling.

The saved item is a Medium piece by Gao Dalie, "Gemma 4 + Turboquant + RAG: Better OCR & Self-Hosted." Only the teaser was available and the article was not read, so its specific pipeline and results are not reported here medium. The stack it names can be checked, and how it fared since April is the useful part.

TurboQuant is Google Research's KV-cache quantiser, published on Google's blog on 24 March 2026 by Amir Zandieh and Vahab Mirrokni and presented at ICLR 2026. Google says it can "quantize the key-value cache to just 3 bits without requiring training or fine-tuning," reduces "the key value memory size by a factor of at least 6x" on needle-in-a-haystack tasks, and that "4-bit TurboQuant achieves up to 8x performance increase over 32-bit unquantized keys on H100 GPU accelerators" (Google Research). Read sceptically, the 8x is "up to," relative to 32-bit keys rather than the usual 16-bit, and covers attention-logit computation rather than end-to-end inference. The text names Gemma and Mistral, while the LongBench chart caption cites Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct. The "zero accuracy loss" claim comes with no stated limitations (Google Research).

In practice, TurboQuant on Gemma 4 lived in community llama.cpp forks. One of the more documented, AmesianX/TurboQuant, claims "5.2x memory reduction with near-lossless quality," for example 5,120 to 990 MiB of KV memory in its v1.0.0 benchmark. For Gemma 4, though, its own numbers are less flattering. On Gemma 4 26B-A4B it reports "4.2x compression, 6% PPL gap," with wikitext-2 perplexity of 445.7 against 419.8 for f16. It warns that "Gemma 4's head_dim=512 extends beyond the TurboQuant paper's validated range (head_dim=128)," and at one point said the QJL 1-bit correction "does not work at D=512," which a later release reversed. The repository has 92 stars and states "this repository is being archived" (GitHub: AmesianX/TurboQuant). Search results also report that the upstream llama.cpp TurboQuant pull request was closed without merging and that mainline adopted Hadamard rotation of KV activations instead. The model card making that claim could not be read (HTTP 401), so it stays unverified.

Does it hold up? The idea, rotating and then scalar-quantising the KV cache, is real and published. But the self-hosted recipe a reader would copy in April depended on an unmerged fork, applied outside its validated head dimension, on a model where the fork's own perplexity gap was 6%. For an OCR-plus-RAG pipeline, where the long context is mostly retrieved document text, KV-cache compression is the right lever to try. The conservative path is mainline llama.cpp's standard q8_0 or q4_0 KV types, with an exotic fork adopted only after testing it on your own documents.
