Anthropic's amended S-1 says it "may imminently" meet its own RSI threshold

4 sources, 4 min read

What happened. On Monday, 2 November 2026, Anthropic filed a revised registration statement (S-1/A) with the SEC. Among the new risk factors is language saying the company may soon meet the Responsible Scaling Policy (RSP) definition that covers AI accelerating AI development. TechCrunch's Julie Bort reported it as fine print sitting under the headline financials. Gizmodo's Mike Pearl (syndicated on AOL) compared it with the 26 October version of the filing and reports that the "imminently" wording is new in this amendment (TechCrunch; Gizmodo via AOL) techcrunch.

Details. The key sentence, as quoted by TechCrunch, is that "our internal estimates and our expectations regarding future models, rather than our current models," indicate "we may imminently meet our Responsible Scaling Policy's definition" of "dramatically accelerated progress attributable to AI." The filing pairs this with an explicit denial: "We have not designated any of our current models as exceeding this threshold." The same passage adds that Anthropic continues to assess the underlying data. The filing calls this an area of the RSP that "sets out our plans to monitor and mitigate the risks as we approach what has been termed 'recursive self-improvement.'" It warns that "Using AI to help build more capable AI could also pose a considerable safety risk". It also warns that AI-assisted R&D could make "the capabilities of our models to advance more rapidly than our ability to develop, validate, and implement" matching safety measures (TechCrunch) techcrunch.

The most important sentence for anyone reading this as evidence is an admission about detection. Anthropic writes that "we have in the past not identified" such an acceleration, and "may in the future not identify, a significant acceleration in our rate of development until after it has occurred." So the company is telling investors that its measurement of the very quantity at issue lags reality. The filing also says Anthropic does not expect to "safely use Claude to fully autonomously design and build successive generations of frontier models" "without meaningful human supervision or understanding," and that "we are considering updates to our Responsible Scaling Policy to keep humans in control of frontier model development." Nothing in the reported text mentions pausing development (TechCrunch) techcrunch.

How hard is the threshold to meet? Gizmodo gives the RSP's operational definition as a goal to "double the baseline rate of improvement, with that doubling plausibly attributable to AI." The piece also describes the mitigations the RSP envisages. Anthropic would "explore ambitious and possibly unconventional ways to achieve unprecedented levels of security," and Gizmodo summarises the aim as "eyes on everything" over its internal activities (Gizmodo via AOL). The RSP text itself was not fetched for this issue, so that wording is Gizmodo's account of the policy, not checked against it. Neither article says what "baseline rate" means in practice: which metric, over what window, measured how. That gap matters, because it decides whether "imminently" is a measurement or a forecast techcrunch.

Facts vs. opinions. The facts: the language is in a securities filing, it was added between the 26 October and 2 November versions according to Gizmodo, and it rests on internal estimates about future models, not on any current model crossing the line. The rest is interpretation. Calling it "self-building AI", as the TechCrunch headline does, goes further than the filing, which talks about accelerated progress attributable to AI, not autonomous self-construction. The filing itself rules out fully autonomous generation-to-generation development without human supervision. No named outside AI-safety researcher, regulator or analyst is quoted in the coverage read for this issue techcrunch.

Other perspectives. TechCrunch points to sceptics, via an X post it links, who argue that an S-1 is a marketing document as much as a disclosure, so saying you are near self-building AI doubles as a sales pitch. Bort's own view in the same piece is that Claude's coding, math and cybersecurity skills have grown sharply, so the warning may deserve to be believed and perhaps acted on with governance. Gizmodo is sardonic. It asks whether Anthropic is disclosing to risk-averse investors or announcing to a world eager to greet its "mechanical god", and says the company had better get moving on its ambitious mitigations (TechCrunch; Gizmodo via AOL). A third reading, which neither article makes, is legal. Securities law punishes risks that are known but not disclosed far more than risks disclosed too early. Lawyers drafting a prospectus have every reason to write "may imminently" if internal forecasts point that way, whatever the marketing team thinks. That reading is an inference, not something the sources state techcrunch.

Two items in today's bundle bear on the substance. The "Sparks of Weight-Space Programming" post (next story) is a concrete case of a frontier model doing hands-on ML work on another model. A Hugging Face paper titled R²Orch, on recursive self-improvement for agent orchestration via self-play, drew 89 upvotes on the same day (quick hits). Neither shows a doubling of any lab's rate of progress. Both show that "AI doing AI research" is now concrete research activity, not just a slogan seangoedecke +1.

Why it matters. For the reader's recursive self-improvement interest, this is a frontier lab putting a forecast of its own RSI-adjacent threshold into a document with legal liability attached. The useful facts are the three admissions inside it. The threshold is defined as a rate (doubling), not an ability. Anthropic says it has missed accelerations until after they happened. And the planned response is to revise the policy to keep humans in control, not to pause. Watch for three things: the RSP revision itself; whether the "baseline rate" metric is ever published; and whether OpenAI's own filing, which CNBC says is targeting a 2027 debut, carries similar language (CNBC) techcrunch +1.

Anthropic's S-1/A financials: $33.24B nine-month revenue, $104B net loss, ~$895.4B committed

The nine-month net loss was $104 billion, against a $25.1 billion loss in the same 2025 period, and the Q3 net loss was $23.29 billion. Total commitments across purchase agreements and leases are about $895.4 billion, "substantially all of which is related to compute and infrastructure" (CNBC) cnbc.

What CNBC does not give: gross or operating margins, cash on hand (a related headline mentions a "cash pile" but no figure), valuation, IPO date, share price or offering size. It says only that the roadshow is coming. CNBC says the S-1 has "more than 90 pages of risk factors," including political headwinds, infrastructure complexity and the tension between safety and commercial goals. It quotes the co-founders: "We consider it our personal responsibility to help the world understand these risks" (CNBC) cnbc.

Facts vs. opinions. The headline loss is real in the accounts but is mostly a non-cash mark-to-market on financing instruments, according to CNBC. Other perspectives. CNBC points to market concerns: rising interest rates are straining data-centre debt, and hyperscaler capital spending is eating into their cash flows, which could slow how fast compute capacity comes online. For a company with close to $900 billion of commitments that are substantially compute, the risk runs both ways. If capacity comes online slowly, revenue is capped by supply. The TechCrunch RSI piece notes that coverage of the filing focused mostly on revenue soaring alongside expenses (CNBC; TechCrunch) cnbc +1.

One link between the stories is worth spelling out. The same document says revenue growth is slowing quarter on quarter and that AI-accelerated R&D may imminently cross a policy threshold. An investor could read the second as the growth engine for the first. A safety reader could read the first as the commercial pressure behind the second. The filing contains both, and the co-founders' quote about responsibility sits between them cnbc +1.

Why it matters. These are the first audited-style quarterly numbers from a frontier lab heading to public markets, and they set the frame for OpenAI's filing. CNBC says OpenAI filed confidentially in June and is targeting 2027. For an engineer choosing providers, the practical reading is that Anthropic's supply problems of 2026 have a scale: compute expense up 329%, and commitments in the hundreds of billions. That is the backdrop to this year's rate-limit, plan-restructuring and access disputes revisited in the archive below (CNBC) cnbc +1.

"Sparks of Weight-Space Programming": GPT-6 Astra reads and edits network weights, and the case that it is ordinary research

2 sources, 6 min read

What happened. On 2 November 2026, Rikiya Takehi and Jayden Teoh (equal contribution), with Alex Zhang and Omar Khattab, all at MIT CSAIL, published a blog post titled "Sparks of Weight-Space Programming: LLMs can natively read & write neural network weights." It tests OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra on eight tasks that involve reading raw weights or writing them directly. The next day Sean Goedecke published a rebuttal titled "LLMs are not superhuman at reading and writing neural network weights." Its thesis is "I think a smart human ML researcher could perform all of these tasks" (Takehi et al.; Goedecke) seangoedecke.

Details. The experiments, as the authors report them. In the first, four PPO policies for grid versions of Breakout, Pong, Pac-Man and Freeway, each an identical 500→32→32→5 tanh MLP with 17,253 weights trained for 1 billion PPO steps, were shown to Astra as raw weights with no tools. It named the correct game for 4 of 4 networks without being given a list of options. In the second, three small diffusion models (23,824 weights) generating 12×12 circles, squares and triangles were identified correctly. Astra drew them as outlines, though the models generate filled shapes. The third used character-level transformers (7.16 million weights) trained on Python, romaji or English, with input and output layers removed and up to 100 Python analysis runs allowed but no execution. Astra identified the language correctly in both attempts for every model, with confidences between 0.48 and 0.62. It also located the copying (induction) heads with no false positives against ground truth (Takehi et al.) seangoedecke.

The writing experiments are stronger claims. In experiment four, a Kinetix car-on-ramp policy (171,664 weights) that succeeded in 0 of 256 episodes was edited one-shot with no tools, changing 3.5% of weights, and then succeeded in 1,024 of 1,024. In experiment five, Astra wrote MinAtar networks from all-zero weights with no data, gradients, tools or rollouts. Over 1,000 episodes it scored 517.4 on Breakout against 25.3 for PPO at 500 million steps, 53.1 on Seaquest against PPO's 88.9, and 53.2 on Asterix against PPO's 55.2. In experiment six, it wrote a 1,480,274-weight 9×9 Go network in a single 336-line Python run with zero forward passes. That network won more than half its games against two small PPO networks over 2,000 games each, and with 50 forward passes it beat an equal-sized PPO network trained on 20 million games. In experiment seven, Astra failed 4 of 4 times to tell which of a pair of Gemma 3 270M fine-tunes was sycophantic from weights alone. Given an attention-pattern tool, it picked correctly in 3 of 3 pairs (Takehi et al.) seangoedecke.

The Qwen result that the newsletters headlined is experiment eight. The base Qwen3.5-0.8B, in thinking mode, loops: 85 of its first 96 practice answers hit the 32,768-token cap. Astra removed a single "looping" direction from the MLPs in layers 15 and 19, changing 131,072 weights, or 0.016% of the model. At the post's 50-pass setting, MATH-500 went from 7.9 to 48.1, GSM8K from 13.8 to 67.1 and IFEval from 21.0 to 62.3. The share of answers hitting the token limit fell from 93.0 to 33.1. At 10 and 25 passes, the gains were far smaller (MATH-500 12.3 and 14.1) and looping barely fell. A control on Gemma 3 270M, which rarely loops (8.5% of answers hit the limit), stayed essentially flat on MATH-500, from 6.8 at base to 7.0 at 50 passes (Takehi et al.) seangoedecke.

Paper facts. This is a blog post, not a peer-reviewed paper or arXiv preprint. Its suggested citation is a @misc blog entry, dated 2 November 2026. Authors: Rikiya Takehi and Jayden Teoh (equal contribution), Alex Zhang, Omar Khattab, all MIT CSAIL. The tested model is GPT-6 Astra; the post does not state access terms or model version details. Most networks tested are small (17k to 7.16M weights), the exceptions being the 1.48M-weight Go net and the 0.8B Qwen. Baselines are PPO at various step counts, plus the unedited base models. For the Qwen edit, AMC23, Minerva Math, OlympiadBench, GPQA Diamond and MMLU-Pro are listed as evaluated but no scores are reported. The authors' stated limitations, verbatim: "Fixing loops, however, is not the same as making a model better at math"; "Editing weights could remove an obstacle that hid an ability, but it could not add an ability the model lacked"; capabilities are "still limited, specifically due to inefficiencies in context length and harnesses". They also describe the results as very early. Full logs (prompts, responses, reasoning summaries, code, networks, evaluations) are published on GitHub. The edited Qwen is on Hugging Face. A benchmark and harness are described as still in development (Takehi et al.) seangoedecke.

Facts vs. opinions. The word "superhuman" does not appear in the MIT post. Its closest claims are that capabilities "may extend beyond what we'd expect from humans or conventional algorithms" and, of reading weights, "For almost any human, this is practically impossible." It also predicts that "our neural network weights will soon be natively optimized by LLMs that deliberately reason about their weights." The newsletter summary in today's bundle says Astra "significantly improved" Qwen's mathematical skills. That framing is contradicted by the authors' own caveat that loop removal is not a math improvement seangoedecke.

Other perspectives. Goedecke's case runs task by task. The game networks have input channels mapped directly to grid cells with labels such as "agent", so the L2 norm of first-layer weights per input gives a heatmap of the game layout, which he reproduces in three lines of Python. He calls doing this without code "superhuman mental mathematics, not superhuman AI research." He treats the Qwen edit as the most impressive result but says it closely resembles standard steering-vector and abliteration techniques (he cites the Heretic tool), and that "this is exactly what a competent human researcher could have and would have done." His conclusion is that weight editing is like patching compiled binaries, and that LLMs will advance AI research "by improving training, not by magically tweaking model weights directly." One point of disagreement in framing: Goedecke characterises the base model as already solving problems that need fewer than 32k tokens. The MIT post's numbers show only that most practice answers hit the cap, which is compatible but not the same claim (Goedecke) seangoedecke.

The Hacker News thread on Goedecke's post (137 points, 22 comments at fetch time) split. User aesthesia argued the post corrects a misreading "that seems to have been encouraged by the authors." User simias called the work "fairly standard reverse-engineering." On the other side, aaroninsf called the headline "False, in a way that genuinely matters," arguing the models are superhuman in the plain wall-clock sense. kenjackson doubted typical ML engineers could match the one-shot Kinetix edit and the zero-shot MinAtar networks. StilesCrisis disputed Goedecke's claim that humans would easily spot Pac-Man from a heatmap (Hacker News). These are commenters' opinions seangoedecke.

Why it matters. Both sides are partly right, and the useful split is between experiments. The reading tasks and the Qwen loop fix are plausibly within a skilled researcher's reach, as Goedecke argues. Writing a MinAtar Breakout policy from zeros that outscores 500-million-step PPO, with no rollouts, is a different kind of result. Goedecke handles it only in a footnote, noting that simple programs can be hand-compiled into networks. For the RSI question in the first story, the relevant observation is the post's own: editing removed an obstacle and did not add an ability. That limits the direct-weight-editing route to self-improvement and leaves training, as Goedecke argues, as the main channel. The logs are public, so this is checkable seangoedecke +1.

OpenAI's Pando: a custom Git backend for an agent-driven monorepo

2 sources, 3 min read

What happened. OpenAI published "Scaling Git at OpenAI" on 3 November 2026, describing Pando, a new server-side backend for its monorepo. OpenAI's page returned HTTP 403 to the fetcher for this issue and was not read. The account below rests on Interesting Engineering's write-up, the newsletter summary in the bundle, and a search-result lead for the @OpenAIDevs post that was not opened (Interesting Engineering) openai.

Details. The newsletter summary says Pando was built for concurrent pushes to many branches and maximum read throughput, was deployed without interrupting engineering work or changing how engineers use Git, "cut median push latency in production by nearly 5x," and "now delivers nearly a petabyte of Git data per day." Interesting Engineering reports the same latency gain as a drop of about 80% in median push latency. The two agree: an 80% cut leaves 20% of the original latency, and 1 ÷ 0.2 = 5x. The article's other figures, all attributed to OpenAI: merge reliability of 99.99%, after earlier periods that sometimes fell "below 90 percent"; more than 57 TB of packfiles served in one hour with over 99% of the data from local cache; 10 Gbps network links saturated during clone and fetch; and growth of 2,442% in quarterly monorepo commits for Q4 2026 against Q1 2024. Since Q4 2026 is still in progress, that figure is presumably a projection; the article does not say. Pando was built in Rust in 10 weeks, with one engineer designing the core architecture and teams of Codex agents implementing it, and went into production in April 2026 (Interesting Engineering) openai.

The architecture, per Interesting Engineering: "Pando takes a different approach through causal consistency." It "coordinates updates independently for individual branches instead of imposing one global sequence." "When a developer pushes code, Pando stores the incoming Git packfile in cloud object storage," and "Its metadata service then records the updated branch version and its dependencies," which "allows unrelated branches to accept updates concurrently." On reads, "Pando also reuses previously generated packfiles during downloads," an "Express delivery service stores packfiles in regional caches," and "Workers combine smaller packfiles into larger groups." The stated trade-off: a reader looking across several branches may see them at different versions, but each branch is internally consistent. The article contrasts this with replica-coordination and shared write-ahead-log designs. It does not name the product Pando replaced, describing it only as traditional Git infrastructure (Interesting Engineering) openai.

Facts vs. opinions. Every performance number is self-reported by OpenAI, and the primary page could not be read here. There is no independent benchmark, no description of the previous system's load profile, and no CPU or cost figure in the article read. A search-result lead claimed Pando serves its traffic with a handful of CPU cores, and that OpenAI is not open-sourcing Pando but upstreaming Git fixes. Neither claim was checked against a page that was read openai.

Other perspectives. Search-result leads (not opened) also mention outlets comparing Pando with an Anthropic system named Scion, and a Greg Brockman post offering Pando to others. Neither is verified here. On the engineering itself, the design is familiar to anyone who has run large Git hosting. Content-addressed packfiles in object storage, per-ref consistency rather than global ordering, and pre-computed pack reuse at the edge are how big hosts scale reads. What is new is the cause. The newsletter summary and Interesting Engineering both tie the commit surge to Codex, with humans and agents pushing in parallel. A global sequence of ref updates is the natural bottleneck when hundreds of agent branches update at once (Interesting Engineering) openai.

Why it matters. For the reader's security and infrastructure interest, this is a concrete data point that agent-driven development changes the load shape of developer infrastructure: many writers, many branches, read amplification from CI and agent sandboxes. The causal-consistency trade-off is one an agent harness has to tolerate. An agent that reads two branches may see them at different moments, which matters for merge tooling and for any security control that assumes a single global ordering of ref updates. The claim that Codex agents built most of it in 10 weeks is a vendor claim with no code to inspect. It also fits the story Karri Saarinen tells in today's Ways of thinking section: output is cheap, and the bottleneck moves to review and coordination openai +1.

Claude message threads: stop resending the transcript

4 sources, 3 min read

What happened. On 2 November 2026, Anthropic put "message threads" into public beta on the Claude Platform. Instead of sending the whole conversation on every Messages API call, an agent sends only new messages plus a reference to the previous response (Claude) claude.

Details. Per Anthropic's page, you "add the thread parameter to Messages API requests." "The first request includes the full conversation and starts a thread." "Each request after that includes only the new messages and the ID of the previous response." Anthropic holds each thread "in memory, where it's encrypted and not written to disk," and "A thread expires 5 minutes after its last request." After that, the client must resend everything to start a new thread. "Claude still reads the full conversation on every turn, so token usage stays the same," and "Standard Claude token rates and existing rate limits apply." The single performance figure: median time to first token fell by about 35% for requests of 500,000 to 1 million tokens, measured with threads on by default for most Claude Code users. The feature is available on the native Claude Platform, on Claude Platform on AWS, in Microsoft Foundry and on Google Cloud (Anthropic managed). Anthropic lists the best fits as long-running coding agents, computer-use agents whose screenshots stay in the thread instead of being re-uploaded, and document- or image-heavy workflows (Claude) claude.

The announcement page does not state a beta header, how threads interact with prompt caching, which models are supported, or limits on thread size or concurrency. It points readers to the documentation, which was not read for this issue claude.

Facts vs. opinions. The 35% is a median on Anthropic's own traffic in a specific band of very long requests. It is not a general latency claim, and the page gives no figure for shorter contexts. The page's own qualifier, "The more context an agent carries, the more message threads can reduce time to first token," implies smaller gains below that band. Since token usage and pricing are unchanged, the saving is wall-clock time, plus whatever client bandwidth and serialisation cost goes away. It is not money claude.

Other perspectives. No independent reaction was found. X searches returned nothing and Reddit search failed with a 403 during research. The engineering logic is easy to reconstruct. A 1-million-token request is several megabytes of JSON, often containing base64 images, that has to be uploaded, parsed, tokenised and matched against the prompt cache on every turn. Holding the conversation server-side removes all of that work before the first token. The trade-offs follow from the page's own terms. Threads are state held by the provider, so a 5-minute idle expiry means any agent that waits on a human, a long CI job or a rate-limit backoff falls back to full resends. The client still needs the full transcript for that fallback, so local state management does not get simpler. And "in memory, encrypted, not written to disk" is a meaningful data-handling statement, but the page does not say how it interacts with zero-data-retention agreements. That question is worth putting to an account team before turning threads on for regulated work claude.

Why it matters. This is harness plumbing, and it matches several of today's quick hits. Framer executes agent code as it streams, another item argues for collapsing tools into one exec tool, and LangChain's harness changes moved a fixed model from 53% to 67% on Terminal-Bench (all in quick hits). The common thread is that latency and overhead in the loop around the model now matter as much as the model. For anyone running Claude Code-style agents at 500,000 tokens and up, a roughly one-third cut in median time to first token compounds over hundreds of turns per session, which the page describes as typical. It also nudges the API toward stateful sessions, closer to OpenAI's Responses-style chaining. The comparison is from background knowledge, not checked for this issue claude +3.

The Claude Mythos leak (AINews, 31 March 2026)

3 sources, 3 min read

What it said. Seven months ago, AINews summarised r/singularity threads on a Fortune report. Anthropic was testing "Claude Mythos," described in leaked draft materials as its "most powerful AI model ever developed." It sat in a new "Capybara" tier above Opus, was described as a "step change" in reasoning, coding and cybersecurity, and was to be rolled out cautiously, first to organisations able to strengthen cyber defences. The leak was attributed to a CMS misconfiguration, which Anthropic called human error. Commenters doubted the "accidental" framing and noted the irony of a cyber-capable model leaking through a security lapse reddit.

How it aged. The main checkable predictions came true, though the release took an unusual shape. According to Wikipedia's Claude Mythos article (dated sources throughout, not an Anthropic page), leaked blog drafts revealed the model on 26 March 2026. Anthropic announced Mythos Preview and Project Glasswing on 7 April and did not release the model publicly, "citing the model's ability to find software vulnerabilities." More than 40 companies got access, including Microsoft, Apple, Google, AWS, the Linux Foundation, Cisco, Nvidia and Broadcom. So the defenders-first rollout described in the leak is what happened (Wikipedia) reddit.

Then access widened step by step. On 28 May, in the Opus 4.8 announcement, Anthropic said it expected to offer "Mythos-class" models to all customers within weeks. On 2 June access expanded to "150 organizations in more than 15 countries." The same article elsewhere says Glasswing grew "from 50 to 200" organisations in May, so the article contradicts itself. On 9 June Anthropic released Claude Mythos 5 and Claude Fable 5, saying "the two models are identical apart from their safeguards." Fable 5 sends flagged cyber, bio/chem and distillation requests to Opus instead. A 12 June US government letter barring non-US nationals from either model led Anthropic to cut access for every customer. Access began returning for some US organisations on 26 June, Commerce lifted the restrictions on 30 June, and access resumed on 1 July. Mythos 5.1 and Fable 5.1 followed on 1 September (Wikipedia) reddit.

The commenters' security irony also aged well. Wikipedia cites Bloomberg reporting on 21 April that people in a private Discord got into Mythos on launch day "using details from the recent Mercor data breach." On capability, Wikipedia records Anthropic's claim of flaws in "every major operating system and every major web browser," Mozilla's 271 Firefox vulnerabilities found and patched, and more than 10,000 high or critical vulnerabilities found by the first 50 Glasswing partners. By September, according to Patrick Garrity as cited there, only about 10% had been disclosed and fewer than 1% fixed. Wikipedia also records Anthropic's own caveat on exploit tests: full code execution succeeded 72.4% of the time, but below 5% without the two most exploitable bugs, because "almost every successful run relies on the same two now-patched bugs." An AISLE test found that all 8 open-source models it tried could also find one of the headline bugs (Wikipedia) reddit.

What the leak got wrong or left out: the "Capybara" tier name does not appear in the later releases in Wikipedia's account. The public-facing brand became Fable, and Mythos stayed the restricted variant. As of 4 November, Anthropic's pricing page lists Fable access on Pro through usage credits, included in Max "up to half your weekly limit," and Fable 5.1 on the API at $10 per million input tokens and $50 per million output tokens. Mythos appears only as a navigation item (Claude pricing) reddit.

Why it matters now. Mythos is the backdrop to today's lead stories. The capability jump it represented is the kind of thing the S-1 says Anthropic has "in the past not identified" until afterwards. The vulnerability backlog (10,000-plus found, under 1% fixed by September) is the context for today's security quick hits on AI-assisted exploitation. The two-track release, with the same weights shipped under different safeguards to different customers, is now a template that other labs may copy reddit +2.

Cursor trains Composer to summarise its own context (17 March 2026)

3 sources, 2 min read

What it said. In a Cursor research post dated 17 March 2026, Federico Cassano and Sasha Rush described building context compaction into reinforcement learning. "Composer generates from a prompt until a fixed token-length trigger is reached." Then "We insert a synthetic query asking the model to summarize the current context," with scratch space to think, and it continues from the condensed context. Crucially, "the self-summaries themselves are part of what gets rewarded": the final reward applies to every token in the chain, so "poor summaries that lost critical information are downweighted" (Cursor) cursor.

Paper facts. This is a company research blog post, not peer-reviewed. Internal DDoS: when output outruns attention

4 sources, 2 min read

Karri Saarinen (Linear) argues, in a post dated 2 November 2026, that organisations now face a denial-of-service attack from inside. No one is malicious. The volume of well-meant work simply exceeds the capacity to review and decide: "We can generate changes much faster than we can give them that attention." The risk is "leadership losing the ability to steer." His three moves follow. Stop "treating output as proof of value," because "A working implementation shows that something can be built, not that it belongs in the product," and "Some changes have negative value." State intent upfront ("what we're betting on, what matters right now, what's out of scope") so that "People and agents can check their own work against it." And gate releases deliberately. Linear "had to lock down our internal release channel because things were changing too quickly," and the gate "gives us one place to consider changes together before they land." He also admits the gate tracks change but does not confirm direction (Karri Saarinen) karrisaarinen.

The model generalises as a queueing statement. When arrival rate exceeds service rate, queues grow without bound, and the fixes are to raise service rate, lower arrivals, or drop requests at admission. Agents raise arrival rate almost for free. Review, the service rate, is still bounded by human attention. Saarinen's intent statement is admission control (rejecting work early by checking it against declared scope), and his release gate is batching. The DDoS analogy also suggests a fix he does not name: rate limiting at the source, meaning a cap on how many open agent branches each person or team may have.

Today's news fits it closely. OpenAI built Pando because agent-driven commits, a 2,442% quarterly increase by its own figure, overwhelmed its Git servers. That fixed the machine side of the queue but not the human side (Interesting Engineering). Maple's 287,000-line React port by about 40 Claude agents took three days. But code review still found about 180 defects the 2,600-variant screenshot harness missed, which is what review capacity is for. Anthropic's S-1 disclosure that it may soon reach its internal definition of recursive self-improvement, where AI systems help develop increasingly capable AI models, is the same structure at a lab: generation outrunning verification. The honest version of Saarinen's argument applies there too: a gate tracks change without confirming direction karrisaarinen +3.
