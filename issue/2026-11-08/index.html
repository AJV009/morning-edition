Harness-S: training one agent policy with help from 20 sibling harnesses

1 source, 5 min read

What happened. A preprint posted to arXiv on 3 November 2026, "Harness-S: Learning from Sibling Harnesses for Generalizable Agent Training", proposes training small agent models on rollouts from a family of related harnesses. The harnesses are used only when the model's own harness fails. The authors then claim the trained policy keeps the gains when run on the original harness alone (arXiv abstract). It had 54 upvotes on Hugging Face's daily papers page when the curator picked it up huggingface.

Details. The paper defines the harness as the "prompts, tool interfaces, context and memory mechanisms, and execution rules" through which a policy acts on its environment. Formally, a harness's utility for a policy and task is the expected verifier success of trajectories sampled under it (arXiv HTML). "Sibling" harnesses all descend from one base harness, H₀, and share its execution foundation, interfaces and verifier. The authors' starting observation, from the abstract, is that these siblings show "Locality, Complementarity, and Nonstationarity": different variants fix different failures, and which one helps changes as the policy learns (arXiv abstract). The method has three parts. First, Directional Harness Evolution builds specialised siblings along separate repair directions over 3 evolution rounds with 4 parent slots, using GLM-5.3 as the editor. The result is a fixed pool of H₀ plus 19 auxiliary harnesses. Second, Complementary Harness Rescue Training runs a GRPO-style optimiser with asymmetric clipping (0.20 and 0.28), no KL term and 40 updates. Rescue triggers only when all K=8 rollouts under H₀ fail. A router then picks the Top-8 auxiliary harnesses, the same policy produces one fresh rollout under each, and those rollouts replace the failed group in the RL batch. Third, an Adaptive Harness Router ranks harnesses using "Harness Behavior Profiles" and multi-LLM voting: each voter ranks 4 candidates with weights 1, ¾, ½ and ¼, and the profiles are refreshed every 10 updates in the AppWorld 4B run (arXiv HTML). At inference the router can also pick a single best harness ("Rank-1") from the 20 huggingface.

Paper facts. This is an arXiv preprint (2611.04277v1, 3 November 2026, CC BY 4.0) with no venue stated. The eleven authors are from Shanghai Jiao Tong University (including corresponding authors Weiwen Liu and Weinan Zhang), Xiaohongshu Inc. (corresponding authors Wenxiang Jiao and Yuan Lu), the Zhongguancun Institute of Artificial Intelligence, Southeast University and Carnegie Mellon (arXiv HTML). The trained models are Qwen3.5-4B and Qwen3.5-9B. The benchmarks are AppWorld (train/val/test 320/150/262), DBBench (1,600/200/300) and SWE-Gym (240/100/160). All scores are means over 3 randomised runs with 3 rollouts per task, reported as Pass@3 / Avg@3. On AppWorld, the 9B model trained with Harness-S and evaluated on the base harness scores 72.52 Pass@3 and 62.34 Avg@3. Plain GRPO on H₀ scores 64.89 and 53.31, and the untrained 9B base model scores 50.00 and 39.69. On SWE-Gym the 4B model goes from 24.38 / 22.50 under GRPO to 38.75 / 28.75 with Harness-S on H₀. On DBBench the 9B model goes from 69.67 / 67.00 to 76.33 / 73.44. Rank-1 routing at inference adds a little more: the 9B model reaches 75.19 / 67.05 on AppWorld, 43.75 / 36.25 on SWE-Gym and 78.00 / 75.67 on DBBench. The compared harness-optimisation methods are Meta-Harness, WHALE and HarnessForge, each evaluated on its own best harness. WHALE is the strongest: its 9B scores are 68.70 / 58.02 on AppWorld, 33.75 / 30.63 on SWE-Gym and 74.00 / 71.11 on DBBench. The frontier reference rows, run on H₀ and not trained, are GPT-6-Sol at 82.82 / 76.34 on AppWorld, 53.13 / 47.50 on SWE-Gym and 71.33 / 68.89 on DBBench (arXiv HTML). All experiments ran on 8 NVIDIA H800 GPUs. Code is at the DeepExperience/Harness-S GitHub repository, and no weights are mentioned (arXiv abstract). The portion of the HTML read for this issue (the first 100,000 of about 111,000 characters) has no dedicated limitations section. The conclusion lists future work: lasting transfer, reusing the pool across policies, exchanging task state across harness interfaces, and "Harness Scaling" (arXiv HTML) huggingface.

Facts vs. opinions. The tables establish that, on three benchmarks and two small Qwen3.5 models, this training recipe beats plain GRPO and three harness-search baselines when the policy is tested back on the base harness. Error bars run from about ±1.2 to ±2.4 points across three seeds. They do not establish the abstract's broader reading, that "harness-induced behaviors transfer into the policy itself". The paper supports that with "behavioral analyses and held-out cases", which is suggestive but not a controlled test of internalisation (arXiv abstract). The authors also report that under external harnesses (Claude Code and OpenClaw), the Harness-S policy still scores below its own H₀ performance (arXiv HTML). So "generalizable" in the title means generalising back to its own base harness more than to arbitrary harnesses. The compute picture is mixed. In the AppWorld 4B training-cost table, Harness-S used 3,032 rollouts and 67.19 GPU-hours to reach 56.87 Avg@3 on H₀. Plain GRPO used 2,560 rollouts and 50.58 GPU-hours to reach 48.98, and the "H₀ rescue" control, which re-samples under the same harness, used 3,616 rollouts and 97.77 GPU-hours to reach 51.91. Counting harness search, the full Harness-S pipeline cost 75.79 GPU-hours and 18.69M API tokens. WHALE cost 95.84 GPU-hours and 9.80M tokens, and Meta-Harness 35.24 GPU-hours and 4.89M tokens (arXiv HTML). Harness-S is therefore not the cheapest pipeline on API tokens, and part of its cost is paid in frontier-model calls during harness evolution.

Other perspectives. One control in the main table deserves more attention than the paper gives it. Mixing a single strong external harness into training, "GRPO Mixed (H₀ + Claude Code)", made the 4B AppWorld result worse than plain GRPO: 57.63 / 46.69 against 60.31 / 48.98. Mixing in the Top-4 siblings indiscriminately was also worse on AppWorld and DBBench (arXiv HTML). That supports the authors' central design choice of using the sibling pool only as a rescue when every base attempt has failed. It also warns against the folk practice of "just train on trajectories from the best harness". The paper's other implicit claim is that harness engineering and policy training are complements rather than substitutes. The Meta-Harness-style search baselines, which only optimise the harness, end up well below the trained policy. No independent replication or third-party reaction was found as of 8 November; the paper is five days old, and the Hugging Face vote count is the only public signal in the bundle huggingface.

Why it matters. For anyone building coding or tool-use agents, the numbers here are on small models and benchmark environments with clean verifiers. Variance in harness quality can be harvested as training signal rather than treated as noise, and selective rescue on all-fail groups addresses GRPO's zero-advantage problem without relaxing the verifier. Before reusing the idea, check two things the paper leaves open: whether the gains hold at frontier scale, where base-harness failure rates are much lower so rescue triggers rarely, and how much of the 18.69M API tokens per run is unavoidable (arXiv HTML) huggingface.

PACT: when a big model's prefill feeds a small model's decoder, is anything actually being communicated?

1 source, 4 min read

What happened. A 35-page preprint from King's College London, Tencent Youtu Lab and the UK National Physical Laboratory, posted on 4 November, studies "Prefill-to-Autoregressive Cross-model Transfer" (PACT). In this setup a large model encodes the prompt and a smaller model generates, to trade capability against inference cost. The authors release an open framework, PLatour. Their more important finding is that on some tasks PACT gains can be reproduced without any useful signal from the large model (arXiv abstract) huggingface.

Details. PLatour connects frozen models with "lightweight, zero-initialized linear bridges trained directly with task rewards", and it supports hybrid-attention architectures (arXiv abstract). There are two experimental families. In the first, Qwen3-235B and Qwen3-8B senders feed a Qwen3-1.7B receiver. They are tested on answer-only tasks (ARC-C, MMLU-Redux, Chemistry) and chain-of-thought generation tasks (Chemistry, GSM8K, MATH500). In the second, Qwen3.5-397B and Qwen3.5-35B senders feed a Qwen3.5-4B receiver. A math model is trained on DAPO-17k and evaluated on AIME, HMMT 2025 and IMO-AnswerBench, and a coding model is trained on DeepCoder and evaluated on LiveCodeBench v6, HumanEval and MBPP (arXiv HTML). The external comparison is Mostik, a closed system that pairs a GLM-5.2 (753B) sender with a Qwen3.5-4B receiver and reports closing 47% of the sender-receiver gap. The paper proposes PACT-Check, a measure of how much of a trained system's gain truly stems from communication, rather than from a bridge that simply acts as an adapter for the receiver (arXiv abstract) huggingface.

Paper facts. This is an arXiv preprint (2611.06976v1, 4 November 2026, CC BY 4.0) with no venue stated. The authors are Zhenyi Shen, Yulan He, Lin Gui, Yali Du and colleagues at King's College London; Di Yin and Xing Sun at Tencent Youtu Lab; and Tameem Adel at the National Physical Laboratory. Funding is from a UK EPSRC Turing AI Fellowship (arXiv HTML). Twelve baselines are compared, including C2C, KV-Lingo and a text-communication baseline. On the Qwen3.5-4B receiver, receiver-alone math averages 48.90 and coding averages 62.87. PLatour with the 397B sender reaches 60.97 math and 77.20 coding; with the 35B sender it reaches 63.32 and 79.73. On its own, the 35B sender scores 68.37 math and 77.09 coding. So the PLatour system with the 35B sender beats that sender on coding. Mostik's reported numbers are 70.74 on AIME-135 and a 74.09 coding average. On the shared benchmarks, PLatour's 35B-sender system scores above Mostik with a much smaller sender. The authors flag that "this is not a fully controlled comparison" because "Mostik's training data are undisclosed". They also note that 55 of AIME's 135 problems overlap with DAPO, 53 of them in training. On Qwen3, PLatour-SFT with the 235B sender reaches 78.57 on answer-only Chemistry, against 39.05 for the receiver alone and 56.19 for the sender alone (arXiv HTML). Code is at the zhenyi4/platour GitHub repository, and no weights are mentioned (arXiv abstract) huggingface.

Facts vs. opinions. The authors undercut their own leaderboard numbers. That is the main reason to read the paper. In their controls, using uninformative senders, self-communication or receiver-only training, "on GSM8K, all four checks achieve comparable performance" to PLatour. On Chemistry, "PLatour substantially outperforms all four sanity checks", which they treat as evidence of real transfer. Receiver-only training (TR) "generally outperforms SC and is the strongest control in most settings". In the Qwen3.5 setting, "neither Qwen3.5-based PACT system surpasses its compared TR baseline in domain-averaged accuracy" (arXiv HTML). The PACT-Score table makes the same point numerically. C2C gains +29.72 points over the receiver on MMLU-Redux with the 235B sender but has a PACT-Score of +22.14. On generation tasks C2C's gains are negative (−0.42, −5.01, −5.50) and its PACT-Scores more so. The GRPO-trained PLatour shows a +27.86 gain on generation Chemistry but a PACT-Score of only +3.34 (arXiv HTML). The authors' own summary: "Performance improvements alone therefore do not establish the contribution of cross-model information transfer", and "even outperforming the sender does not by itself establish beneficial cross-model information transfer" (arXiv HTML) huggingface.

Other perspectives. The paper takes on a closed competitor (Mostik) and also argues against its own headline results, which makes it more credible than most method papers. Its stated limitations are concrete. With the 397B sender, "A single pronounced spike in clipping during training is followed by a substantial and persistent decline in performance". When the bridge behaves as an adapter, the setup risks "reducing the sender to an expensive source of noise". Extending PACT across model families "with heterogeneous representation spaces" remains open, and the evidence for shared representations is only "consistent with the possibility" (arXiv HTML). No outside reaction was found as of 8 November. The appendix tables for the controls (A20 and A21) were beyond the portion of the HTML read, so per-control accuracies are not reported here huggingface.

Why it matters. Latent communication between models is being sold as an inference-cost lever: run the expensive model once over the prompt and let a cheap model decode. This paper's lesson applies beyond PACT. Any system with a trainable interface between components, such as adapters between retriever and generator or bridges between vision encoder and LLM, needs ablations where the upstream signal is replaced by noise, by the receiver itself, or removed. Otherwise a parameter-efficient fine-tune gets attributed to "communication". If a vendor quotes a "% of gap closed" figure for a large-small pairing, ask for the PACT-Check-style controls first huggingface.

EvoMemState: giving agent memory "relational" and "reasoning" state, not just facts

3 sources, 3 min read

What happened. A Xiamen University-led preprint, posted on 2 November, proposes EvoMemState for long-term memory in personal agents. Instead of storing user facts as independent items, it attaches states describing how each fact remains valid, how it relates to others, and how it applies to the current request (arXiv abstract) huggingface.

Details. The abstract argues that today's systems extract, organise, update and retrieve facts but mostly keep them as "independent facts". They therefore miss how new facts revise, limit or contradict older ones, and how much each fact should matter for a given request. EvoMemState is "a state-centric framework that augments persistent facts with two complementary states". Relational States track validity, scope and evolving relations among facts and are built by "Ontology-Guided Memory State Induction". A Reasoning State, produced per request by an RL-trained policy, decides how facts apply. A State-Aware Reasoning step combines the facts, their relational states and the reasoning state to guide the response (arXiv abstract). In the implementation, DeepSeek-V4-Flash builds the memory offline, the policy is Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 trained with GRPO, and response backbones are Qwen3-8B and GPT-4o-mini (arXiv HTML) huggingface.

Paper facts. This is an arXiv preprint (2611.03743v1, 2 November 2026, cs.IR, CC BY 4.0) with no venue stated. Corresponding authors are Jinsong Su (Xiamen University) and Qinggang Zhang (Jilin University), with co-authors at NTU, UBC and Waterloo. The page lists some author emails against the wrong names (arXiv HTML). It is evaluated on four benchmarks: MemSyco-Bench, MemTrapBench, LoCoMo and a refined LoCoMo. Nine baselines are compared: NaiveRAG, MemGPT, MemoryBank, Mem0, A-MEM, LightMem, Supermemory, SimpleMem and PGMem. Zep and MemOS are cited but not compared, and LongMemEval appears only in related work. With Qwen3-8B, EvoMemState's overall score is 59.9, against 53.6 for the best baseline (SimpleMem) and 39.2 for Mem0. With GPT-4o-mini it is 61.2, against 54.2 for MemoryBank and 52.8 for SimpleMem. The claimed margins are +12.2 on the MemSyco-Bench average with Qwen3-8B and +6.1 on the LoCoMo-Refined judge score. In ablations with Qwen3-8B, the overall score falls from 59.9 to 55.4 without the Relational State, to 53.0 without the Reasoning State, and to 53.2 without RL training. The stated limitation is that "Our study focuses on text-based interaction histories" while "Real-world personal memory systems must also handle multimodal information". Code is at XMUDeepLIT/EvoMemState on GitHub, and no weights are mentioned (arXiv HTML; arXiv abstract) huggingface.

Facts vs. opinions. EvoMemState wins clearly on the averages but not on every column, and the abstract's "consistent improvements" covers over that. With Qwen3-8B, SimpleMem beats it on the MemTrapBench average (79.9 against 79.1) and on original-LoCoMo F1 (46.4 against 41.6). With GPT-4o-mini, SimpleMem leads on original LoCoMo on both judge (47.2 against 46.4) and F1 (48.4 against 45.2) (arXiv HTML). The big gains are on MemSyco-Bench, which measures sycophancy-like misuse of memory, and on the authors' refined LoCoMo variant. On the unrefined, widely used LoCoMo, it roughly ties the strongest baseline. Two preliminary findings are also worth noting. MemoryBank, LightMem and Supermemory miss some relevant memory in 58.9%, 61.8% and 58.0% of cases. A fixed self-reflection step lowered average accuracy from 50.5% to 48.9% (arXiv HTML). Latency and token cost appear only in an appendix (C.4) that was beyond the portion read, so the cost of an RL policy call plus ontology induction per request is not stated here huggingface.

Other perspectives. Baseline selection matters in agent-memory papers. Mem0, a widely used production system, scores poorly here (39.2 overall with Qwen3-8B, and 25.7 on refined-LoCoMo judge), which may reflect configuration as much as design. Zep, whose temporal knowledge graph targets exactly the "fact supersedes fact" problem, is cited but not run (arXiv HTML). This is the kind of graph-memory approach covered in today's Towards AI quick hits medium. The transfer experiment is the most practical result. Adding the trained policy's reasoning states to other memory systems gave gains of 0.2–4.9%, including 4.9 for NaiveRAG and 4.8 for MemoryBank. That suggests the "reasoning state" can be bolted on without adopting the whole framework (arXiv HTML) huggingface.

Why it matters. Personal agents are now a product category. Claude Duet can send daily briefs and keep tabs on connected apps, according to CNBC's coverage of the launch, and Meta's Muse and OpenAI's Dots compete in the same space (CNBC on Duet). Their typical failure modes are acting on a stale preference or over-weighting a remembered fact in an unrelated request. These are exactly what the paper's relational and reasoning states target huggingface +1.

BestRand: random subsets almost compress benchmarks well enough, and here is how to fix the tail

2 sources, 3 min read

What happened. A Bosch Research preprint with a CMU co-author, posted on 31 October, analyses why benchmark-compression methods are unstable and proposes BestRand. It is a cheap variant of random sampling that cuts the rare large errors (arXiv abstract) huggingface.

Details. The theory says that a random subset's estimation error is long-tailed. Most subsets give small errors, a few give large ones, and the tail is governed by a "common shift" term that depends on the average correlation between models. Because that term tends to a nonzero limit as the number of models grows, the instability is "inherent to modern foundation models" and does not go away by evaluating more models (arXiv abstract; arXiv HTML). The algorithm is simple. Sample a pool of n·k items uniformly, run p cheap probe models on the pool, and take the pool's mean probe-score vector as a stand-in for the full benchmark's. Draw m random k-subsets from the pool and keep the one whose probe mean is closest in L2 distance. Then evaluate every target model on that one subset. The defaults are p = 5 probes, m = 128 candidates and n = 4. The only overhead beyond random sampling is p × |pool| probe evaluations (arXiv HTML) huggingface.

Paper facts. This is an arXiv preprint (2611.00883v1, 31 October 2026), labelled "preprint" by the authors, with no venue. The authors are George Ling, Lijin Yang, Hao Yang, Nanxiang Li and Zhongzhan Huang of Bosch Research and Shanshan Zhong of Carnegie Mellon. It is tested on 70 benchmarks (44 text, 16 multimodal, 10 agent) plus a VLA extension in an appendix, at retention ratios from 2% to 50%. Random and BestRand were each run with 10,000 seeds; the other methods with 3. The baselines are Random, SparseEval (linear and MLP), Anchor Points, TinyBenchmarks and ZipBench, plus four more in an appendix. Mean absolute error averaged over all retention ratios is 2.92% for BestRand on text against 3.47% for Random, 3.23% for ZipBench and 3.65% for TinyBenchmarks. On agent benchmarks it is 5.43% against 6.14% for Random. OOD mean error is 1.69% against 1.95% for Random. The abstract's headline figures are defined as follows. The 16.4% is the average MAE reduction relative to Random, ZipBench and TinyBenchmarks. The 50.2% is the reduction in "high-error" probability against three strong baselines, where high error means exceeding Random's 70th-percentile or mean error. The 51.3% is BestRand's construction cost as a share of data-dependent baselines' cost (8.0% at 2% retention). The 21.8% is the average OOD-error reduction across score-range, model-family and temporal shifts. The limitations appendix (D.1) was past the portion read; only its headings, "Nonzero estimation error" and "Probe inference cost", were visible. The main text calls its assumptions "useful approximations rather than exact descriptions of the data". Code and data are at George-Ling3/BestRand on GitHub (arXiv HTML; arXiv abstract) huggingface.

Facts vs. opinions. The comparison table supports the title's half-joke. Random sampling is already competitive with the clustering-based TinyBenchmarks and ZipBench, and the regression-based SparseEval variants are far worse (11.87% and 20.98% MAE on text) (arXiv HTML). BestRand's improvements over Random are modest in absolute terms: half a percentage point of mean error on text, and about 0.7 on agents. The paper's real claim is about tail risk, which matters if a leaderboard or regression gate runs on one subset with no re-draws. The theoretical improvement is idealised; in the authors' words it reflects "the idealized benefit of probe matching, rather than a guaranteed contraction for finite-pool selection" (arXiv HTML). The agent-benchmark errors stay above 5% even for BestRand. That is a reminder that compressed agent evals cannot resolve small differences between models huggingface.

Other perspectives. The finding that adding more models does not remove the tail runs against a common intuition behind IRT-style methods: that with enough calibration models, item parameters stabilise and subsets become reliable. The paper's argument is that correlated errors across modern models create a shared shift that no model count averages away (arXiv HTML). That fits METR's experience with time-horizon suites (see the archive), where measurement becomes "extremely noisy" near saturation x. No outside commentary on BestRand was found as of 8 November huggingface.

Why it matters. Teams that run nightly evals on cut-down benchmarks get a nearly free method here: five cheap probe models and 128 candidate draws, with no item-response modelling. They also get a theoretical reason to stop trusting one random subset blindly. The practical advice from the paper's own numbers is to treat compressed scores on agent benchmarks as having errors of roughly 5–6 points, and to pick a subset by probe matching rather than taking the first random draw huggingface.

"No, it doesn't cost Anthropic $5k per Claude Code user" (Martin Alderson, 9–10 March 2026)

2 sources, 3 min read

Reading path: State of Brain Emulation Report 2025, part 2: mapping brain structure (connectomics)

3 min read

What to read. Read the connectomics chapter of the State of Brain Emulation Report 2025, by Niccolò Zanichelli, Maximilian Schons, Isaak Freeman, Philip Shiu and Anton Arkhipov. It is posted as arXiv 2510.15745, with a third version on 5 November 2025 and no journal reference (arXiv). A companion website carries a Zenodo DOI and lists funding from Fieldcrest Ventures, MxSchons GmbH and the Foresight Institute (report site). The site says the chapter starts around page 58 of the full PDF. Start with the website's connectomics overview, then the LessWrong launch post of 28 January 2026, which summarises each chapter's conclusions and points newcomers to an Asimov Press explainer (LessWrong). The arXiv HTML version returned a 404 when fetched, so the chapter's own tables were not read for this issue. The figures below come from the site and the launch post.

Why this step. The report organises brain emulation around three capabilities: recording activity (neural dynamics), mapping structure (connectomics) and modelling (computational neuroscience) (arXiv). Connectomics is the step where the field's progress is most measurable and where the scaling gap is starkest. The site's framing is that "The central challenge is acquiring data, not computation" (report site).

Key ideas. First, where things stand. "Complete connectomes at synaptic resolution currently exist only for small organisms." There are about ten whole-nervous-system reconstructions of C. elegans (about 300 neurons), each from a single specimen. Fully proofread connectomes exist for both the male central nervous system and the female brain of Drosophila (about 140,000 neurons). For mammals, the largest densely reconstructed volume is one cubic millimetre of mouse visual cortex, with roughly 120,000 neurons and 523 million automatically detected synapses, and larger organisms remain at "the proof-of-concept stage" (report site). Second, cost has fallen fast. According to the launch post, cost per neuron dropped "from an estimated $16,500 in the original C. elegans connectome to roughly $100 in recent larval zebrafish projects". The drivers were faster electron microscopy, automated tissue handling, and algorithmic image processing and neuron tracing (LessWrong). Third, the bottlenecks have moved. "Dataset sizes now increasingly reach petabyte scale", which strains storage and makes sharing data harder. "Proofreading… remains the most time- and cost-consuming factor", especially for large mammalian neurons. Current reconstructions are essentially contour tracing that "lack molecular annotations of key proteins" (LessWrong). Fourth, the proposed way out is expansion microscopy with molecular annotation, including "protein barcoding for self-proofreading", along with machine learning to remove the proofreading bottleneck (LessWrong). Fifth, the scale ahead. A mouse has about 500 times as many neurons as a fly, and a human about a million times as many. The site quotes "The map of our brain is 1000x larger than the map of earth" and says emulating sub-million-neuron organisms such as flies, larval zebrafish and bees looks feasible within about a decade at a cost in the low hundreds of millions of dollars (report site). The site also says fewer than 500 people work on the field worldwide.

A detail that complicates the project. The site states that "40-50% of synaptic connections differ between identical worms" (report site). If true at that scale for a genetically identical nervous system of about 300 neurons, then a single connectome is a sample from a distribution, not the blueprint. That is why the report recommends aligned structural and activity datasets from multiple individuals of the same organism as ground truth (LessWrong).

Open questions. The report's conclusion warns of a "widening gap between our ability to reconstruct ever-larger connectomes and our much more limited capacity" to record activity across them. It calls for methods to "infer functional properties of neurons and synapses primarily from structural and molecular data" (LessWrong). Two questions stay open: which biological details (gap junctions, glia, neuromodulators) an emulation needs, and whether structure alone can reliably predict function. One timeline caveat: a third-party blog claims the report implies human emulation around 2055–2065. According to search summaries, the report's own site says it gives no specific timelines; that specific statement was not found in the portion of the site read here, so treat any year attached to this report as unsourced. A useful next step after this chapter is the neural-dynamics chapter, because the structure-versus-activity gap the authors emphasise is only half the argument.

Ways of thinking

The empty self, and the harness as situation. In Psyche, Jack W Klein of Flinders University and William B Swann, professor emeritus at UT Austin, argue that much of 20th-century psychology implicitly modelled people as Homo vacuus. This is a creature whose attitudes shift with trivial cues like a hot drink or a washed hand, because its self contains "little that isn't susceptible to the random influences of the situation". They call this "the empty-self metaphor" (Psyche) psyche. Their evidence is the replication record. Their own survey of studies found that "studies reflecting an empty-self view were far less likely to hold up", and one report found "only 17 per cent" of social-priming findings replicated. They reread the classics too. In Milgram's obedience studies, traits linked to diligence and helpfulness predicted willingness to shock, which recasts the results as "not mindless obedience, but often wilful collaboration". They argue the Stanford prison experiment had "a selection bias that likely caused dispositionally authoritarian individuals to be overrepresented". The alternative they propose is interactionism: "the person and the situation interactively dictate behaviour", and people show "a remarkable propensity to shape our situations just as they shape us" (Psyche) psyche.

The model generalises well beyond psychology. When a system behaves differently in different contexts, it is tempting to conclude that context is everything, because context effects are easy to show and surprising to read about. The interactionist correction is to ask how much behaviour is carried by stable internal structure, and to measure it by moving the system between situations. Today's lead paper is a direct agent-engineering instance. A harness is the agent's "situation": prompts, tools, memory and execution rules. Harness engineering as a field rests on an empty-self assumption, that the right scaffolding will overwrite whatever the policy does. Harness-S tests both sides. Pure harness search (Meta-Harness) and policy–harness co-evolution (WHALE, HarnessForge) get real but limited gains. Training lets the harness-induced behaviour become part of the policy, which then keeps most of the gain under the plain base harness. Yet under foreign harnesses like Claude Code and OpenClaw the trained policy still loses ground (arXiv HTML) huggingface. That is interactionism in numbers: behaviour depends on both the disposition and the situation, and neither erases the other.

The same framing helps with the PACT result. A bridge that improves the receiver whether or not the sender says anything shows that the "situation", the sender's signal, was doing less than the leaderboard implied huggingface. Klein and Swann also warn about why the empty-self view persists. It promises hidden levers and counterintuitive findings, which journals and audiences reward. Agent-benchmark culture has the same incentives. The practical habit is to report every scaffolding gain together with the result after the scaffold is removed. Klein and Swann's caveat applies here too: "reality is much messier" (Psyche) psyche.
