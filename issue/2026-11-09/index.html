Latent Sparse Attention: removing the "search tax" from sparse attention at 10M tokens

1 source, 8 min read

What happened. Researchers at Prefrontal AI and ETH Zurich have published "Latent Sparse Attention: Towards Subquadratic Ultra-Long Context Attention" (arXiv 2610.42147). It is an arXiv preprint: v1 went up on 30 October 2026, v2 on 3 November, and the abtract page shows no venue. The code is Apache-2.0 at Prefrontal-AI/latent-sparse-attention. James Whittington announced it on X on 2 November as "one of our first open research releases". AlphaSignal's Sunday deep dive, written by Ben Dickson, summarised it for developers. That summary is the item in today's bundle source.

Details. The problem is narrower than "attention is quadratic". Sparse-attention methods such as DeepSeek Sparse Attention already attend to only a few thousand keys per query. To choose those keys, though, a cheap indexer still scores every earlier key or block, so the selection step stays quadratic over a full prefill. The paper's headline diagnosis comes from GLM-5.3-Flash, which uses a DSA variant. At 10M tokens, the indexer takes 98% of a sparse-attention layer's prefill time, and the authors estimate that a "free" indexer would make that layer 58x faster in prefill and 8.9x faster in decode (arXiv HTML) source. An X summary by @milhouscc says the 98% was measured on one global attention layer, on one H200, processing the final 32,768-query chunk. That detail matches the paper's prefill chunk size of 32,768 queries, but the post is commentary, not the paper.

LSA projects keys into a 64-dimensional index space, pools consecutive keys, and clusters them into buckets within 512-key blocks. Each bucket averages 8 keys, with a minimum of 4. The system caches a centroid for each bucket. A query scores the centroids, expands the winning buckets into up to 4,096 long-range keys, and adds 4 sink tokens and a 512-key recent window. The model's normal attention kernel then runs over that list. The README says "The attention kernel is unchanged. LSA only decides which keys it reads" (GitHub). There are two search modes. Flat search scans every centroid, which is much cheaper than scanning keys but still asymptotically quadratic. Tree search builds a hierarchy and beam-searches it, which the blog describes as moving the cost "from O(T²) to O(T log T)" (Prefrontal blog). There are also two ways to integrate it. Distilled LSA (D-LSA) trains only the index projections to imitate a frozen model's attention; the blog gives the distillation budget as 352M tokens. Native LSA (N-LSA) clusters the attention's own keys during pretraining, uses only next-token loss, and needs no teacher source.

Paper facts. The paper has ten authors: Ales Kubicek, Andrea Caciolai, Liangzu Peng, Sukrit Sumant, Benjamin Hoover, Fraser Greenlee, Ruairidh M. Battleday, Radim Urban, Torsten Hoefler and James C. R. Whittington. It is a preprint under CC BY 4.0 with no venue stated.

  • Models and baselines. D-LSA is tested on two models. Qwen-3.8-27B is compared against full attention. GLM-5.3-Flash is compared against its own block-sparse attention.
  • Single-layer speed on one H200, 10M tokens. On GLM, prefill is 22.7x faster and decode 4.6x faster. On Qwen, prefill is 72.4x and decode 42.5x faster.
  • The GLM prefill gain depends heavily on length. It is 2.1x at 512k and 3.5x at 1M.
  • Single-layer speed on B300, 10M tokens. GLM: 12.5x prefill and 5.2x decode. Qwen: 42.9x prefill and 18.4x decode.
  • End-to-end serving on eight H200s, 10M tokens.
  • Qwen (tensor-parallel 8 with decode context parallelism 8): time to first token (TTFT) improves 9.21x with Flat and 8.83x with Tree. Time per output token (TPOT) improves 2.87x. The full-attention baseline's TTFT is 4,582.2 seconds.
  • GLM with context parallelism: TTFT improves 2.24x and TPOT 2.10x, against a baseline TTFT of 451.5 seconds.
  • GLM with tensor parallelism: TTFT improves 5.83x and TPOT 1.91x.
  • Short contexts can be slower. At 512k, several end-to-end figures fall below 1x; Qwen's TPOT, for example, is 0.87x. On B300, some 512k and 1M entries drop as low as 0.63x.
  • RULER, Qwen. D-LSA Flat stays within 5 points of full attention up to 1M and beats it at 10M, 63.9 against 59.9. The gain comes mainly from two subtasks: MultiQuery (92 against 72) and HotpotQA (35 against 20).
  • RULER, GLM. D-LSA Flat stays within 3 points of the sparse baseline up to 1M, trails it by up to 8 points at 2M to 5M, and is within 4 points at 10M.
  • RULER, Tree search. On GLM at 10M, Tree scores 55.8 against Flat's 66.8. On MultiKey 1, MultiValue and variable tracking (VT), Tree falls to 60, 56 and 55, where the baseline scores 100.
  • RULER, all methods. MultiKey 2, MultiKey 3 and common-word extraction (CWE) drop to 5 or below at 10M for every method, baselines included.
  • Short-context benchmarks. Every delta on MMLU-Pro, GPQA-Diamond, AIME25, MATH500 and LongBench v2 sits inside the reported 95% confidence intervals. The LongBench v2 interval is plus or minus 11.6 points on 60 questions, which makes that check weak. Qwen D-LSA Flat's LongBench v2 delta is minus 5.0.
  • PaperTrail, the authors' new benchmark. Qwen at 10M scores 29.1 with D-LSA Flat against 19.8 with full attention.
  • N-LSA setup. About 0.5B parameters in a hybrid with Gated DeltaNet; every fourth of 24 layers is attention. Trained on 100B FineWeb-Edu tokens at 4k, then 12B tokens at 128k.
  • N-LSA validation loss at 128k. N-LSA and full attention both score 1.085. PISA scores 1.100, NSA 1.117 and sliding-window attention (SWA) 1.154.
  • N-LSA RULER at 128k. N-LSA 32.2, full attention 34.7, NSA 19.2, PISA 14.6, SWA 3.0.
  • N-LSA training speed and memory. Each layer trains 1.1x faster than FlashAttention-3 at 64k and 1.6x faster at 128k. The whole model finishes its 128k decay phase 1.24x faster. Peak per-layer memory is higher: 6.8 GiB against 5.8 GiB at 128k.
  • Stated limitations. LSA cuts the cost of finding keys "but not of storing them", so the KV cache still grows linearly. Tree search loses accuracy faster than Flat past 1M on GLM. N-LSA was tested only at about 0.5B parameters, up to 128k, with Flat search. Full-model sparse training was skipped because of cost.
  • What is released. Code, D-LSA weights for both models, ten N-LSA checkpoints, an SGLang fork and Docker images. The N-LSA trainer is not released.

The related-work section was not read for this issue, so whether LSA is compared with earlier clustering-based KV retrieval methods is not checked (arXiv HTML; GitHub) source.

Facts vs. opinions. AlphaSignal's numbers match the paper, but its framing chooses the flattering configuration each time. Its "more than 9 times" TTFT figure for Qwen is the Flat result in one parallelism layout. Its "2.2 times" for GLM is the context-parallel layout; the tensor-parallel layout gives 5.83x. It describes the N-LSA 1.24x as "end to end", where the paper's figure covers the 128k decay phase of a 0.5B model. It says N-LSA "retained more long-context accuracy than other baseline attention methods such as" SWA. That is true against the sparse baselines, but N-LSA still trails full attention on RULER at 128k, 32.2 against 34.7. The deep dive does pass on the main caveats: GLM's RULER loss at 2M to 5M, and that "the reported gains become much smaller in shorter context tasks" source.

Whittington's launch post says LSA "matches accuracy of full attention at ultra-long context". That is the authors' claim, and it rests mostly on RULER, a synthetic suite, and on PaperTrail, a benchmark they built themselves. The blog's wording is the same, and it quotes the GLM shortfall alongside it (Prefrontal blog). The Milhous summary notes that RULER above 1M uses 20 prompts per task, and that the 10M results are past the models' advertised context windows. Neither point was checked against the paper's appendix for this issue. If they hold, the 63.9-versus-59.9 Qwen result is a small-sample comparison in a regime neither model was trained for.

The blog says very little about the company: no founders and no funding are stated (Prefrontal blog).

Other perspectives. The most useful independent signal so far comes from Christopher Owen, who maintains a DeepSeek V4.1 Flash serving stack on DGX Spark machines. He built a training-free variant that uses plain BF16 means over every 8 keys instead of a learned latent space. He reports the key search running 1.27x faster at 32K and 4.07x faster at 1M in prefill. End-to-end answer time "barely moved: +1.9%, inside the noise". His fastest recipe "lost one 128K answer that native got right". He ships it off by default and posted "unfavorable runs included" (X post, 8 November).

Replying to him, Joseph Sauvage made a point worth keeping. On 12 questions, "9 vs 8 of 12 is one discordant answer", which is no evidence of an improvement under a sequential test. He also noted that his own production contexts are short, so LSA "may show little".

A third post, from @Oluwaphilemon1, relays an Apple Silicon port of Qwen3.8-27B on an M3 Ultra. At 128K it reportedly cut TTFT from 640s to 440s and peak memory from 40.7GB to 28.8GB, by using LSA only for prefill and dense attention with multi-token prediction for decode. This is secondhand and unverified.

The repo has 441 stars, 53 forks and 5 open issues. Its README warns that "a failed sync does not fail loudly later". It also warns that the server must be checked for an armed log line, because without it the server "is running the checkpoint's own attention" (GitHub). Anyone benchmarking it needs that check. Otherwise a misconfigured run would quietly measure the baseline.

Why it matters. For agent builders, the useful result is a correction to how sparse attention gets reasoned about. Choosing which keys to read is now the cost to measure, not the attention itself. At 10M tokens, the paper's own measurements show that search outweighs everything else in an already-sparse layer. LSA does not solve memory. A 10M-token KV cache for Qwen still needs eight GPUs, and the index adds 246 MiB per layer for D-LSA, against 331 MiB for GLM's existing index (arXiv HTML). That is why the archive item on Prefill-as-a-Service below belongs next to this one: hybrid linear attention attacks storage and transfer, while LSA attacks selection, and a production long-context stack probably needs both.

Practical guidance follows from the paper's own tables. Below about 1M tokens, expect little or nothing. Prefer Flat search to Tree until accuracy past 1M is fixed. Measure TTFT and TPOT separately on your own traces before trusting any multiplier in a headline source.

KernelEvolve: Meta's agentic kernel writer (originally covered 6 January 2026)

1 source, 3 min read

What it said. KernelEvolve was presented as an agentic framework that takes a kernel specification and generates and tunes kernels for Meta's recommendation models. It targets several generations of Nvidia and AMD GPUs and Meta's own MTIA accelerators, with the stated aim of lowering "the programmability barrier for new AI hardware" arxiv.

Paper facts. The paper is arXiv 2512.23236, by Gang Liao and 38 other authors. v1 went up on 29 December 2025 and the current v4 on 6 July 2026; the arXiv page has no comments field naming a venue. The abstract claims a "100% pass rate on all 250 problems across three difficulty levels" of KernelBench, "100% correctness" on 160 PyTorch ATen operators across three hardware platforms, and development time cut "from weeks to hours". Speedups in production use cases are given as 1.25x to 17x in search summaries of the paper; the 17x was for an MTIA-specific RMSNorm, according to a secondary summary not checked against the PDF. Code release: not stated, and no official repository was found. Baselines for the production speedups: Meta's blog says the comparison was against a baseline already using torch.compile and vendor libraries. Limitations: not checked.

How it aged. It shipped, at least by Meta's own account, and it became infrastructure for further research.

Meta Engineering's post of 2 April 2026 places KernelEvolve inside the "Ranking Engineer Agent" programme. It reports more than 60% higher inference throughput on the Andromeda ads model on Nvidia GPUs, against that torch.compile-and-vendor-library baseline, and more than 25% higher training throughput for an ads model on MTIA. It also says the system is "optimizing code that serves trillions of daily inference requests" (Engineering at Meta). These are vendor-reported production numbers and cannot be reproduced from outside.

Two details in the post explain why the approach might generalise. The first is the retrieval design. "Because these chips are proprietary, no public LLM has been trained on MTIA code," so the system injects MTIA documentation at inference time. Retrieval stands in for pretraining on a hardware target no model has seen. The second is breadth. The language list has grown to Triton, TLX, CuTe DSL and FlyDSL, plus CUDA, HIP and MTIA C++ (same source).

The blog says the paper "will appear at the 53rd International Symposium on Computer Architecture (ISCA) 2026". The IEEE Computer Society listing did not render when fetched, so acceptance is confirmed only by Meta's own post. A citing source cited in a search summary calls it the 52nd ISCA, a discrepancy left unresolved.

The strongest evidence of lasting value is a follow-up paper. "Experience Graphs: The Data Foundation for Self-Improving Agents" (arXiv 2606.29823, 29 June 2026), again first-authored by Gang Liao, proposes Trellis. Trellis stores an agent's search history as governed, queryable database state, on the idea that "search over experience graphs is a database access pattern". The authors "ground the design in KernelEvolve". They report that reusing experience across sessions "reaches a target speedup roughly 10x faster at 52% lower token cost". In other words, the kernel agent's search trees turned out to be worth keeping, which is the same lesson the claude-mem and externalisation items below reach from the agent side.

No independent replication of the production figures was found. A Chinese-language article headlined as Meta open-sourcing KernelEvolve appeared in search results but was not read, and no official release could be confirmed as of 9 November 2026.

Why it matters now. Today's lead runs custom selection kernels on H200 and B300; the LSA blog credits CuTe DSL kernels with making key selection 2.3x to 2.4x faster than an earlier Triton version (Prefrontal blog). Hand-tuning kernels for each new attention variant and each accelerator generation is the work KernelEvolve claims to automate. If Meta's numbers hold, new attention ideas could reach deployment faster on heterogeneous hardware. The open question from January remains open: whether any of this leaves Meta arxiv.

The release-day clash: Claude Opus 4.6 vs GPT-5.3-Codex (AINews, 6 February 2026)

1 source, 3 min read

What it said. AINews framed the simultaneous launch as open war between the two leading coding labs. Its scorecard: Anthropic won the publicity battle with a 1M context window, compaction, adaptive thinking, agent teams, a clean-room C compiler that boots Linux, and "500 zero-days". OpenAI "won on most benchmarks with 25% higher speed with higher token efficiency". The newsletter warned that "all first day third party reactions are either biased or superficial".

The issue carried several specific claims: - TerminalBench 2 at 65.4% for GPT-5.3-Codex. - 2.09x fewer tokens than GPT-5.2-Codex-xhigh on SWE-Bench-Pro. - An Opus 4.6 system-card line giving a staff-estimated productivity uplift of "30%–700% (mean 152%, median 100%)". - A SemiAnalysis claim that 4% of public GitHub commits were authored by Claude Code, with a projection of "20%+ by end of 2026". - A forecast that "Claude 5 and GPT 6 battles this summer" would follow these "minor version bumps".

source

How it aged. Start with the prediction. Wikipedia's Claude timeline, as of this issue, lists the following releases: - Sonnet 4.6 on 17 February. - Mythos Preview on 7 April, to 11 organisations through Project Glasswing. - Opus 4.7 on 16 April and Opus 4.8 on 28 May. - Mythos 5 and a public Fable 5 on 9 June. - Sonnet 5 on 30 June and Opus 5 on 24 July. - Fable 5.1 and Mythos 5.1 on 1 September. - Opus 5.5 on 22 September.

Anthropic did ship a 5-generation model that summer, so the "Claude 5" half came true, though under several brand names. On OpenAI's side, the Wikipedia stub for GPT-5.3-Codex lists GPT-5.4, GPT-5.5, GPT-5.6, GPT-6 and GPT-6.1 as successors but gives no dates (Wikipedia: GPT-5.3-Codex). A search snippet from the GPT-5.4 article puts that release on 5 March 2026, but the page itself was not read. Whether GPT-6 arrived "this summer" is not established here.

Several of the PR-day claims became the year's main storylines. The C compiler is now in the encyclopaedia: Nicholas Carlini reported that 16 Opus 4.6 agents built a C compiler in Rust capable of compiling the Linux kernel, at a cost of nearly $20,000, and said it is not very efficient (Wikipedia: Claude). The zero-day theme grew into Mythos and Glasswing. Wikipedia records Mozilla patching 271 Firefox vulnerabilities with Mythos Preview, and a 12 June directive from the Commerce Department that disabled all Mythos-class access until restrictions were lifted later in June. Whatever one thinks of the February framing, "AI finds vulnerabilities at scale" turned out to be the most consequential thread in that announcement, and it stopped being an Anthropic marketing line once a government regulator got involved.

Benchmarks aged worse. The 65.4% and 2.09x figures were never independently pinned down in sources read for this issue; the GPT-5.3-Codex Wikipedia page carries no benchmark scores at all. The February warning in the same issue has, if anything, been borne out: an Anthropic engineering post showed that infrastructure configuration can swing agentic coding scores by several points. Later model comparisons in sources read today are vendor-run. GitLab reports that Opus 5 "resolved 93.3% of benchmark tasks, a 20.3-point improvement over Opus 4.8's 73.0%", on an unnamed internal set with no stated sample size (GitLab blog, 27 July 2026). Wikipedia also records a backlash in which some users called Opus 4.7 worse than its predecessor.

The SemiAnalysis projection is unresolved. No later SemiAnalysis figure was found. Two third-party trackers that appeared in search results, one claiming roughly 9.7% of public commits by mid-March and one reporting that most Claude Code commits land in repos with almost no stars, could not be read: one returned a server error and the other a 403. Whether "20%+ by end of 2026" is on track cannot be stated from what was read.

Why it matters now. Two of February's undercurrents shape today's lead. The theme that 2026 is no longer assuming infinite-budget compute, token efficiency over raw score, is the same economic pressure behind LSA source. The open-weight models that were background in February are now priced against the frontier: GitLab's internal benchmark says "GLM 5.3 matched Claude Opus 5's task completion at approximately one-fifth the cost" (GitLab blog, 13 October 2026). That GLM family is the one LSA's authors modified.

Prefill-as-a-Service and linear attention across datacentres (AINews, 21 April 2026)

1 source, 3 min read

What it said. AINews relayed a Zhihu Frontier summary of a Moonshot AI and Tsinghua proposal. Standard attention produces KV caches too large to send across datacentre links, but hybrid linear-attention models such as Kimi Linear shrink that state enough to make remote prefill workable. The quoted proof of concept: a 1T-parameter "linear-attention model across mixed H200/H20 clusters over a 100 Gbps inter-DC link, reporting +54% throughput and -64% P90 TTFT, with outbound bandwidth around 13 Gbps". The newsletter added a conditional: "If those numbers hold more broadly, linear-attention families may matter as much for serving topology as for asymptotic context scaling." The same item also covered work on inter-layer communication, recurrent-depth transformers, and an image-model patch-expansion trick; those were not followed up here x.

Paper facts. The paper is "Prefill-as-a-Service: KVCache of Next-Generation Models Could Go Cross-Datacenter" (arXiv 2604.15039), submitted by Ruoyu Qin. It is an arXiv preprint, v1 16 April 2026 and v2 22 April; no venue is stated.

  • The model. "an internal 1T-parameter hybrid model following the Kimi Linear architecture", interleaving KDA and MLA layers 3:1. It is not a pure linear-attention model, and its weights are not public.
  • The setup. A prefill-only cluster of 32 H200s alongside a 64-H20 local prefill/decode cluster, joined by an aggregate link of about 100 Gbps. The baseline is 96 homogeneous H20s.
  • Throughput. 3.24 requests per second, against 2.11 for the homogeneous baseline (1.54x) and 2.45 for a naive heterogeneous setup.
  • Latency. P90 TTFT falls from 9.73s to 3.51s. The naive setup has a lower mean TTFT, 1.74s against 2.22s, and the same P90.
  • Bandwidth. Prefill-cluster egress averages about 13 Gbps.
  • Cost. "at equal cost, the throughput gain is approximately 15%".
  • Limitations.
  • The results are "derived by feeding the measured profiling data into the throughput model", not measured end to end.
  • Output length is fixed at 1,024 tokens.
  • Hybrid models alone do not make cross-datacentre transfer "free enough for indiscriminate transfer".
  • The prefill cluster was compute-bound, so bandwidth as the bottleneck was not tested.

(arXiv HTML)

How it aged. Reading the paper changes how the April summary should be read. The +54% and -64% are real figures from the paper, but they compare different hardware: a mix of H200s and H20s against H20s alone. The authors themselves put the gain at equal cost at about 15%. They also come from a throughput model fed with profiling data, not a production deployment. The "13 Gbps" is accurate. The underlying KV measurements support the architectural thesis well. At 32K tokens on 8 H200s, KV throughput for hybrid MiMo-V2-Flash is 4.66 Gbps against 59.93 Gbps for the dense MiniMax-M2.5, which the paper calls 13x lower, and Qwen3.5-397B is 4x below the dense Qwen3-235B. The paper also projects that a dense model like MiniMax would need 3.8 Tbps of egress at 512 H200s, against about 170 Gbps for Ring-2.5-1T (same source).

The architectural bet itself has aged well in one respect: the hybrid model families the paper measured have kept spreading. Today's lead experiments on GLM-5.3-Flash, which already uses sparse attention, and LSA's own native model is a Gated DeltaNet hybrid (arXiv HTML). Follow-up work treats hybrid serving as a first-class systems problem. "KVBuffer: IO-aware Serving for Linear Attention" (arXiv 2605.19049, v1 18 May, v2 5 November 2026) by Longwei Zou and Lin Zhong reports batch decoding "up to 1.47x" faster and "5x" more serving requests for speculative decoding with four draft tokens, implemented in SGLang for Qwen3.5 and Nemotron-3. A search summary says KVBuffer cites PrfaaS. The abstract read for this issue does not mention it, so that link is not confirmed. No evidence was found that Moonshot or anyone else runs cross-datacentre prefill in production as of 9 November 2026.

Why it matters now. LSA and PrfaaS address two different costs of long context. LSA states plainly that it reduces search, not storage. PrfaaS depends on reducing storage so the state can travel. A 10M-token dense-attention KV cache of the kind LSA serves on Qwen would be impractical to move between datacentres. A hybrid model with a few global layers using LSA-style selection would combine both savings, and nobody in the sources read for this issue has measured that combination x.

claude-mem: did a memory plugin survive native memory? (first saved December 2025; latest mention 9 October 2026)

1 source, 2 min read

What it said. claude-mem was described as a plugin that "automatically captures and AI-compresses Claude's coding session activities", injects relevant context into later sessions, exposes search through four MCP tools, and offers a beta "Endless Mode" github.

How it aged. It survived and grew, but changed shape, and some of the changes deserve scrutiny. As of this issue the repo shows 114.9k stars, 10.0k forks, 4,049 commits, 53 open issues and a README version badge of 15.12.1 (GitHub). The tagline is now "Your coding agent forgets everything between sessions. Claude-Mem remembers for it." The project no longer serves only Claude Code. It claims full capture and recall for Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, OpenCode and "11 more", plus search-only support for others. The architecture has moved from MCP tools to an "observer" agent that turns tool activity into compact notes and gives the next session a short index. Search summaries also describe it as running through lifecycle hooks and a local worker, not as an MCP server; Endless Mode no longer appears in the README.

Native memory arrived during this period. Claude Code's documentation now describes "two complementary memory systems": CLAUDE.md files that the user writes, and auto memory that Claude writes, scoped "Per repository, shared across worktrees" and loaded at the start of every session up to the "first 200 lines or 25KB" (Claude Code docs). The docs page did not state the release date, and secondary sources found in search disagree on it, so that date is left unstated here.

The plugin's answer is telling. For Claude Code, its installer asks "whether to disable native auto-memory" (GitHub). The two systems are competing to fill the same context window.

Three changes since January matter for anyone running this on a work machine: - Anonymous usage analytics are on by default; npx claude-mem telemetry disable turns them off. - There is now a hosted observer with a 30-day trial that asks for a payment method, and opt-in cloud sync. Screenshots are uploaded only when sync is enabled. - The README promotes a third-party crypto token, CMEM, that the author "officially embraced".

None of these is disqualifying alone, but together they turn a local SQLite memory tool into a product with a commercial funnel. The privacy model should be re-read, not assumed from the 2025 version (same source).

Why it matters now. The pattern behind the plugin has been confirmed three times over. A vendor shipped native memory. A popular third-party layer kept growing anyway. Meta's Trellis paper argues that agent experience should be database state, not throwaway logs (arXiv 2606.29823). The differences now lie in what gets remembered and who decides. Native memory saves preferences and corrections in plain Markdown the user can read. claude-mem saves model-compressed observations of everything the agent did. Running both duplicates context. The installer's question is the right one to answer deliberately github.

Claude Code Routines (first covered 15 April 2026)

1 source, 2 min read

What it said. Routines ran saved Claude Code configurations unattended on Anthropic's cloud. They were triggered by a schedule, an API call or a GitHub event, and suited to recurring work such as code review or alert triage claude.

How it aged. The feature survived but was renamed and is still officially experimental. The same documentation URL is now titled "Automate work with scheduled tasks", and describes "A cloud scheduled task, formerly a routine". A banner says "Scheduled tasks are in research preview. Behavior, limits, and the API surface may change", seven months after launch (Claude Code docs).

The trigger model is unchanged: schedule, an API endpoint with a bearer token, and GitHub events, which can be combined. There are two notable additions. Tasks can now run "on your organization's self-hosted environment when routed there" instead of only on Anthropic-managed infrastructure. Availability covers "Pro, Max, Ultra, Team, and Enterprise plans", the list including an Ultra tier. Management still happens at claude.ai/code/routines or with /schedule, and the docs note that "the earlier name, routines, still appears" (same source). The more detailed limits sections, such as runs per day and branch-push restrictions, were not read for this issue.

Evidence of real-world use is thin in what was found. Search results were dominated by tutorials and guides, with typical examples of PR review, nightly audits and dependency checks. Tutorial summaries in search results mention restrictions such as cron schedules more frequent than hourly being rejected and no access to local files, but those were not checked against the docs. No independent usage data, incident reports or adoption numbers were found as of 9 November 2026.

Why it matters now. Self-hosted routing is the substantive change. Unattended agents that hold repository write access and connector credentials are a security-architecture decision as much as a convenience feature. Running them inside the customer's own environment answers the obvious objection to April's cloud-only design. The rename suggests the product now sits within a broader scheduling surface rather than standing alone. Treat it as preview software for anything critical claude.

"Externalization in LLM Agents" review (Hugging Face Papers, 11 April 2026)

1 source, 2 min read

What it said. The bundle item is only the title and an upvote count, 33 on Hugging Face Papers. The paper argues that agent capability is increasingly built by reorganising the runtime around the model instead of changing its weights huggingface.

Paper facts. arXiv 2604.08224 has 21 authors, starting with Chenyu Zhou, Huacan Chai and Wenteng Chen; Weinan Zhang is listed last. Only v1 exists, submitted 9 April 2026. The comments field reads "54 pages, tech report on Externalization in LLM Agents", with no venue named, and the page links no repository. It is a review, so there are no datasets, baselines or headline numbers.

The abstract says agents are "increasingly built less by changing model weights than by reorganizing the runtime around them". It sets out the framework: "memory externalizes state across time", "skills externalize procedural expertise", "protocols externalize interaction structure", and "harness engineering serves as the unification layer". It traces a path "from weights to context to harness". It names open challenges in evaluation, governance and "the long-term co-evolution of models and external infrastructure", and points to "self-evolving harnesses and shared agent infrastructure" as directions. Stated limitations: not stated in the abstract.

How it aged. The framing has held up better than most reviews, mainly because products moved its way. - Claude Code's documentation now treats instructions the user writes and memory the model writes as separate externalised layers (Claude Code docs). - claude-mem's growth shows a large demand for externalised memory outside the vendor's own (GitHub). - Trellis turns "memory externalizes state across time" into a database design: frontier selection becomes a query, reuse becomes graph retrieval, and training-data extraction becomes a materialised view (arXiv 2606.29823). - Scheduled tasks move the harness itself off the developer's laptop (Claude Code docs).

There is academic uptake too. "Harness Engineering as Categorical Architecture" by Bogdan Banu (arXiv 2605.12239, 12 May 2026) maps "four pillars of agent externalization" onto category theory. The abstract does not name its source, and a search summary of the full text says it cites this review. It describes harness engineering as a fourth pillar, while the review's abstract calls harness engineering the "unification layer" that coordinates the other three. Followers are already blurring that distinction. The categorical paper's own experiment is small: two models and one task.

No citation count was available, and the paper remains an unrefereed technical report as of 9 November 2026.

Why it matters now. Today's lead can be read with this frame. LSA keeps everything in context and makes searching it cheaper. Externalised memory keeps less in context and retrieves more from outside. Both are bets on the same question: where state should live for an agent that runs for days. The review called this co-evolution of model and infrastructure an open problem, and it still is huggingface.

Ways of thinking

Honesty over flattery, applied to benchmark tables

2 sources, 2 min read

This week's Brain Food from Shane Parrish includes a short line: "Don't punish honesty because flattery feels better." It sits next to a related one: "Loyalty that protects your ego is not the same as loyalty that protects your interests." source

The idea, applied to technical reading, is that the way people respond to unwelcome information decides whether they keep getting it. Teams that reward a clean headline and penalise caveats will get clean headlines. Readers who share the flattering multiplier and skip the regression table teach authors to lead with the multiplier. The cost shows up later, when a decision rests on the number that felt best instead of the number that was true for the reader's own workload.

Today's lead offers both sides of this. The LSA paper volunteers its unflattering results: - Tree search, the asymptotically better variant, scores 55.8 against Flat's 66.8 on GLM at 10M. - Some B300 configurations run as slow as 0.63x at shorter contexts. - LongBench v2 confidence intervals of plus or minus 11.6 points make the short-context check weak. - The discussion states plainly that LSA does not reduce KV storage.

That is honest reporting that a hasty reader could easily punish by ignoring it. The newsletter summary is not dishonest, but it picks the context-parallel 2.2x over the tensor-parallel 5.83x in one place, and calls a 0.5B decay-phase speedup "end to end" in another. Each choice is defensible; together they lean towards the more flattering reading source.

The independent replication shows the healthy version. Christopher Owen published "unfavorable runs included", reported that end-to-end gains were "inside the noise", and asked for criticism. The first reply told him that his 9-versus-8 result was "one discordant answer". Encouraging honesty here means thanking the person who said the improvement was not real, then using their sequential test.

A practical rule follows. When evaluating a vendor result, read the worst row in the table before the best one, and treat the absence of a worst row as information. GitLab's "93.3%" and "approximately one-fifth the cost" come with no sample size and no task list (GitLab; GitLab). That does not make them false, but it gives a reader nothing to check them against source.

The Mimikatz Missing Manual

Why this is here: exploring LLM personalities, open models, and AI commentary.

The curator's stated topic and this item barely overlap. This is a Windows-security reference, not AI commentary, so it is best read as a sideways step into the security side of the reader's interests.

Carlos Perez (DarkOperator) turned private conference training material into a public manual, "refined with the direct help and insight of Benjamin Delpy, the author of Mimikatz". It aims to explain "not just the commands, but the why and the how" of Windows identity internals. The repo is MIT-licensed, with 467 stars, 64 forks and 18 commits, and builds as an MkDocs site. It addresses three audiences, and for blue teams it promises "Detection strategies, event log signatures, and mitigation guides". The README does not say how deep that defensive material goes (GitHub) github.

For someone working on security and infrastructure, the value is less the tool than the threat model it teaches. A defender who understands why a technique works can reason about variants that signature-based detection misses.

There is also an AI angle the curator's topic hints at. Agents with shell access on corporate endpoints, such as the scheduled cloud tasks discussed above or self-hosted equivalents, inherit whatever identity material lives on those machines. Knowing what an attacker can extract from a compromised host is a prerequisite for scoping agent permissions.

Whether it holds up: the authorship credentials are strong and the structure is sensible. How current the techniques are against present Windows defences was not checked for this issue, and neither was the depth of the detection guidance. Read it as an explanation of internals, with the defensive chapters checked against current vendor guidance before relying on them github.

GitLab Duo's model menu, from Opus 4.6 to open-weight models at a fraction of the price

1 source, 2 min read

Why this is here: exploring Cursor, coding agent tools, and GitHub repos.

The saved item is GitLab's 17 February 2026 note that Claude Opus 4.6 had joined the model selector in the GitLab Duo Agent Platform, for "All agents" and "Agentic Chat" on GitLab.com but not for older Duo Classic features. Usage was billed in credits at "1.2 requests per credit" for prompts of 200k tokens or fewer and "0.7 requests per credit" above that. The post made no benchmark claims (GitLab blog) gitlab.

Read as a snapshot, the interesting part is the pricing mechanism, not the model. Tying the credit rate to prompt length makes long context visibly expensive to the end customer. That is the cost LSA tries to reduce at the infrastructure layer.

Nine months on, GitLab's menu says a lot about where coding-agent platforms went: - July 2026. It added Opus 5 with a vendor-run claim of 93.3% resolution against Opus 4.8's 73.0% on undisclosed tasks (GitLab blog). - October 2026. GitLab 19.4 added GitLab-managed open-weight models. The vendor puts GLM 5.3 at about one-fifth the cost of Opus 5, Kimi K3 at about one-third the cost of Claude Fable 5.1, and MiniMax M3 at about one-eighth the cost of GPT-5.5. - Spend controls. The same October release made spend caps generally available, at subscription, group and per-user level, with alerts at 50%, 80% and 100% (GitLab blog).

Whether it holds up: the trend is clear even if the numbers are not. Platforms have turned the model into a per-feature setting, and the selling point has moved from "the newest frontier model" to cost control. The quality-at-lower-cost claims rest entirely on GitLab's internal benchmark, with no methodology published. The GitLab docs also warn that a pinned model that becomes unavailable has no automatic fallback, according to a search summary of the docs that was not read directly. For a reader comparing Cursor, Claude Code and platform-native agents, the takeaway is to treat model choice as configuration and to measure cost per resolved task on your own repositories gitlab.
