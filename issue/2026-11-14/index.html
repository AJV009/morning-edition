Google ships Gemini 4 Hydrogen and an in-session model router

1 source, 5 min read

What happened. On November 12, Google DeepMind's Koray Kavukcuoglu published the launch post for Gemini 4 Hydrogen, updated on November 13. It is the "fast execution" member of the Gemini 4 family, next to the frontier models Carbon and Argon. The post says "Hydrogen is built for rapid execution across diverse agentic workflows", and describes it working on its own or alongside Carbon and Argon (Google). Google also introduced Antigravity Orchestrator, which moves between models such as Carbon and Hydrogen within a single session blog.

Details. Pricing is $1.50 per million input tokens and $7.50 per million output tokens. Google says it is "priced flat across its entire 1 million-token context window", so long prompts carry no premium tier. Input context is 1M tokens natively. The multimodal section says output can run to 1M tokens. There are four thinking levels: low, medium, high (the default) and a new xhigh (Google). The Decoder adds a cache-hit price of $0.075 (The Decoder). Most of Google's comparisons are against its previous fast model, Gemini 3.8 Flash. Hydrogen finishes tasks "2.5 times faster at its high default setting". It scores 57% against 3.8 Flash's 18.2% on Terminal-Bench 4.1, makes more than 5x fewer fabricated claims, and in early partner tests on SQL, complex graphs and data-heavy enterprise workloads was 2–2.8x faster while using 12–23% fewer tokens. Google also lists benchmark results that are not comparisons with its own models: 58.8% on HLE-Diamond, 52.3% on Vals Tax Agent Bench (ranked first), 55.8% on Vals Legal Research Bench (tied first), 71.6% on Agents' Last Exam v1.1, 53.4% on a new Multimodal Knowledge Agent benchmark, and fifth place overall on arena.ai's live Agent Arena. For the Orchestrator, the claim is that on Terminal-Bench 4.0 it "reached near-Carbon task success rates while, on average, consuming 24% less quota and completing 19% faster." In parallel-agent mode, Carbon coordinates while Hydrogen runs the worker and verifier agents in isolated workspaces. Google's safety numbers are also against 3.8 Flash: overreach in agentic tasks down by up to 81%, under-service down by up to 79%, 12x fewer misaligned actions and task shortcuts on tool-use and coding evaluations, up to 2x less likely to bypass permission boundaries when a task is blocked, 94% on an internal agent-honesty evaluation, and 99.9% chain-of-thought legibility with zero deceptive reasoning edits across more than 200,000 audited turns. Google also claims more than 2.3x more resilience on the Gray Swan indirect-prompt-injection benchmark and under 0.4% false refusals on HealthBench Pro. Rollout started on publication day and covers AI Studio on all tiers, Android Studio, Antigravity for paid AI Pro and Ultra subscribers, Gemini for business, Gemini in Sheets and Google Search. Free Gemini app users get Hydrogen only when auto model selection picks it (Google) blog.

Facts vs. opinions. Three kinds of number are mixed together here, and they deserve different weight. The first kind is third-party leaderboard figures that Google quotes, mainly the Artificial Analysis (AA) Intelligence Index. Google says Hydrogen scores 51 at default high thinking against 50 for Argon, and 54 at xhigh (Google). The Decoder reports the same 51 and 54. It puts Claude Sonnet 5.5 and GPT-6.1 Sol at 55 each at their maximum settings and Carbon at 59. It adds that at high, Hydrogen's 51 trails GPT-6.1 Sol at xhigh and Claude Opus 5.5 at high, both on 52 (The Decoder). These agree with each other. The second kind is Google's own benchmark choices, and here the sources part ways. Google leads with Terminal-Bench 4.1 (57% against 18.2%). On AA's run of Terminal-Bench 4.0, according to The Decoder, Hydrogen scores 54% against 64% for Sonnet 5.5 at max, 56% for GPT-6.1 Sol at max, 65.2% for Carbon and 57.1% for Argon. On TB-Science 0.1, AA measures Hydrogen at 50% where Google reports 51.4% (The Decoder). The Decoder's summary is plain: "Hydrogen is weaker at agentic terminal work". The third kind is Google's internal evaluations: the honesty score, chain-of-thought legibility, overreach and under-service reductions, and the Orchestrator result. None of these can be checked from outside. "Near-Carbon" is not given as a number. Quota savings measured in Google's own quota units are a product metric, not a cost-per-task figure. The 2.5x speed claim compares Hydrogen at its default with 3.8 Flash. Third-party write-ups appear to measure task time in different ways, and those were not checked here.

Other perspectives. The Decoder reads the release as Google undercutting itself. It says "Hydrogen apparently ends up both more capable and cheaper than Argon" and that Hydrogen should leave "Argon obsolete less than four weeks after its release." It also warns that "Intelligence scores fall sharply at the lower levels". AA puts Hydrogen at 41 on medium and 27 on low. Cost per task on AA's index is $3.16 at xhigh, $1.87 at high, $0.80 at medium and $0.29 at low. The Decoder describes the xhigh figure as about one-third of Sonnet 5.5 at max but about 2.5x GPT-6.1 Sol at max. It also notes that xhigh uses almost double the output tokens of high (The Decoder). Its strongest independent number is hallucination: Hydrogen fabricates answers 8% of the time on AA-Omniscience. In the r/GeminiAI launch thread, which had more than 1,200 upvotes, the most-upvoted pushback is that Hydrogen replaces the Flash line "at double the price". One commenter claims that on TerminalBench it "scores lower than 6.1 Sol while also costing double". Another answers that competing models have "4-6x the hallucination rate of Hydrogen on Omniscience". A third says Google's model card shows Hydrogen built from Carbon rather than Argon; that was not checked here. These are user opinions, not measurements. The curator's note mentions an independent index placement reported by AgentBrief; that report was not read here.

Why it matters. For an engineer choosing a default worker model, the relevant facts are flat pricing across 1M tokens, an 8% fabrication rate on one independent benchmark, and a measurable gap on terminal-style agentic coding against GPT-6.1 Sol and Sonnet 5.5 (The Decoder). The structural news is the Orchestrator. Google is now productising, inside its IDE, the planner-worker-verifier split that Anthropic shipped as dynamic workflows in October (see the archive below). Carbon plans and Hydrogen does the parallel work in isolated workspaces (Google). Routing between models within one session means the model choice becomes a runtime decision made by the vendor's harness, not a fixed decision made by the developer. That helps with quota. It also makes per-task cost and behaviour harder to attribute and reproduce. The Orchestrator figures are the ones to re-measure on your own tasks before trusting blog.

Anthropic opens self-hosted Claude Managed Agents in private beta

6 sources, 4 min read

What happened. On November 12, Anthropic announced that "Self-hosted Claude Managed Agents is now available in private beta." Access goes through a customer's Anthropic account team. The article says "every part of the service runs in your own cloud or on-premise": the harness (the agent loop), sandboxes, credentials and session history (Claude). The platform release notes for the same date put it more precisely: "You run the whole Managed Agents service, not only self-hosted sandboxes, on your own infrastructure, and you store agents, sessions, and session history there." (Claude Platform release notes) claude.

Details. According to the announcement, "You operate the Managed Agents server, its database, and the workers where agents run their shell and file tools." Credentials can sit in Anthropic vaults or, in addition, "you can also store credentials in your own secrets manager". "Session history is stored in your own database", and if the customer hosts the sandbox, allowed-host network restrictions are enforced in the customer's own firewall. Model calls are the only thing that leaves, and "Model requests go where you choose": Anthropic directly, Amazon Bedrock, Google's Agent Platform, Microsoft Foundry, or the customer's own gateway. Developers use "the same core APIs as the hosted Managed Agents service." On retention, a zero-data-retention agreement "applies to the requests sent to Claude in the same way as any other Messages API call." The article lists four security principles. The harness (the "brain") is kept separate from the sandbox (the "hands"). A proxy outside the sandbox injects credentials so they never enter it. Network access can be limited to an allowlist. Every session leaves a full record that can be exported over OpenTelemetry. The page gives no pricing, quotas, limits or performance numbers (Claude). The release notes show how Anthropic got here step by step. Managed Agents runs under a managed-agents-2026-04-01 beta header. Memory entered beta on April 23, and multiagent orchestration and Outcomes on May 6. "Self-hosted sandboxes" arrived on May 19 as an alternative to tool execution on Anthropic's infrastructure, reached Claude Platform on AWS on May 29, and gained attachable memory stores on August 19. Dynamic workflows arrived on October 9. Only now does the control plane itself move out (Claude Platform release notes).

Facts vs. opinions. What is established is the architecture as Anthropic describes it, and the beta status. What is not stated on Anthropic's page: price, licence terms, system requirements, feature parity with the hosted service, and a general-availability date. "Self-hosted" here does not mean offline or air-gapped. The prompts, conversation and tool results still go to whichever inference provider is configured, unless the customer's "own gateway" points at an on-premises deployment of Claude, and nothing on the page says that option exists. One release-note detail is worth reading closely. On October 7, Anthropic made limited networking apply its allowed_hosts to the web_search and web_fetch tools, then added: "unrestricted networking and self-hosted environments do not limit these tools." (Claude Platform release notes). That entry predates this launch and concerns self-hosted sandbox environments. It still suggests that the server-side web tools sit outside the customer's firewall rules, so "network limits in your own firewall" may not cover everything the agent can reach. Whether this still holds for the fully self-hosted service is not stated claude.

Other perspectives. Reaction on X has mostly been restatement, with a few useful corrections. One user, Vladimir Golub (@vladigolub), wrote that the self-hosted service "still sends the conversation and tool results to an inference provider. a file can stay on your server while the text the agent reads leaves it. private beta, not offline Claude." An account called tsn_hub wrote that "On-site storage is not the same as on-site inference", and claimed that customers run a cma-server with PostgreSQL 16 or later, install each release themselves, and that "Some features stay hosted". Those specifics were not checked against Anthropic documentation here. The curator notes that AlphaSignal and AgentBrief also covered the launch. The press coverage that summarised the licence terms could not be read here (403), so those terms are left out. The competitive picture this week is crowded. Solo.io made its Agent Substrate and kagent generally available, with suspend-and-resume for idle sessions cloudnativenow. Databricks put Unity Gateway in charge of tool access, approvals and spending for its new Genie databricks. AWS claims AgentCore sessions start 15x faster x. DigitalOcean's Firecracker MicroVMs already power its own Managed Agents (DigitalOcean). Anthropic's answer differs because it ships the whole agent runtime, not just the sandbox or the gateway.

Why it matters. For security and infrastructure people, the credential proxy outside the sandbox is the design point to copy. It is the specific defence against what the wfx-loot campaign did this week: harvesting OpenAI, Anthropic and cloud tokens out of CI runner environments (Aikido) aikido. Self-hosting also moves the audit trail (sessions, tool calls, OpenTelemetry export) into systems the customer already monitors. The open questions are commercial and operational. Anthropic's private beta now competes with open harnesses (Qwen Code ships with local-model support and agent teams github) and with cloud-native runtimes. Its distinctive feature is that agents built against the hosted API can move inside the perimeter without being rewritten. Whether that is worth being tied to Claude for inference depends on licence terms not yet public claude.

United Airlines moves to restrict AI agents

2 sources, 4 min read

What happened. On Wednesday, November 11, United Airlines announced it would limit AI bots' access to its digital platforms, citing data security. The limits apply directly, on United's own website and app, and indirectly, through travel agencies and aggregators, through changes to its booking, ticketing and distribution policies. CNBC calls it "the latest pushback against AI agents increasingly capable of planning and booking trips for travelers" (CNBC). A newsletter item also carried it cnbc.

Details. CNBC gives three reasons from United. Handing login credentials to third-party assistants could expose sensitive information. That could lead to "unintended purchases, account modifications or the unauthorized use of frequent-flyer miles." And automated check-in carries regulatory risk because check-in "requires human acknowledgment for prohibited items such as explosives." CNBC quotes no named executive (CNBC). Engadget adds two more motives: reputational risk from agent incidents, citing Meta's Muse leaking a user's address, and keeping AI from sitting between the airline and its customers. It quotes CEO Scott Kirby, via The Wall Street Journal, from an event on Tuesday: "To be honest, I don't want to create another middleman." (Engadget). PYMNTS reports the scope more narrowly. United will stop unauthorized outside assistants from booking, but agents can still look up flights and display fares, and United says agent-made bookings are currently minimal (PYMNTS). Engadget's piece talks only about stopping agents booking and does not say whether search stays open. Search snippets mention changes to United's contract of carriage and a right to cancel agent-made reservations. Those were not confirmed in any article read here and are left as not checked.

Facts vs. opinions. The facts: United announced restrictions on November 11, the stated reasons are credentials, unintended transactions, miles, and check-in rules, and the Kirby quote. The rest is framing. CNBC presents it as part of a wider pushback. Engadget's author concludes that agentic commerce "might not be taking off with regular people" because letting an agent shop "isn't as frictionless or foolproof as it needs to be". The usage numbers back the claim that volume is small. Engadget reports that during Halloween week, out of 320 million Uber trips and deliveries, AI agents accounted for 500 Uber Eats orders and four rides (Engadget). PYMNTS reports Booking Holdings' traffic from large language models was under 1% of room nights in Q2, and that Expedia CEO Ariane Gorin said agent-driven volume is too small to draw conclusions from (PYMNTS). The regulatory argument about check-in is United's own interpretation. No article quotes the DOT or FAA confirming it.

Other perspectives. The industry is visibly split. CNBC lists Amazon blocking Meta's Muse agent, Walmart partnering with Muse, and Expedia and Booking working with OpenAI (CNBC). Engadget adds JPMorgan Chase and Amazon on the blocking side, and Southwest, which built a plugin for booking through ChatGPT, on the other (Engadget). PYMNTS reports the more interesting middle position. Delta permits bookings by assistants "that can authenticate the traveler's credentials". JPMorgan a month earlier barred outside agents from using customers' credentials to log in, move money or pay bills, with Jamie Dimon saying "You don't want rogue agents, and there could be thousands of them." Airbnb is building its own agent for the first half of 2027, and Brian Chesky expects "all bookings going through the app for every vertical in travel". OpenAI's purchase of Odessia, an agent that handled itinerary, pricing and reservations in one conversation, became public on November 10, though OpenAI has not announced a standalone travel product (PYMNTS). PYMNTS also cites its own survey of 2,299 U.S. adults, run from November 10 to December 10, 2025: 70% were interested in agentic AI for travel planning, and 58% of dedicated AI platform users preferred to check out inside the AI platform. That is a vendor survey from a year ago, not a measure of current behaviour.

Why it matters. The dispute is not about whether agents can book a flight. It is about who holds the credential and who carries the liability when something goes wrong. United's list of harms (miles spent, itineraries changed, purchases nobody meant to make) is the threat model for a delegated session with full user rights. Delta's reported model, where an agent that authenticates the traveler's credentials is allowed, points to a fix: scoped, attributable agent identity instead of screen-scraping with a borrowed password. The AgentBrief footer in today's bundle is a commercial version of the same idea. Its ".agent" community describes its mission as "verified agents", where "someone real" stands behind an agent and answers for it source. Until something like that exists at the protocol level, expect large merchants to keep making the business calculation Kirby described: the agent is a middleman, and refusing it costs little while volume is in the hundreds of orders. For anyone building consumer agents, the practical point is that "the agent logs in as you" is losing support among large merchants, and architectures need a path that uses merchant-issued, scoped authority cnbc.

Microsoft's FORGE Lab: 59 kernel bug reports, 33 CVEs, and a repair bottleneck

3 sources, 4 min read

What happened. Microsoft's FORGE Lab (credited as FORGE Lab and Cen Zhang) published a retrospective on November 11 of an AI-driven Linux kernel bug hunt, which the post calls MDASH. It covers a subset of kernel subsystems scanned from late May to mid-June 2026 by one full-time researcher and an intern, as part of a three-month effort on two servers (64 cores with 256 GB RAM, and 32 cores with 128 GB RAM). The thesis is in the post's own words: "Finding bugs and fixing them are drastically out of balance." (Microsoft Security) microsoft.

Details. The funnel went like this. 26,502 raw findings came out in about two weeks. Confidence filtering and fast screening agents cut those to 11,769 candidates. 621 cases had proof-of-concept evidence reproduced in QEMU, and 59 became public upstream reports, which the authors put at 9.5% of the PoC cases, "roughly one… from every ten". Of the 621, 266 (42.8%) shared a root cause with another case, 163 (26.2%) were dropped after review, mostly for limited security impact, and 133 (21.4%) had already been reported or fixed upstream. Those three groups plus the 59 reports add up to 621. Outcomes so far: 33 CVEs, 24 of them rated High or above (CVSS v3.1 of 7.0 or more), and eight CVEs with confirmed working root privilege-escalation exploits under lab conditions. The post notes that some of those needed non-default settings or disabled mitigations. Seven of the eight are use-after-free or refcount bugs in networking and lib. The eighth, CVE-2026-98222, is a heap overflow from a 16-bit length overflow in encrypted keys. Bug age averaged 8.1 years. Six bugs were 15–19 years old and three were at least 20. The oldest example, a cls_u32 shift-out-of-bounds (CVE-2026-74544), had been present for at least 21 years. The models, by share of tokens, were Sonnet 4.6 (71.1%), GPT-5.4 (20.1%) and GPT-5.5 (7.1%) for scanning, and GPT-5.5 (77.3%), Opus 4.6 (13.2%) and GPT-5.5-cyber (9.4%) for reproduction. The post gives no dollar or token-cost figures (Microsoft Security).

Facts vs. opinions. Two details in the newsletter summary need correcting against the primary source. TLDR says the scan used "mostly Sonnet 4.6 and GPT-5.5". By the post's own token shares, scanning was mostly Sonnet 4.6 and GPT-5.4, and GPT-5.5 dominated reproduction microsoft. TLDR also implies the rhashtable bug is one of Microsoft's root exploits on stock systems. The post says the bug, CVE-2026-64563, was found independently by Yuan Tan of NebuSec, and that NebuSec's Frank Wu built the working exploit. The post describes it as "a one-line state-reset bug in rhashtable, the Linux kernel's shared resizable hash table". A 2018 commit moved the reset of iter->p, but the resize path returns -EAGAIN without clearing the pointer. "If that entry was freed while the walk was paused, dereferencing iter->p->next reads from freed memory." Triggering it needs three things to coincide: a paused-and-resumed walk, a resize that frees the old table, and the entry being freed. "Most rhashtable walkers finish in a single pass without ever resuming a stopped iterator". Of about two dozen files using the walker API, only __netlink_diag_dump() is exploitable, and unprivileged users can reach it. The result is "a constrained 64-bit write" and local root on Linux, plus Android with SELinux enforcing. "The upstream fix simply clears the stale pointer before returning" (Microsoft Security). On the repair side, the numbers are: over four person-months of human effort to report the 59; 38 fixes merged to mainline five months on; about half accepted on first submission and the rest after 1–4 revisions; and a median of 41 days (maximum 108) from first public patch to mainline release. The first public report came on June 27, more than five weeks after scanning began. By September 1, 55 of the 59 were public, and the last four, first reported privately, went public by mid-October.

Other perspectives. The authors' framing is careful, not triumphant: "More tokens may yield more bugs, but not a secure system." They add that "Both bug hunters and maintainers can be overwhelmed by the volume of real findings", and they call for security work that is "operational, cumulative, and economically sensible" (Microsoft Security). An X search for reactions from kernel maintainers turned up almost nothing as of November 13, so the maintainers' side is not represented here. The attacker side of the same capability shows up in this week's other security items. Unit 42 counted 609 internet-facing hosts running the ARTEX AI pentest framework and 1,323 running CyberStrikeAI. It reports agents turning public password-reset pages and exposed OpenAPI specs into cloud access, sometimes in under a day, without exploiting a single vulnerability paloaltonetworks. And a commentary on what it calls "the Hugging Face incident" argues the lesson is about AI goal pursuit, not hacking capability karthiktadepalli.

Why it matters. The useful number here is not 26,502. It is the ratio between machine discovery measured in weeks and human repair measured in person-months and 41-day medians. The funnel also shows where AI triage spends its effort. Nearly 43% of reproducible cases duplicated another root cause, and over a fifth were already known upstream. Deduplication against upstream state is therefore a first-class engineering problem, not something to tidy up afterwards. For maintainers, the post is a warning that well-resourced labs can now generate findings faster than volunteers can review patches. That makes report quality, root-cause grouping and patch-ready submissions the scarce contribution. For a security-minded engineer, the takeaway is to budget the fixing pipeline before running the finding pipeline. The authors' own conclusion is that human review remains "necessary, making human effort the bottleneck." microsoft

Anthropic's dynamic workflows (Latent Space, October 14, 2026)

1 source, 2 min read

What it said. This is an old item revisited, a month on. The Latent Space write-up by Richard MacManus covered an AI Engineer New York workshop by Anthropic's CJ Avilla and Harrison Stall on "flows", which Anthropic described as "a new type of multiagent orchestration, built for your most ambitious workloads." A lead agent drafts a plan, then Claude writes an orchestration program that spawns agents, gathers their output and routes information between them, so developers no longer hand-build a coordinator and a roster of subagents. The demo was a stock watchlist scanner: one isolated agent per ticker, then a review phase and a report. Avilla said flows cost "much more" than a single agent but are "much more effective at finding all of the details". Developers can set a session budget, but the running model does not know it. Stall said telling models their budget might make them "a little bit anxious." Asked how many agents Claude launches, he answered "The answer really is evals and lower defaults." (Latent Space) latent.

How it aged. Four weeks is short, but three things have happened since. First, the platform record confirms the launch and its form. The October 9 release note says a Managed Agents agent can "write a workflow", defined as "a program that runs many agents in phases and combines their results", run server-side as a workflow run with workflow_run.* events. It is enabled with {"type": "multiagent_20261001", "workflows": {"type": "enabled"}}. A Claude Code "Workflows" research preview had appeared earlier, on May 28 (Claude Platform release notes). Second, Google has now shipped the same pattern as a product feature. Antigravity Orchestrator's parallel mode has Carbon as coordinator and Hydrogen running worker and verifier agents in isolated workspaces. General Agentic Memory via Deep Research (Hugging Face Papers, November 26, 2025)

3 sources, 3 min read

What it said. An archive item, about a year old. The Hugging Face daily-papers listing gave only the title and 120 upvotes. The paper proposes "general agentic memory" (GAM) on a "just-in time (JIT) compilation" principle. Keep only a lightweight memory offline, store the full history in a "universal page-store", and at request time have a Researcher agent assemble the context, guided by a Memorizer's summaries. The argument is that static, pre-built memory "is inevitably subject to severe information loss." (arXiv) huggingface.

Paper facts. arXiv 2511.18423, a single version (v1) submitted November 23, 2025, by B.Y. Yan, Chaofan Li, Hongjin Qian, Shuqi Lu and Zheng Liu. No venue is stated, so it remains a preprint. The backbones were GPT-4o-mini and Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct, with BGE-M3 retrieval, reflection depth 3, top-5 pages and 2,048-token pages. Benchmarks: LoCoMo, HotpotQA at 56K/224K/448K tokens, RULER at 128K, and 300 NarrativeQA questions. Baselines: long-context LLM, RAG, A-Mem, Mem0, MemoryOS and LightMem. Selected results with GPT-4o-mini: LoCoMo temporal F1 of 59.45 against Mem0's 48.93; RULER multi-hop tracing of 93.20 against long-LLM's 60.60 (RAG scored 0.00 there); NarrativeQA F1 of 36.86 against long-LLM's 31.26; HotpotQA at 448K of 59.81 against 54.01 for the best baseline. The costs are significant. Online serving takes 12.43–18.49 s per request on HotpotQA, against under 0.6 s for the memory baselines. Total time at 56K was 69.32 s, against 37.57 s for Mem0 and 5.13 s for LightMem. Performance "deteriorates significantly" with a Researcher of 7B or smaller; Qwen2.5-0.5B averages 9.08 F1. Code is at VectorSpaceLab/general-agentic-memory (arXiv HTML).

How it aged. Modest adoption, a pivot in the codebase, and a trained successor. As of November 14 the repository shows 869 stars, 89 forks, 124 commits, an MIT licence and no releases. The README now describes GAM as "a highly modular agentic file system framework" that handles text and long video, with a Python SDK, CLI tools, a FastAPI REST API and a Flask web platform. The original paper code sits in a research/ folder (GitHub). That is a shift from a memory paper to an "agent file system" product, in line with where agent tooling went generally. The direct follow-up is "Just-In-Time Agent Memory with Runtime Agentic Research" (JAM), arXiv 2609.34385v1, dated September 28, 2026. It is by an overlapping BAAI team (Bingyu Yan, Chaofan Li, Hongjin Qian, Shuqi Lu, Chaozhuo Li, Zheng Liu) and points its code to the same repository. JAM fixes the original's two weak points. It trains the Researcher (verified-trajectory SFT, then hint-guided GRPO on a synthesized "Memory-Gym" of 16,794 retained instances), and it runs on a small Qwen3.5-4B backbone. Online latency is 13.81 s per query against MemAgent's 58.08 s. Training took about 5 hours of SFT and 6 of GRPO on 8×H100. Two cautions. JAM is also still a preprint with no venue (arXiv HTML).

Why it matters now. The JIT idea held up; the latency did not get much better. Thirteen to eighteen seconds per memory lookup is fine for a background agent and hard to sell for a chat. Ways of thinking

Treat the agent's output as a hypothesis: cognitive science for supervising AI-written code

3 sources, 2 min read

Marina Altés, a Criteo engineer who spent nearly a decade in clinical neuropsychology, sets out four principles for developers working with AI (Criteo Tech) criteo. Working memory is "always very limited" and "gets wiped as soon as you stop using it", so chunk work and handle one piece at a time. She adds that "LLMs aren't the only ones that start hallucinating when their context gets bloated." Recognition is not recall: "recognizing something is much easier than recalling it", so after using an assistant, close it, explain the idea yourself, rebuild a minimal version, and revisit it days later. The default mode network does recombination when you step away, though it "isn't a magical 'aha machine'", so write the problem down, switch context, and come back instead of prompting through every block. The fourth is scientific debugging: hypothesise, predict, then try to disprove, which is the step people skip. Her rule: "Treat the assistant's output as a hypothesis, not as evidence." She cites a systematic review on automation bias showing over-reliance increases under workload and time pressure. She also cites Anthropic data that the longest agent turns grew from under 25 minutes to over 45, and that experienced users auto-approve more often but also interrupt more.

Today's news tests this framing three ways. DocSpring's founder runs 49 parallel Claude Code sessions and spends his days reading their updates and answering their questions. He reports ending each day exhausted at first docspring. That is the working-memory limit in practice: 49 contexts, each needing a reload on every switch, and the only way through is to make each session's state readable at a glance. Next, AgentBrief's summary of Microsoft's ThinkingBox finding is that about two in three failed agent runs looked complete in the transcript, while 98.95% of failures left the final database wrong source. That is recognition standing in for verification. A transcript that reads well is the "recognition" signal Altés warns about, and the database state is the evidence. Finally, Microsoft's kernel funnel is scientific debugging at scale. Of 621 reproducible cases, the 266 duplicates and 163 low-impact cases were hypotheses that had to be disproved by people, at a cost of more than four person-months (Microsoft Security).

The practical version is to design the review step around disproof. Ask what observable state would be different if the agent were wrong, check that state directly, and set up the work so it can be checked one piece at a time. The cognitive-science claims in the post come from well-known literature (Carpenter, Pan and Butler on retrieval practice; Sweller on cognitive load). The mapping to AI supervision is the author's argument, not a tested result.
