The FAA ground stop: from "aging system" to one router flooding the rest

1 source, 6 min read

What happened. On Friday, November 13, the FAA halted departures across the United States. Morning Brew's account of the White House press conference, held just after the stop was lifted, says Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin blamed new technology upgrades "overwhelming" the aging air traffic control communication system. He said it did not appear to be a cyberattack or anything "nefarious." Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said of the upgrades, "I didn't anticipate that we would have an outage like this," and asked Congress for $50 billion yahoo. By Saturday the story was more specific. According to Yahoo News' live blog, Duffy said the outage began while contractor L3Harris was switching telecom lines "from old copper wire, and they're bringing it over to fiber," and that a router "started flooding other routers in our air traffic control system" (Yahoo News live blog).

Details. The Wikipedia article on the event, which cites the FAA, CNN and an ATC System Command Center advisory, says the stop began at Chicago O'Hare and Midway. It went nationwide at 3:54 p.m. EST (20:54 UTC), with the FAA citing "extraordinary circumstances and for reasons of safety." It was lifted at 8:15 p.m. EST, 4 hours 21 minutes later, according to CNBC as cited there (Wikipedia). At the FAA's request, Eurocontrol suspended US-bound departures from its 42 member states from 22:00 UTC on November 13 to 05:00 UTC on November 14. Flights within Alaska and within Hawaii were allowed to operate. SpaceX's Starship Flight 15 still launched, because its airspace restriction had been issued before the outage (Wikipedia). Yahoo's live blog gives the most technical detail. The faulty router generated more than 1,000 connection attempts every 4 to 5 seconds. Tracing the problem took about 45 minutes. The work had been under way around 3 p.m. Friday at "at least one" remote site. The FAA then disabled the problem function on roughly 60 routers of the same type. The network, the FAA Telecommunications Infrastructure, links more than 4,000 FAA facilities, and L3Harris's predecessor won the contract in 2002. L3Harris says more than 70% of the fiber conversion is complete. The same blog notes that a misconfigured router disrupted 129 facilities in 2009, and that a 2023 FAA-commissioned review called the network obsolete (Yahoo News live blog). FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford said "there's no backup to the backup," that "No facilities went dark. Nothing was fully lost," and "Let's not kid ourselves; it will continue to break until we replace it." Duffy said officials were checking "whether we had incompetence," and added, "Until I can build" a new network, "I'm stuck with L3Harris" (same source).

The impact numbers depend on who counted, and when. Morning Brew, as of 9:30 p.m. ET Friday, had more than 6,600 Friday cancellations and another 1,500 for Saturday, and cited Cirium's estimate of 1.5 million stranded travelers yahoo. Wikipedia, citing CBS News and BBC, says nearly 8,000 flights were cancelled and over 10,000 delayed, cargo included (Wikipedia). Yahoo's blog says about 12,000 Friday flights could not be completed, roughly 9,000 commercial and 3,000 private or general aviation. It also reports that Saturday cancellations passed 5,000 as of 2:48 p.m. ET according to FlightAware, against Bedford's forecast of 4,000 to 5,000 out of about 41,000 scheduled flights (Yahoo News live blog). AirLive, updated Saturday 17:40 UTC, reports about 50,000 flights scheduled on Friday, about 30,000 of which departed before the stop, and more than 2,000 cancelled on Saturday (AirLive). Each count captures the Saturday backlog at a different hour, so the numbers grow from source to source. None of them is final.

Facts vs. opinions. Several things are established. The stop happened. It lasted more than four hours. Officials across DHS, DOT and the FAA have consistently said there is no evidence of a cyberattack. A router on the L3Harris-run network was identified as the trigger, and the FAA disabled a function on about 60 similar routers. What remains a claim is whether a "faulty installation," a "faulty configuration" or defective hardware caused it. Duffy said, "We haven't determined what the fault was," and the FAA has promised a root cause analysis (Yahoo News live blog). L3Harris's statement says only that it is "supporting the FAA in its investigation to understand the conditions that led to the Nov. 13 ground stop." It does not answer Duffy's suggestion of contractor error (same source). The official story also shifted. Mullin's Friday framing was new upgrades overwhelming old systems yahoo. Duffy's Friday framing, per ABC as cited by Wikipedia, was overloaded switches and routers producing inconsistent data. Saturday's framing was one contractor router in the middle of a fiber cutover. These are compatible in hindsight, but they did not arrive together. On the AI question: three days earlier, on November 10, the FAA had expanded its AI-based SMART traffic management system to all centers. The FAA and the system's maker, Air Space Intelligence, both deny any link (Wikipedia). Bedford said there was "zero evidence of anything AI related," and Yahoo puts SMART's cost at $875 million (Yahoo News live blog). Wikipedia notes Mullin initially did not rule SMART out. The claim that this was only the third national ground stop in US history, after 9/11 and the 2023 NOTAM outage, comes from that fast-moving Wikipedia article and its cited outlets. It has not been checked independently here.

Other perspectives. The controllers' union is the most useful counterweight to the press-conference version. NATCA president Nick Daniels said, "We know we didn't have communications with airplanes," and that controllers were "switching airplanes to different frequencies." His criticism was about scheduling, not blame: "If the worst outcome is we lose all of our frequencies," then "that work should be done when there's the fewest airplanes in the sky." He added, "I don't think blame changes anything. Action changes things" (Yahoo News live blog). Duffy partly conceded the point: "There'll be a reevaluation of when we do this work." The money question is also contested. Duffy wants $50 billion yahoo. Wikipedia says that would come on top of $12.5 billion already approved, and that Duffy had asked for $30 billion on November 12, the day before the outage, per Roll Call (Wikipedia). Bedford, by contrast, said finishing modernization needs about $10.1 billion more (Yahoo News live blog). That gap, roughly $10 billion from the administrator against $50 billion from the secretary, is not explained in any source read. Wikipedia records Rep. Ted Lieu calling for hearings and Sen. Tammy Duckworth demanding an investigation. As of its last edit, no NTSB or Inspector General involvement was listed.

Why it matters. For an infrastructure engineer, this is a textbook case of the migration window being the riskiest point in a system's life. A legacy network with no "backup to the backup" was having its physical layer cut over during peak traffic. One device in a bad state produced a connection storm, and nothing at the network level stopped it spreading to its peers. A 45-minute detection-to-isolation time on a safety-critical network suggests a lack of per-device rate limiting or blast-radius containment, though that is an inference and the FAA's root cause analysis has not been published. The episode also shows how "AI" is now reflexively ruled in or out of any outage. Every official felt they had to address SMART, even though the evidence points to a router. And the same week produced a near-identical failure shape at Microsoft, covered next yahoo.

Microsoft 365's cascading health-check failure, and what "$7 billion" means

1 source, 4 min read

What happened. On Thursday, November 12, Exchange Online began failing for users in the Americas. The problems spread to Teams, SharePoint Online, Microsoft 365 Copilot, Defender XDR and other services that depend on Exchange, and recovery stretched into Friday (BleepingComputer; SecurityWeek). Morning Brew, citing Downdetector, says users in more than 70 countries and territories were hit. It reports Microsoft's X post saying "We are deeply sorry," and a Michigan think tank's estimate of at least $7 billion in lost productivity cnn. According to CNN's report as syndicated by KCCI, Microsoft's preliminary cause is that "the incident was introduced by an issue during an earlier network capacity upgrade that surfaced following a recent network change" (KCCI/CNN).

Details. Three incident IDs matter here. TM1550488 was a separate Teams incident from 9:45 to 10:53 a.m. ET on November 12, which Microsoft attributed to resource contention. EX1550543 covers Exchange Online. MO1550821 is the broader Microsoft 365 incident, with a stated start of 1:26 p.m. ET (BleepingComputer; SecurityWeek). Microsoft's explanation changed in the same way the FAA's did. It first blamed elevated infrastructure load across regions. It then clarified that "the elevated load isn't driven by an increase in traffic, but by a number of servers being unexpectedly unavailable" (BleepingComputer). SecurityWeek reports that servers "were failing automated health checks," that "a process that establishes secure connections was becoming overwhelmed," and that Microsoft began "rolling back a recent network configuration change that coincided with the start of the disruption" (SecurityWeek). Mitigation, per BleepingComputer, included:

  • pausing all deployments
  • redistributing connection traffic globally
  • restarting servers
  • considering reverting a small number of recent configuration updates
  • extending the email timeout window to 48 hours so delayed mail would not bounce

Control Plane's timeline compiles status messages as reported by BleepingComputer and The Register. It quotes Microsoft saying servers restored under a new health-check method "can become unresponsive again once they begin receiving traffic," and that "Actions taken thus far have not provided the anticipated relief." It also says services were still "recovering more than 20 hours later" (Control Plane). That blog notes Azure's status page showed no active events, which supports reading this as a failure inside Microsoft's SaaS tier rather than the cloud substrate. The post is vendor content, and it says so.

Facts vs. opinions. Microsoft's own statements establish the mechanism at a high level. Servers dropped out after failing health checks, the remaining fleet was overloaded, and a connection-setup process was a choke point. They do not establish what started it. Microsoft has promised "a full Post-Incident Report with root cause and defense-in-depth actions" (SecurityWeek). Calling it a "cascading failure" is Control Plane's characterization, not Microsoft's, though the description fits. The impact figures need care. The $7 billion comes from Anderson Economic Group, whose president Patrick Anderson said "perhaps as many as 26 million users on Thursday and 51 million users on Friday" suffered economic losses, and that the cost will "likely increase beyond this initial estimate" (KCCI/CNN). This is a modelled estimate from an early, partial picture, not a measured loss. Morning Brew's wording, "could cost at least $7 billion" cnn, is a fair paraphrase. The same CNN piece says "As many as 430 million users were reportedly affected at some point." That phrasing does not identify a measurement, so treat it as an upper-bound guess. Downdetector peaked at 130,000 Outlook reports, per CNN. The "more than 70 countries" figure appears in Morning Brew via Downdetector, but not in the CNN text read here. On security: Microsoft said it had "no evidence of malicious activity" (SecurityWeek). SecurityWeek points out the outage began two days after a Patch Tuesday that "addressed 1,094 vulnerabilities," including CVE-2026-124194, an exploited Outlook spoofing flaw. It also says Microsoft's advisories have not linked that flaw to the outage. Report the coincidence and nothing more.

Other perspectives. SecurityWeek adds a detail the consumer coverage missed: some Google Workspace users had "trouble accessing their Google-hosted email through Outlook desktop and mobile email clients." The dependency on Microsoft's client stack reaches beyond its own mail tenants (SecurityWeek). CNN's piece links an item headlined "Microsoft outage shows the risks of relying on a single technology provider for essential services," but the text read here contains no substantive expert quotes on concentration risk (KCCI/CNN). Charles Lamanna, Microsoft's EVP for Copilot, Agents and Platform, said Microsoft would "regret the impact" and is "reviewing what happened and how we can prevent similar issues going forward" (BleepingComputer). According to Wikipedia's ground-stop article, Mullin explicitly dismissed any connection between the Microsoft outage and the FAA stop the next day (Wikipedia).

Why it matters. Put the two stories side by side. In both, a network change during a capacity or physical-layer upgrade sat quietly until a later change surfaced it. In both, the system's own protective machinery amplified the failure instead of containing it. At Microsoft, health checks evicted servers and concentrated load on the survivors. At the FAA, a router's connection retries flooded its peers. At the FAA, officials ruled out "cyber" and "AI" early, while Microsoft said it had no indication of malicious activity, and both stayed vague about what actually happened for a day or more. The engineering lesson is old: health checks that cannot tell "broken" from "busy" turn overload into an outage. That is Control Plane's general point, and nothing in Microsoft's statements yet confirms it applies here. What is new is how much now depends on Exchange specifically: Copilot, Intune, Defender, and Teams calendars all failed with it. If agent products built on these suites depend on the same back end, one Exchange incident takes them down together cnn.

Bessent blames Commerce over Megaspeed as Nvidia's leadership vacuum widens

1 source, 5 min read

What happened. On CNBC's Squawk Box on November 13, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said Treasury, Defense, State and the US Trade Representative all "were in favor of putting Megaspeed on the entity list for months." He said the decision belonged to Commerce: "you will have to ask them about their internal processes" (CNBC). Morning Brew renders the quote slightly differently, as "You'll have to ask them," and lists Treasury, Defense and State without USTR cnbc. Megaspeed is the Singapore-based firm suspected of passing advanced Nvidia chips to China. It is at the center of the scandal that ended with Jensen Huang's resignation as Nvidia CEO. Nvidia said in a regulatory filing that its board accepted the resignation on Wednesday, November 11, and CFO Colette Kress is interim CEO while staying CFO (TechCrunch).

Details. Bessent's broader remarks were about export leakage. He said, "I am frustrated when President Trump made it very clear that China should not receive any Nvidia chips above H100s," "any leakage is very frustrating," and "I think that there is a lot of leakage in the system." He also turned to Nvidia's diligence: "Did Nvidia know their client, and was there a robust effort at Nvidia to make sure that there was no leakage," asking whether Nvidia was "selling to what were obviously pass-through shell companies" (CNBC). CNBC reports that Trump approved H200 sales to approved Chinese buyers in December, with the US taking a share of sales. That sits awkwardly with Bessent's "above H100s" line, and the article does not reconcile the two. Commerce spokeswoman Kristen Eichamer denied a reported Situation Room confrontation, saying there was "no such conversation." She also said Lutnick "has proactively agreed to appear voluntarily before the Banking Committee." CNBC lists a closed-door House briefing for Huang on December 1, an interview with Nvidia general counsel Tim Teter on December 2, and a hearing for Commerce under secretary Jeff Kessler on December 3. CNBC also cites Axios as reporting that Lutnick "repeatedly stood in the way" of crackdowns on suspected diverters (same source).

On Megaspeed itself, CNBC's explainer draws on corporate filings. The company was "incorporated in 2015 as 7Road International" and is "wholly owned by Swiftdata, a Singapore entity incorporated in October 2023." Its assets went to "about $3 billion in 2024, from just $33 million a year earlier, and just $75 in 2022," and its revenue was "$294 million in 2024 from zero in 2023" (CNBC explainer). The chip-volume numbers differ by source. Trade records cited by The New York Times show the Malaysian subsidiary Speedmatrix bought "at least $4.8 billion worth of advanced AI equipment" between June 2024 and February 2026. The Information says Alice Huang's ties to the Nvidia CEO helped "facilitate at least $7.5 billion in Nvidia chip shipments," a figure CNBC says it "could not independently verify." Alice Huang "served as managing director for eight months in 2023," and Bloomberg reports she "has returned to Beijing." Singapore police opened an investigation into Megaspeed in October 2025. Singapore's Home Affairs ministry says "There were no such investigations conducted in respect of Alice Huang." Malaysia's trade minister says his ministry has not opened a probe into Speedmatrix. Megaspeed says it operates "fully within the bounds of all applicable export control regulations." Nvidia says it sees "no reason to believe that U.S. national security or Nvidia confidential information" was compromised (all from the CNBC explainer).

On Nvidia, the board told CNBC: "We are in the process of selecting a search firm for the Nvidia CEO role... We have not formally engaged any potential candidates." Morning Brew attributes to the FT a report that the board approached Arm CEO Rene Haas, and to Wired a report that some employees want Huang back. Neither report was read here cnbc. TechCrunch reports shares "down nearly 12% in early morning trading on Thursday" and "fallen by as much as 19%," a market cap loss of "more than $855 billion" measured from the previous Friday. It notes Huang holds "only about 3.5% of the company's shares" and that China was "about $7.9 billion," "roughly 8% of total revenue." Harvey C. Jones, a director since 1993, becomes the company's "first-ever chair of the board of directors" (TechCrunch). Nvidia reports earnings on Tuesday, per the same piece.

Facts vs. opinions. Established: Huang resigned, Kress is interim CEO, Megaspeed has not been put on the Entity List as of these reports, and Bessent publicly assigned responsibility to Commerce's BIS. Unestablished: that Alice Huang is a Chinese intelligence asset. CNBC attributes this to US intelligence "reportedly," and the underlying NYT reporting rests on "people briefed on the intelligence" (CNBC explainer). Morning Brew's shorthand of "a paramour" and "an alleged Chinese spy" and its claim of affairs "with two Nvidia employees" cnbc are stated more confidently than the primary sources read here support. TechCrunch does not cite an official reason for the resignation. It only ties the timing to Huang's admission of "a romantic affair with an alleged Chinese spy." Whether chips were actually diverted is also unproven. No charges against Megaspeed appear in the sources read, and the reported volumes range from $4.8 billion to $7.5 billion depending on the outlet and method. A letter from Sen. Tom Cotton to Lutnick in October 2025 asking for an investigation is widely referenced, but the Senate page could not be read for this issue.

Other perspectives. The political reading is that Bessent is distancing Treasury from a scandal that could reach the Commerce secretary. Morning Brew's framing that the Treasury secretary "blames" the Commerce secretary is accurate to the CNBC transcript cnbc. The policy reading is more interesting. Bessent moved the conversation from "Jensen Huang's personal foibles" to whether Nvidia ran adequate know-your-customer checks. That puts the company's compliance processes, not one executive's conduct, at issue (CNBC). From the market side, TechCrunch notes CoreWeave, Nebius and IREN all fell on Thursday. Nvidia's web of investments and guarantees means a leadership shock propagates into the neoclouds it finances (TechCrunch).

Why it matters. For people who buy or depend on compute, three things follow. First, export controls are likely to tighten in practice even if the rules do not change. Entity-listing Megaspeed and enforcing stricter know-your-customer rules on Southeast Asian intermediaries would raise friction for every legitimate GPU cloud in the region. Second, Nvidia is entering an earnings week and a December of congressional appearances with an interim CEO who is also its CFO. That is not an operations crisis, but it is a governance one. Third, the fact that Megaspeed's assets went from $75 to about $3 billion in two years and still escaped enforcement is a case study in how much can pass through a single-director Singapore shell before anyone acts cnbc.

Meta's TP/CP/EP inference post (Meta Engineering, October 17, 2025)

1 source, 3 min read

What it said. TLDR Data summarized Meta's post as introducing tensor, context and expert parallelism techniques that cut end-to-end latency "by 10–50% for key workloads compared with baseline libraries." The summary also highlighted direct data access (DDA) collective algorithms and context-parallel attention enabling "under one-minute inference of 10 million tokens" fb.

How it aged. The first finding from re-reading the original is that the newsletter summary overstated it. The post, by Cen Zhao, Xiaodong Wang and Jianyu Huang, says "DDA outperforming RCCL baseline by 10-50% for decode (small message sizes)" and "10-30% speedup for prefill, resulting in approximately 10% reduction in TTIT," meaning time to incremental token. All of these are on AMD MI300X against RCCL (Meta Engineering). So 10 to 50 percent is a collective-level speedup on one vendor's hardware against one library. It is not an end-to-end latency reduction. The end-to-end figure the post does give is about 10 percent on decode token latency. The post says it achieved significant speedups against NCCL too, but gives no NCCL numbers. Its framing for why collectives matter is that allreduce "can contribute up to 30% of end-to-end latency" in tensor parallelism, and all-to-all "10-30%" in expert parallelism. The long-context claims are more specific than the summary implied. They are "less than one minute for one million tokens on a single H100 host," under one minute for 10 million tokens across multiple H100 hosts, for example 32, and, for Llama 3 405B, "128K token prefill in 3.8 seconds with CP over 16 nodes, and 1M-token prefill in 77 seconds." The post also says Meta "achieved overall performance parity with NVIDIA H100" on MI300X, a vendor's own measurement without disclosed workloads. The context-parallel work is detailed in arXiv 2411.01783, which was not re-read here. The post itself mentions no open-source code release.

A natural place to look for an open-source release is torchcomms, Meta's communications API with its NCCLX backend, though neither the Meta post nor the torchcomms README links the post's techniques to it. As of November 15, 2026, the GitHub repository is titled "(deprecated) torchcomms" and says "Its features have been upstreamed into PyTorch Distributed (c10d)" and "new code should use torch.distributed directly." It also says "NCCLX is deprecated for OSS usage and must be explicitly enabled with USE_NCCLX=ON" (GitHub: torchcomms). The repo shows 405 stars and 199 forks. The README checked here does not mention DDA specifically. So whether the DDA flat and tree allreduce algorithms reached upstream c10d, and in what form, is not established by anything read for this issue. Searches turned up other low-latency allreduce work aimed at inference serving, including MSCCL++ and an expert-parallel NCCL API. Those papers were not read here, so no claim is made about whether vLLM or SGLang adopted Meta's specific algorithms.

Why it matters now. The general idea held up well: decode is dominated by small messages, latency-bound collectives matter more than bandwidth, and context parallelism makes million-token prefill practical on modest clusters. Meta's torchcomms did not last as a standalone project; its features were upstreamed into PyTorch Distributed (c10d), though whether the DDA algorithms were among them is not established. Two cautions remain useful. Read vendor engineering posts for their baseline: here, MI300X against RCCL, with no NCCL figures given. And a newsletter's one-line summary can turn a kernel-level number into an end-to-end claim. Today's lead stories show the same pattern in reverse: the interconnect, not the compute, is where things break fb.

Thinking Machines' "Modular Manifolds" and manifold Muon (September 26, 2025)

1 source, 2 min read

What it said. TLDR AI described a method that constrains weight matrices to curved surfaces, which it says makes training more stable and predictable. It ended with the judgment that the experiments "required computational overhead, which limits real-world adoption" thinkingmachines.

How it aged. The original post, by Jeremy Bernstein on the Connectionism blog, is more careful than that summary (Thinking Machines). Manifold Muon constrains weights to the Stiefel manifold, where all singular values equal one. It measures updates in the spectral norm and solves the constrained step with dual ascent on a matrix of Lagrange multipliers, then retracts using the matrix sign function. The post's "modular" idea composes modules as a Cartesian product of manifolds, with a weighted max of norms, so the learning rate can be budgeted across layers according to their sensitivity. The only experiment was "a small MLP for 3 epochs on the CIFAR-10 dataset," averaged over three seeds. The post reports that manifold Muon "attained higher train and test accuracy than AdamW," with no accuracy figures given in the text read. On overhead, it says "Manifold Muon increased the wall clock time per step compared to AdamW," that this "could be improved by running fewer steps of dual ascent or adding momentum," and that "Depending on other systems bottlenecks, the overhead may not be an issue." It also concedes that "hard manifold constraints may not ultimately be the right way to constrain weight matrices." A minimal implementation was released at github.com/thinking-machines-lab/manifolds. So the claim that overhead "limits real-world adoption" was the newsletter's prediction, not the author's.

Fourteen months later, the follow-up work is academic and small-scale. The clearest example is "Different Layers, Different Manifolds" by Kirato Yoshihara, arXiv 2606.13276, submitted June 11, 2026 and listed as accepted at the WSS workshop at ICML 2026 (arXiv). It applies manifold Muon to GPT-2 pretraining and finds that Stiefel constraints on attention combined with DGram constraints on MLPs worked best among the setups tested. The reverse assignment became unstable, because singular values growing in DGram-constrained attention weights can "induce softmax saturation." The abstract page gives no loss or perplexity numbers, and the code is on GitHub. Searches also surfaced a Stiefel-manifold steepest-descent method (arXiv 2601.21487) that uses manifold Muon as a baseline, and a paper claiming an exact closed-form Stiefel Muon update (arXiv 2608.06218). Neither was read, so their results are not reported. As of November 15, 2026, no evidence was found of manifold Muon being used to pretrain a model at billions of parameters. That is an absence in search results, not proof that no one has.

Why it matters now. The open questions in Bernstein's post are still open and well framed: "What manifolds should attention heads live on?", whether embeddings and unembeddings need different constraints, and whether constraints "make low-precision training easier." The Yoshihara result begins to answer the first, at GPT-2 scale and in a workshop paper. The practical takeaway: plain Muon spread widely, while manifold Muon is still a research tool. Its overhead is a dual-ascent loop, which is an engineering problem and not a fundamental one, so cheaper solvers could change that thinkingmachines.

Hermes Agent's agent-driven readability cleanup (September 2026)

1 source, 3 min read

What it said. AgentBrief reported on September 7, 2026, that Teknium was optimizing the Hermes Agent codebase for readability by both humans and agents across "3000+ contributors." It said one agent-driven cleanup "shed 375,000 lines while preserving behavior," citing @iamlukethedev for these figures: median defining-file size down 66%, average lookup tokens down 55%, multi-window reads down 71%, and tasks hitting files too large for 128k context "dropping from 1,589 to zero" x. The newsletter calls it "Hermes 4 Agent," but the project is Hermes Agent. This item is only about ten weeks old, so "aging" here mostly means checking the claims against the full write-up.

How it aged. Nous Research published "Refactoring Hermes with 1,393 agents" in September 2026 (Nous Research). The run started September 2 and merged September 4. It used 1,393 subagents with a peak of 218 concurrent, about 19 active hours, and 36 non-overlapping work groups, on an i7 desktop with 64 GB of RAM. The post says "Claude Fable 5.1 handled inference remotely." Non-test Python went from 1,063,826 to 698,363 lines, which the post calls a 34.4% reduction. The difference is 365,463 lines, so the "375,000" in the tweets is close but not the blog's figure. Files over 5,000 lines went from 37 to 6. Functions over 300 lines went from 192 to 2. gateway/run.py went from 34,847 lines to 5,512. In a simulated lookup over 4,000 symbols, average tokens per lookup fell from 2,218 to 993, about 55 percent. Lookups needing an additional read window fell from 628 to 184, about 71 percent. So the 55% and 71% figures from the newsletter match the blog. The "1,589 to zero" and "median defining file size down 66%" figures were not found in the text read here; the page may have been truncated. Cost was about $19,300 for the main run, roughly $25,000 with follow-ups, "excludes human review time," compared with a $150,000 to $1.8 million estimate for a human team.

The limitations are the valuable part, and Nous states them plainly: "These are lookup costs; we didn't measure agents completing engineering tasks." "The median lookup actually returned more tokens." "Splitting files increased the module count and import dependencies, and some entry points took longer to import." "The refactor made individual pieces easier to read without resolving all the coupling between them." The "preserving behavior" claim also has a qualifier. About 65 sites had their exception handling changed by a suppress() rewrite, and these "were real regressions the existing tests had missed," fixed over two rounds of community review before merge. The verification method was solid. A frozen test baseline was set first. Each tool's JSON schema had to stay identical. CLI --help output was compared byte for byte. Workers committed after each verified step. A check flagging removed public names was also added.

Why it matters now. The finding that holds up is narrow: breaking up giant files cuts the average cost of an agent looking up a symbol, and a large swarm can do that refactor in a day for low five figures. What the cleanup does not show is that agents perform engineering tasks better afterwards, because Nous did not measure that. The median result also went the wrong way. A team trying the same thing should measure task success on a held-out set of real issues before and after, not lookup tokens. The suppress() regressions are a warning that "behavior-preserving" refactors at this scale reach places the test suite does not cover x.

Claude Haiku 4.5 and the planner/executor bet (October 15, 2025)

1 source, 2 min read

What it said. AlphaSignal's and TLDR's coverage summarized Haiku 4.5 as "nearly frontier coding quality at much lower cost." It listed $1/$5 pricing per million input and output tokens and "~73.3% on SWE-bench Verified." It added claims that Haiku 4.5 reaches about 90% of Sonnet 4.5 in agentic coding and runs "4-5× faster than Sonnet 4.5." It also pitched the split where "Sonnet plans, Haiku executes" anthropic.

How it aged. Anthropic's announcement is dated October 15, 2025, and is more careful on conditions (Anthropic). It gives SWE-bench Verified as "73.3%, which was averaged over 50 trials, no test-time compute, 128K thinking budget." The scaffold had two tools, bash and string-replace file editing, ran on all 500 problems, and the prompt told the model to use tools "ideally more than 100 times." Anthropic's own speed claim is relative to Sonnet 4, not Sonnet 4.5: "similar levels of coding performance but at one-third the cost and more than twice the speed." The "4-5 times faster than Sonnet 4.5" line comes from a customer, Andrew Filev, quoted on the page. It is a testimonial, not an Anthropic measurement. The "90% of Sonnet 4.5" figure was not found in the text read for this issue. The planner/executor idea was Anthropic's: "Sonnet 4.5 can break down a complex problem into multi-step plans, then orchestrate a team of multiple Haiku 4.5s." Safety was framed as ASL-2, with a "statistically significantly lower overall rate of misaligned behaviors than both Claude Sonnet 4.5 and Claude Opus 4.1."

Its replacement is Claude Haiku 5.5, released October 7, 2026. Simon Willison wrote that day that "The previous Haiku, 4.5, was very much showing its age." He called its $1/$5 pricing "relatively expensive even back then." He noted that Haiku 5.5 "exactly matches the price of GPT-6 Luna—$0.10/$0.50—up to 100,000 tokens," rising 5x beyond that. He also found that Haiku 5.5 uses "a new, less generous tokenizer," with his counter showing roughly 1.25x more tokens for the same prompt, "so there's a hidden price increase there" (Simon Willison). Anthropic's system-prompt release notes now list Claude Haiku 5.5 alongside Haiku 4.5, confirming the lineage (Claude Docs). Reports that Haiku 4.5's earliest retirement date was October 15, 2026 appeared in search results but were not confirmed on an Anthropic page. Treat that as not checked.

The planner/executor bet has partial, mixed evidence. Anthropic's Agent SDK guidance, below, does push subagents for parallelism and context isolation. But the most prominent large-swarm example in this issue, Nous's 1,393-subagent refactor, ran its workers on Claude Fable 5.1, a frontier model, not a small one (Nous Research). One case proves nothing, but it fits a pattern: for long autonomous coding work, builders seem willing to pay for the stronger model at every level.

Why it matters now. Haiku 4.5's lasting contribution was showing that a small-tier model could pass 70% on SWE-bench Verified under a simple scaffold. Its pricing did not last: within a year the small tier dropped by an order of magnitude in list price, partly offset by a tokenizer change. The economic argument for multi-agent setups now rests on Haiku 5.5-class pricing, and anyone budgeting by token count should re-measure with the new tokenizer before trusting a migration estimate anthropic.

Claude Code SDK becomes the Claude Agent SDK (September 29, 2025)

1 source, 2 min read

What it said. AlphaSignal's one-line item reported the rename alphasignal. The newsletter link is a click-tracking redirect and was not opened. The primary source is Anthropic's post "Building agents with the Claude Agent SDK" by Thariq Shihipar, dated September 29, 2025 (Claude blog). It explains that the Claude Code harness "has begun to power almost all of our major agent loops," and "To reflect this broader vision, we're renaming the Claude Code SDK to the Claude Agent SDK." It describes the loop as "gather context -> take action -> verify work -> repeat." Its recommendations are agentic file-system search over semantic search, subagents with "their own isolated context windows," compaction that "automatically summarizes previous messages when the context limit approaches," and MCP for integrations. Its examples include finance, personal-assistant, customer-support and deep-research agents.

How it aged. The bet was that a coding harness would generalize into an agent runtime, and the evidence for that is moderate. The Python repository shows "8.5k stars" and "1.4k forks," an MIT license, 976 commits, 256 open issues and 407 pull requests (GitHub). That is a healthy library but not a dominant one. Third-party comparisons in search results put OpenAI's Agents SDK and Google's ADK well ahead on stars. Those pages were not opened, so their figures are not cited here. Several adoption claims appeared only in search snippets and were not checked: Apple Xcode integrating the SDK in February 2026, Spotify merging hundreds of agent-authored PRs a month through it, and a June 2026 billing change for SDK usage that was reversed the same day. The design choices in the original post have mostly become standard practice: subagents for context isolation, compaction, and "verify work" as an explicit stage. As disclosure, this briefing is itself produced by an agent built on this SDK.

The post's own caveats held up too. It called LLM-as-judge verification "generally not a very robust method" with "heavy latency tradeoffs." It called semantic search "less accurate, more difficult to maintain, and less transparent" than agentic search. And it said performance can shift as features are added, recommending a representative eval set built from real usage.

Why it matters now. The rename marked the point where "coding agent" and "general agent" runtimes converged. The harness-first approach has a quiet advantage: it inherits a decade of software-engineering tooling for verification, such as tests, linters, diffs and byte-for-byte output comparisons. Nous's refactor relied on exactly those. Domains without that tooling still lack a cheap "verify work" step, which is where the post's own caveats about LLM judges matter most alphasignal.

Meta's CWM, a 32B open-weights "Code World Model" (September 24, 2025)

1 source, 2 min read

What it said. TLDR AI and TLDR Founders reported Meta's release of CWM, "a 32B decoder-only LLM trained on code execution traces and reasoning tasks to explore world models in code generation" meta.

Paper facts. The technical report is arXiv 2510.02387, posted September 30, 2025 according to the Hugging Face card. Meta's publication page lists September 24, 2025 (Meta AI; Hugging Face). The authors are the Meta FAIR CodeGen team, about fifty people including Jade Copet, Jonas Gehring, Taco Cohen and Gabriel Synnaeve. It is an arXiv preprint, and no venue is stated on the pages read. CWM is a dense decoder-only model "trained with a context size of up to 131 k tokens." It was mid-trained on "observation-action trajectories from Python interpreter and agentic Docker environments," then given multi-task RL across coding, math and multi-turn software engineering. Its reported pass@1 scores are:

  • SWE-bench Verified: 53.9 without test-time scaling and 65.8 with it, on all 500 problems. The card notes that a gpt-oss comparison used a custom 477-problem subset.
  • LiveCodeBench: 68.6%
  • Math-500: 96.6%
  • AIME 2024: 76.0%
  • AIME 2025: 68.2

Meta released checkpoints "after mid-training, SFT, and RL" under the FAIR non-commercial research license. The model card lists "Use in any commercial product or service" as out of scope. The limitations section of the paper was not read here, so the authors' stated limitations are not reported.

How it aged. Adoption has been small, which the license makes unsurprising. As of November 15, 2026, the Hugging Face card shows 385 downloads in the last month, 272 likes, 3 adapters and 9 quantizations (Hugging Face). No successor CWM model turned up in searches. The most notable follow-up is odd. On May 1, 2026, with v2 on May 8, a 24-author "Code World Model Preparedness Report" (arXiv 2605.00932) appeared. It concludes that "CWM does not pose additional frontier risks beyond those present in the current AI ecosystem" and states "We therefore release it as an open-weight model" (arXiv). The weights had been public since September 2025, so this reads as a retroactive publication of pre-release testing rather than a release decision. The page does not explain the delay. A Medium critique arguing that most of CWM's SWE-bench gain comes from retries rather than world-model planning appeared in search results but was not read.

Why it matters now. The idea that a model trained to predict execution state line by line would code better is still untested in the strong sense. The benchmark gap between CWM with and without test-time scaling, 53.9 against 65.8, is larger than any gain clearly attributable to the execution-trace mid-training in the material read here. CWM remains a useful research artifact. But it did not become a lineage, and "world models for code" has since moved toward agentic environments rather than interpreter traces meta.

Brain & consciousness

Reading path: State of Brain Emulation Report 2025, part 3: emulation and embodiment (computational neuroscience)

3 min read

What to read. The report is arXiv 2510.15745 by Niccolò Zanichelli, Maximilian Schons, Isaak Freeman, Philip Shiu and Anton Arkhipov. It was first posted October 17, 2025, and the current version, v3, is from November 5, 2025 (arXiv). The full text and companions are at the project site (brainemulation.mxschons.com). The abstract organizes the field into three capabilities: "recording brain function (Neural Dynamics)," "mapping brain structure (Connectomics)," and "emulation and embodiment (Computational Neuroscience)." This step is the third. The site's own layout differs. The modelling material sits in the Technical Overview starting at PDF page 68, with neuron models, synapse dynamics and hardware requirements in a section the site calls "Part 3, Methods." So "part 3" here means the third capability, and the reading is the modelling chapter plus the Methods material. Read Part 1's distinction between "simulation (matching outputs)" and "emulation (reproducing the causal machinery)" first, because the rest depends on it.

Paper facts. This is an arXiv preprint in q-bio.NC, CC BY 4.0, with a Zenodo DOI. No journal reference is listed. LessWrong's launch post, from January 28, 2026, describes it as a one-year project with more than 45 expert contributors (LessWrong). It is a review and roadmap, not an experiment, so there is no dataset or baseline in the usual sense. Its funders, per the site, are Fieldcrest Ventures, MxSchons GmbH and the Foresight Institute. That is worth knowing, because the report argues the field deserves funding.

Key ideas. The headline claims, as quoted in the LessWrong summary, are these. "In C. elegans, connectome-constrained and embodied models now reproduce specific behaviors." "In the fruit fly, whole-brain models recapitulate known circuit dynamics." And "feasibility studies on large GPU clusters have demonstrated simulations approaching human-brain scale," "albeit with simplified biophysical assumptions." Three ideas matter most for this reading path.

First, the bottleneck is data, not compute. The site says "Data is the bottleneck, not hardware or algorithms" and "Data constraints set a hard upper bound on model quality." The LessWrong summary adds that "passive recordings alone struggle to uniquely specify model parameters." In other words, many parameter settings fit the same observed activity, which is the classic identifiability problem.

Second, where hardware does constrain, the limits are "hardware memory capacity and interconnect bandwidth, not raw processing power." That is the same constraint as Meta's inference-collectives post in today's archive, in a different field.

Third, the report proposes evaluation criteria: "neural activity prediction, embodied behaviors and responses to controlled perturbations." The perturbation requirement is what separates an emulation from a curve fit.

For the embodiment side, the best concrete case is BAAIWorm, published in Nature Computational Science on December 16, 2024. Its Peking University summary describes "a biophysically detailed neural network simulating the entire 302-neuron C. elegans neural system." Each neuron is "a multi-compartment model," and the body "incorporates 96 muscle actuators" in a body "composed of 3,341 tetrahedrons" inside "a 3D fluid environment," with a food-gradient input closing the loop. The authors report that it "replicates C. elegans' locomotive behaviors" and that "synthetic perturbations of synaptic connections impact the neural control of movement and affect embodied motor behavior." They acknowledge "simplified sensory inputs and essential model simplifications," and the code is open (PKU NBIC). The Nature article page itself redirected to a login and was not read. The fly-side counterpart is whole-brain modelling built on the adult Drosophila connectome, credited on the site with predicting responses for "feeding and grooming." The site cites no specific paper, so its specifics are not checked.

Open questions. Several numbers from the site frame the hard problems. "40-50% of synaptic connections differ between identical worms," which makes "the" connectome a statistical object rather than a blueprint. Recording coverage is about 80% for zebrafish larvae and about 50% for C. elegans, with calcium imaging "often 1-30 Hz," far too slow for spikes. The LessWrong summary adds that "many biological mechanisms like neuromodulation are still largely omitted." The report's sequencing is "Faithful emulation of small brains is the first step," and its cost guess for organisms under 1 million neurons is "potentially within the decade, at a cost in the low $100Ms." That figure comes from advocates and has no detailed breakdown on the overview page. The site mentions a "minimum threshold for a model to qualify as an emulation," but the overview page does not state it. Read that definition in the full PDF before relying on any summary of it. Finally, the field is tiny: "less than 500 people globally are actively dedicated to the direct objectives of brain emulation." Taken at face value, that means one well-run zebrafish or fly closed-loop project would move the field more than any amount of argument about timelines.

Fractal trainability boundaries

1 source, 2 min read

Why this is here: exploring inference infrastructure, kernels, and model tooling.

The saved item is a Towards AI piece by Lian Kim titled "Fractal Recursion, Bounded Infinity, and Adapted systems," teased as "What fractals, loss landscapes, and dynamical..." medium. Medium returned a 403, so the article was not read and nothing here describes its argument. The topic its teaser points to has a citable anchor. Jascha Sohl-Dickstein's "The boundary of neural network trainability is fractal," arXiv 2402.06184, was submitted February 9, 2024 and is listed as "3 pages, mesmerizing fractals" (arXiv). The argument is an analogy that the paper then tests. The Mandelbrot and Julia sets come from iterating a function and asking whether the sequence diverges. Gradient descent is also an iterated function, and training "can result in convergent or divergent behavior, and can be extremely sensitive to small changes in hyperparameters." The experiment sweeps hyperparameters and maps where training stays bounded versus diverges. The finding: "this boundary is fractal over more than ten decades of scale in all tested configurations."

The reader's interest is infrastructure and tooling, so the honest fit is with training more than inference. The practical point is this: if the boundary between stable and divergent hyperparameters is fractal, then near the edge, a small change in learning rate does not produce a small change in outcome, and no amount of grid refinement will smooth it out. That explains several things practitioners see: why tuning for the edge of stability is fragile, why learning-rate warmup and clipping exist, and why results at the optimal learning rate often fail to reproduce across seeds or hardware. Today's archive offers a connection, though it is an interpretation and no source makes it. Manifold Muon's constraint that singular values stay near one is one way of stepping back from that fractal edge. It changes the dynamics so the boundary sits further from typical settings.

Does it hold up? As checked on November 15, 2026, the arXiv page lists only v1, no venue and no linked code. The abstract page gives no network sizes or architectures, so how far "all tested configurations" generalizes is not stated there. Treat it as a well-known, striking preprint demonstration from a credible author, likely on small networks, and not as a characterization of large-model training. Whether the Medium piece adds anything beyond the analogy cannot be judged, because it could not be read medium.
