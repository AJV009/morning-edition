Nvidia researchers say agents don't need long context; also: Raschka on RoPE, Huang leaves Nvidia, Anthropic's IPO book and SoftBank's turbine write-off

Today: The lead is a new Nvidia technical report, "You don't need long context." It finds that Claude Opus 4.5 and GPT 5.6 Sol lose little accuracy when an agent's history is compacted to a few thousand tokens. The issue covers which numbers hold up, why the cost picture depends on prompt caching, and why one Qwen coding model fell apart under the same treatment. Second is Sebastian Raschka's long explainer on positional embeddings, from GPT-2's learned table to partial RoPE, NoPE hybrids and a learned relative bias in one 2026 model, along with the Hacker News argument over whether RoPE is maths or luck. Third, a markets newsletter's three-story week is checked against primary reporting: Jensen Huang resigned as Nvidia's CEO, SoftBank posted a ¥1.12 trillion loss after writing off turbine prepayments, and Anthropic's IPO is reported about six times oversubscribed. The archive revisits six items: Meta-linked "early experience" agent training, now at ICML 2026; Karpathy's SRAM-versus-DRAM thread, which the Groq and Cerebras deals have since tested; Nvidia's Nemotron 3 launch promises against what shipped; MemEvolve; Grafana Mimir's 92% memory claim; and the endpoint agent-security startup Manifold. Ways of thinking takes Hamming's important-problems rule. The wildcards are Markus Buehler's "recursive meta-intelligence" and the OmniVerifier paper, and one quick hit covers AI-written books on Amazon.

Nvidia: "You don't need long context". Frequent compaction keeps agents accurate, but the cost depends on caching

1 source, 7 min read

What happened. AlphaSignal's Sunday deep dive, written by Ben Dickson, covers a new Nvidia study which argues that agents often do not need their full, growing interaction history. On SWE-bench Verified it reports that Claude Opus 4.5 and GPT-5.6 Sol held or improved accuracy when their context was compacted at a fixed threshold source. The primary source is the arXiv technical report "You don't need long context" (arXiv 2611.05306, v1 submitted 3 November 2026). It has twelve authors, from Peter Belcak to Pavlo Molchanov. The abstract ends: "Our findings question the need for long-context language models" (arXiv abstract). The newsletter's summary matches the paper on most numbers it repeats. It flattens several qualifications that matter for anyone deciding whether to tighten an agent harness's context limits source.

Details. The paper starts from serving cost, not accuracy. On H100 cloud serving, cutting the configured context window from 256K to 16K tokens raised output-token throughput 3.6x for GLM 5.2, 5.6x for Kimi K2.6 and 7x for Qwen 3 Coder Next. The authors turn this into 16K-to-256K cost ratios of 0.2772, 0.1777 and 0.1439, and use a rounded mean of r = 0.20 in their cost estimates. They price GPUs at $3.85 per H100-hour (arXiv HTML). GLM and Kimi appear only in this throughput study, with no task-accuracy results. The 13 benchmarks split into six long-horizon tasks (SWE-bench Verified, SWE-bench Multilingual, Terminal-Bench 2.1, FRAMES, τ²-bench, BFCLv3) and seven long-input tasks (LongBench v2, LOONG, AA-LCR, NIAH, RULER, Counting-Stars, BABILong) (arXiv HTML) source.

Three regimes are compared. The baseline keeps the full history (FHA). End-of-Conversation Compaction (EoCC) compacts when the context reaches a threshold, then restarts from the original task plus the compacted state. Single-Action Compaction (SAC) allows one environment action per context and carries only a rewritten state forward. EoCC settings are written as total limit over threshold, in thousands of tokens: 48/36, 32/24, 24/18 and 16/12. On SWE-bench Verified, Opus 4.5 scored 70.0 with full history, 73.6, 72.0, 74.8 and 68.8 across those four settings, and 70.2 under SAC. GPT 5.6 Sol scored 84.4 with full history, 88.8, 85.0, 85.0 and 84.6 across the settings, and 82.4 under SAC (arXiv HTML). The newsletter says both models "almost matched" the baseline under SAC source. That is fair for Opus (70.2 against 70.0). GPT lost 2.0 points (82.4 against 84.4).

On Terminal-Bench 2.1, Opus went from 47.2 with full history to a best of 52.8 at 32/24 and 51.8 under SAC. GPT went from 87.6 to a best of 88.8 at 48/36 and 84.3 under SAC. The paper notes that the GPT SAC figure covers 75 of 89 tasks (arXiv HTML). On FRAMES, every compaction setting scored below the full-history baseline for both models: Opus 81.0 against 77.3 to 79.7, GPT 90.2 against 86.9 to 88.7. Across the six long-horizon benchmarks, the authors summarise the accuracy change as −3.7 to +5.6 points for Opus and −3.3 to +4.4 points for GPT. At most 5 of 22 configurations per model lose more than 2 points (arXiv HTML).

The peak-context table is easy to miss. With full history on SWE-bench Verified, Opus had a median peak context of 32K and a maximum of 200K. Under SAC these fell to 8K and 16K. For GPT 5.6 Sol, the full-history median was 18K with a 173K maximum. At its best compaction setting (48/36) the median rose to 22K while the maximum fell to 35K (arXiv HTML). So for GPT the threshold regime mostly cut off the long tail rather than shrinking the typical run. That matters for serving because the tail sets the KV-cache budget. The newsletter's "median peak context was just 7,000–9,000 tokens" is accurate for SAC only: 8K and 9K for Opus, 7K and 8K for GPT across the two benchmarks source.

For long-input tasks, the documents are stored as files and the agent reads them with tools such as grep and sed. The authors report accuracy changes of −0.4 to +40.0 points for Opus and +1.0 to +30.9 for GPT (arXiv HTML). The newsletter's reading is that "Most of the benefit came from deciding what to read, rather than summarizing what had already been read" source. The per-benchmark long-input breakdown fell outside the portion of the paper read for this issue, so which benchmark produced the +40-point gain is not stated here.

Paper facts. It is an arXiv technical report (v1, 3 November 2026; the comments field reads "Technical report. 31 pages, 8 figures."), with no venue named and not peer-reviewed. The authors are Belcak, Muralidharan, Jiang, Fu, Byeon, Ye, Angilly, Taghibakhshi, Liu, Ouyang, Heinrich and Molchanov. Affiliations are not listed on the abstract page, though the newsletter identifies them as Nvidia researchers. Main accuracy experiments use Claude Opus 4.5 and GPT 5.6 Sol on the 13 benchmarks above, with the software tasks run in the third-party mini-swe-agent v2.4 harness. Open-weight accuracy results cover Qwen 3.6 27B, Qwen 3 Coder Next, MiniMax M2.7, Gemma 4 31B and Step 3.7 Flash. Baselines are full-history agents. The stated limitations are, verbatim: "Our evidence does not rule out the existence of tasks for which long context is beneficial", "nor does it prove that every possible task can be solved with short context", and "Compaction is a lossy context compression method." The portion read does not say how many seeds or runs back each number, so single-run noise on 2-5 point gains cannot be ruled out from what was read. No code release is stated (arXiv abstract; arXiv HTML).

Facts vs. opinions. The accuracy tables, peak-context figures and throughput ratios are the authors' own measurements. The "estimated optimized" costs are a projection, as is the claim that short contexts are much cheaper. The paper reports two cost columns. "Nominal" costs use API list prices with prompt caching (Opus at $5/$0.50/$25 per million new-input, cached-input and output tokens; GPT at $4/$0.40/$20). Under those prices compaction lowered Opus's SWE-bench Verified bill from $420.00 to as little as $295.75 at 24/18. GPT's bill rose at every setting, from $167.68 to $186.08, $184.16, $188.96 and $203.12 (arXiv HTML). The newsletter reports the GPT rise correctly and explains it: compaction rewrites the prefix and invalidates the cache source. The "estimated optimized" column, which falls as low as $40.62 for GPT and $62.53 for Opus on the same benchmark, applies the r = 0.20 throughput-derived ratio. It assumes a provider would price short-context serving to reflect its lower cost, and no provider is shown doing so. The headline savings ranges (Opus 2.2-8.8x and GPT 2.4-5.0x on long-horizon tasks, and up to 233.3x for GPT on long-input tasks) are estimates of that kind, not invoices (arXiv HTML). "Question the need for long-context language models" is an opinion drawn from these results, and the stated limitations argue against reading it as a general rule.

Other perspectives. The open-weight results complicate the story. Qwen 3 Coder Next fell from 53.0 to 49.2, 40.4 and 3.6 on SWE-bench Verified as the limit tightened to 16/12. The authors attribute this to the model's "verbose trajectories" leaving "too little room for useful progress between compactions." Step 3.7 Flash fell from 70.0 to 56.6, and Gemma 4 31B from 65.2 to 58.0, at the same setting. MiniMax M2.7 (72.6 to 67.8) and Qwen 3.6 27B (68.6 to 65.0) held up better (arXiv HTML). The newsletter says Qwen3.6-27B and MiniMax M2.7 "showed very little accuracy drops" source. At 16/12 the drops were 3.6 and 4.8 points, which is small next to the Qwen Coder collapse but larger than the 2-point line the paper uses for the frontier models. So the result that frontier models barely notice is specific to two closed frontier models, and the authors' own open-weight table shows that compaction tolerance is a property of each model, not a free lunch. The paper also says good compactions are "not narratives of the execution history but carefully cherry-picked briefs for further execution" (arXiv HTML). That claim describes a prompt the authors designed, and the full Appendix G prompt text was not visible in the portion read. A reader cannot tell from that portion how the prompt was tuned, or whether tuning touched the evaluation benchmarks.

Why it matters. For anyone building agent harnesses there are three practical points. First, structured handoffs (objective, established facts, relevant files, failed approaches, next action) are a better compaction format than narrative summaries source. Second, on a cached API, compaction can raise your bill, so measure cost per completed task rather than tokens per request. Third, test each model, because a 16K limit that is harmless for Opus wrecked Qwen 3 Coder Next. The Semantic State Space Model proposal is a model "post-trained to (1) comprehend an input language description of a state of a conversation," then act and rewrite that state for a fresh instance (arXiv HTML). It is a research direction with no trained model behind it yet. The paper also lands in a week when Nvidia's leadership and its memory suppliers are in the news (story three). A result suggesting agents need far less KV cache is, for the company that sells HBM-heavy GPUs, an odd thing to publish. Read it as a statement of research priorities, not a hardware forecast.

Raschka's "Understanding Positional Embeddings in Modern LLMs": from GPT-2's lookup table to partial RoPE and NoPE hybrids

1 source, 5 min read

What happened. Sebastian Raschka published a long, deliberately technical walkthrough of how LLMs encode token position, with a small PyTorch implementation. He frames it as a building block for a coming architecture-trends article, and says readers had long asked for a RoPE tutorial source. The web version on Ahead of AI (Raschka) carries the full Section 6 survey of positional choices in 2026 open-weight models, which the newsletter excerpt cuts off. It was also discussed on Hacker News.

Details. The piece starts from first principles. With no positional signal and no causal mask, self-attention is permutation-equivariant, so "The red fox jumps over the gray fox" and its swap give the same pairwise attention scores. A causal mask adds some implicit order, but "doesn't distinguish a separation of one position from a separation of ten," which is why explicit methods remain source. GPT-2 learns a table of 1,024 position vectors and adds one to each token embedding before the first block. Raschka works out that in GPT-2 Medium those position weights are 0.30% of parameters at 1,024 positions, but would be more than 22% at about 100k positions. This is one concrete reason learned absolute tables did not survive the move to long contexts source. The 2017 transformer's fixed sinusoidal encodings are covered as a stepping stone. With eight features the four frequencies are 1, 0.1, 0.01 and 0.001 radians per position, and a shift by a fixed offset becomes a fixed rotation of each sine/cosine pair. Raschka quotes the original paper's hypothesis that this lets the model "easily learn to attend by relative positions" source.

RoPE, from Su et al.'s 2021 RoFormer paper, moves the rotation inside attention. Queries and keys, not values, are rotated pairwise by position times a per-pair frequency θ_i = b^(−2i/d), with base b = 10,000 in RoFormer. The angle between a rotated query and key then depends only on the offset between their positions (Raschka). The worked example uses 20 degrees per position and both pairs starting at (1, 5). Positions 5 and 2 give a dot product of 13, positions 8 and 5 also give 13, and moving the key to position 3 gives about 19.92. Raschka contrasts the split-half pairing, which pairs feature j with j + d/2 and appears in his Qwen3 code, with the interleaved pairing of the original paper and the original LLaMA. The two are mathematically equivalent under a consistent reordering of features. This is a classic source of silent bugs when porting checkpoints between codebases (Raschka) source.

Section 6 is the reason to read it now. MiniMax-M3 applies partial RoPE: 64 of 128 head dimensions are rotated, with QK-Norm before RoPE, at a 1M-token context. Raschka calculates that partial RoPE saves 0.134 GB (128 MiB) of rotation-table memory in BF16 at that context, or 0.038 GB in NVFP4, and leaves the KV cache unchanged. He cites a study on 1B-8B models where rotating about 10% of features was often comparable to full RoPE, though full RoPE won some evaluations, and he calls partial RoPE "hardly worth it" on the memory argument (Raschka). SmolLM3 (3B) mixes three RoPE layers and then one NoPE layer, repeated across 36 layers, and its authors report better long-context performance. Inkling, a 975B model, uses a learned relative position bias added to attention scores before softmax, in both its sliding-window and global layers, conditioned on the query's hidden state and the distance back to the key. Raschka describes it as a descendant of T5's simpler learned bias. On trends from his architecture gallery, he says RoPE in every softmax layer is still the most common choice, but its share fell from 2025 to 2026 and again in late 2026. Among RoPE models, partial RoPE moved from minority to majority, and every "NoPE only" entry is a recurrent hybrid (Raschka).

Facts vs. opinions. The mechanics, parameter counts and table-memory arithmetic can be checked against the code. The gallery-share trends are Raschka's own count over his own gallery, which is a curated sample of open-weight releases, not a census. His outlook is labelled as opinion: "RoPE is still the predominant position embedding method, and I think this will carry over into 2027 for sure," and "Perhaps, though, we'll see more architectures with an Inkling-like design coming up in 2027" (Raschka). The NoPE evidence he cites is mixed. A 107M-parameter model ranked first across ten length-generalisation tasks, ahead of RoPE, while a 1.3B code model degraded at the longest lengths tested. Neither reaches frontier scale. The piece does not cover context-extension methods (YaRN, NTK scaling, base-frequency changes). In practice those are how RoPE models reach their advertised windows, so readers who want to know why a 1M context is achievable need another source.

Other perspectives. The Hacker News thread split along a familiar line. Commenter programjames argued that the post explains construction, not understanding, because "Positional embeddings are group representations," and RoPE is a representation of the translation group. They added that the RoPE authors "knowingly designed their embedding to be translation invariant." nlitened called the group framing "post-factum rationalization", saying RoPE won empirically "not because it's good math." fc417fc802 took a middle view: "Both things can be true and useful." Another commenter, senderista, said a linked Jane Street post on group theory and positional encodings was "better than TFA" (Hacker News). These are opinions from anonymous commenters. The useful point in them is that the group view predicts which generalisations are natural: 2D and 3D relative positions for images, video or point clouds. The empirical view explains why the field keeps trying ablations such as partial RoPE and NoPE layers.

Why it matters. For local-inference and open-weights work, the positional scheme is a compatibility surface. Partial-RoPE fractions, split-half against interleaved pairing, and mixed NoPE layers all have to match exactly when you port, quantise or write custom kernels. There is also a link to the lead story. If agents can work well at 8K to 32K tokens of live context, as Nvidia's report claims for two frontier models, then the long-context positional machinery matters less for agent loops and more for single-pass long-input tasks. That is an inference from today's two lead stories, not something either author claims.

"Agent Learning via Early Experience", Meta-linked reward-free agent training (originally covered 13 October 2025)

2 sources, 3 min read

What it said. TLDR AI described a Meta training approach, "early experience," that uses data from an agent's own interactions without external rewards and improves policy learning through implicit world modelling and self-reflection arxiv. The arXiv abstract frames it as a middle ground between imitation learning, which scales poorly, and reinforcement learning, which is hard where rewards cannot be verified (websites) or rollouts are long (multi-turn tool use). In the abstract's words, the agent's actions generate data where "the resulting future states serve as supervision without reward signals" (arXiv).

Paper facts. arXiv 2510.08558. v1 was posted 9 October 2025, v2 on 13 October 2025 and v3 on 24 May 2026. v3 is 303 KB against 2,214 KB for the earlier versions, so it was substantially reorganised. The comments field reads "ICML 2026." There are thirty authors, led by Kai Zhang and ending with Yu Su and Yifan Wu. Affiliations are not listed on the abstract page, so the "Meta" attribution rests on the newsletter. The abstract reports evaluation on "eight diverse environments" across multiple model families and says the methods "consistently improve effectiveness and out-of-domain generalization." It also reports "promising signals" as a warm start for RL. The abstract gives no numbers. Limitations were not read in the paper text for this issue (arXiv). Code: see below.

How it aged. Academically, it aged well. Acceptance at ICML 2026, per the arXiv comments field, is the main signal. The most concrete adoption evidence is an open-source reproduction, OSU-NLP-Group/EarlyExperience. It describes itself as an "Open-source reproduction" backed by OSU NLP and NeoCognition, built by Xiangchao Chen, Tinghui Zhu and Kai Zhang, and released under MIT. As of today it has 103 stars, 1 fork and 15 commits, so it is a modest following, not broad adoption. The repo ships full pipelines for seven environments: ALFWorld, WebShop, BFCL v4, ScienceWorld, TravelPlanner, AppWorld and TextCraft (the last two not in the paper). Tau-Bench and SearchQA are marked "coming soon", and WebArena data is released with results pending arxiv.

The reproduction's numbers support the claim on these environments, compared with imitation learning (IL) on Qwen2.5 7B: - ALFWorld: IL 58.6, implicit world modelling (IWM) 68.8, self-reflection (SR) 74.2. - WebShop: 39.1 to 48.4 (IWM) and 55.5 (SR). - BFCL: 44.9 to 51.1 (IWM) and 49.6 (SR). - TravelPlanner: 25.0 to 29.4 and 30.0. - TextCraft: 73.0 to 76.8 and 74.5. - ScienceWorld: 65.4 to 68.6 and 66.0. - AppWorld, on Qwen2.5 14B: 43.7 to 59.7 (IWM) and 51.0 (SR).

The repo itself states that every number comes from a single training run's final checkpoint with no re-runs, and that it uses a unified training setup rather than the paper's per-environment hyperparameters. The gains on ScienceWorld (+0.6 for SR) and TextCraft (+1.5 for SR) are small enough that single-run variance could account for them (GitHub). A search for papers that build on it surfaced nothing usable, so its downstream influence is not established here. The reproduction page does not mention Meta, so whether an official Meta codebase exists was not checked.

The fair summary is that the method is real and reproducible on small open models. Reward-free supervision from observed next states is not yet shown to beat RL where RL is feasible. The paper's own framing is that it is a starting point for RL, not a replacement.

Why it matters now. Today's Nvidia report shows that the agent's state representation can be compressed aggressively at inference time. Early experience works on the training side of the same problem. Predicting the next state from the agent's own actions is a cheap way to teach a policy environment dynamics, which is roughly what a good compaction "briefing" must capture. If SSSM-style models, post-trained to maintain explicit state, ever get built, implicit world-modelling data is an obvious ingredient. That link is this newsletter's inference, not either paper's claim arxiv +1.

Karpathy on SRAM, DRAM and the "tokens tsunami" (originally covered 26 February 2026)

2 sources, 3 min read

What it said. The AINews item summarised a high-engagement Karpathy thread arguing that the core constraint is two distinct memory pools: fast, tiny on-chip SRAM and large, slow off-chip DRAM. Orchestrating memory and compute across prefill, decode and training is, in that summary, the central puzzle. Decode under long context in tight agentic loops is the hardest workload for both the "HBM-first" (Nvidia-like) and "SRAM-first" (Cerebras-like) camps. The same item also covered Inception Labs' diffusion LLMs and Goodfire's interpretability infrastructure x.

How it aged. The original post, read through X search, is dated 25 February 2026. It describes off-chip DRAM as memory "you can only suck through a long straw." It calls the design of the substrate and the orchestration problem "probably today's most interesting intellectual puzzle with the highest rewards (\cite 4.6T of NVDA)." It ends by congratulating MatX on a raise, noting Karpathy's "small involvement." A same-day post by MatX co-founder Reiner Pope announced a $500M Series B for the MatX One, a chip he said "combines the low latency of SRAM-first designs with the long-context support of HBM," with Karpathy among the listed participants. So the thread was partly a disclosure-adjacent endorsement of a company he invested in. The diagnosis may still be right, but that context matters x.

On the diagnosis itself, events since have largely borne it out, by both camps merging rather than either winning. IEEE Spectrum (15 September 2026) reports that Nvidia paid about US $20 billion for Groq talent and IP at the end of 2025, a deal the article calls controversial. About three months later, at GTC 2026, Nvidia unveiled the Groq 3 LPU: 500 MB of on-die SRAM wired directly to its floating-point units, with 256 LPUs per Groq 3 LPX rack. Nvidia's Ian Buck says "The LPU has seven times the memory bandwidth of the GPU." The resulting design splits the work: Rubin GPUs take prefill, the LPU takes decode. Buck says "We do all the attention math and context processing on the Vera Rubin [GPU] rack" and the expert matrix multiplications on the LPU (IEEE Spectrum). That is the "HBM-first" camp buying an SRAM-first decode engine, which is Karpathy's framing turned into a product.

The same article reports that Cerebras's WSE-3 (44 GB of SRAM per wafer, enough for roughly 40-80B parameters on one chip) serves OpenAI's GPT-5.3-Codex-Spark at more than 1,000 tokens per second, against 50-125 tokens per second for standard GPT-5.4. AWS pairs Trainium for prefill with Cerebras for decode. d-Matrix stacks logic directly on commodity DRAM, and Majestic Labs pursues up to 128 TB of DRAM per rack against about 20 TB of HBM3E in a GB300 NVL72 (IEEE Spectrum). The diffusion-LLM speed claims and Goodfire's interpretability infrastructure from the same AINews item were not followed up for this issue. MatX does not appear in the Spectrum article, and whether its chip taped out was not checked.

One part of Karpathy's framing is unresolved: "inference decode and over long token contexts in tight agentic loops." SRAM decode engines hold weights on-chip, but long contexts need a large KV cache, and that pulls you back toward HBM or DRAM. Today's Nvidia compaction report attacks the same problem from the software side. It reports median peak contexts of 7K to 9K tokens under single-action compaction on two benchmarks (arXiv HTML). If that generalises, it weakens the "long context" half of the hardest workload and favours SRAM-first decode.

Why it matters now. The bet in February was that memory orchestration, not raw FLOPs, would decide inference economics. Nine months later, Nvidia's own roadmap splits prefill and decode across different memory hierarchies, and its research group argues that agents may not need the contexts that make decode memory-bound. For capacity planning, the practical question is whether your agent traffic looks like Nvidia's compacted traces (short contexts, many turns) or like long-input document work. The hardware answer differs for each x +1.

MemEvolve: meta-evolution of agent memory systems (originally covered 25 December 2025)

2 sources, 2 min read

What it said. A Hugging Face daily-papers entry for MemEvolve, with 13 upvotes at the time huggingface. The abstract describes a framework that evolves both an agent's stored experience and the architecture of its memory system. It also introduces EvolveLab, which reimplements twelve representative memory systems in a modular design space of four components (encode, store, retrieve, manage). On four agentic benchmarks it reports improving frameworks such as SmolAgent and Flash-Searcher by "up to 17.06%," with evolved memory architectures that transfer across benchmarks and backbone LLMs (arXiv).

Paper facts. arXiv 2512.18746, v1, 21 December 2025, one version only, by Guibin Zhang, Haotian Ren, Chong Zhan, Zhenhong Zhou, Junhao Wang, He Zhu, Wangchunshu Zhou and Shuicheng Yan. Affiliations are not listed on the abstract page, and the arXiv page lists no venue. Tested on four agentic benchmarks; the repo names GAIA (validation set), WebWalkerQA (a sampled 170-task subset) and xBench DeepSearch-2505. Baselines are the reimplemented memory systems. The headline is "up to 17.06%". The baseline that gain is measured against and the authors' stated limitations were not read here. Code is at github.com/bingreeky/MemEvolve (arXiv; GitHub).

How it aged. The repo README announces "MemEvolve has been accepted by ICML 2026!" and the repo title carries an "[ICML'26]" tag. As of today it has 286 stars, 33 forks, 3 commits on main, no releases, one open issue and two open pull requests (GitHub). It reproduces 11 baseline memory systems, including Agent-KB, ExpeL, Voyager and Dynamic Cheatsheet, plus two evolved ones (Lightweight Memory and Cerebra Fusion Memory). The README says plainly: "This is not the final release. A more robust version is still being organized" (GitHub).

So the paper got peer-reviewed acceptance, but the engineering artefact has not moved past its initial drop, almost eleven months on. Searches surfaced third-party projects that appear to build on EvolveLab and an aggregator review critical of its experimental detail, but none of those pages were opened, so their content is not checked. No official follow-up paper from the authors was found. The fact that a 33-fork repo has three commits suggests people are using EvolveLab as a baseline zoo more than MemEvolve as a meta-evolution system. That is an inference from the counts, not a measurement.

Why it matters now. Nvidia's report today amounts to a hand-designed memory policy: compact at a threshold into a structured brief (objective, facts, files, failed approaches, next step), and keep full artefacts available for retrieval source. MemEvolve's premise is that such policies should be searched for, not designed, and that the best memory architecture depends on the task and the backbone. The compaction report's open-weight table is weak evidence for that premise. The same 16K budget left MiniMax M2.7 at 67.8 and Qwen 3 Coder Next at 3.6 on SWE-bench Verified (arXiv HTML). A memory policy that adapts to the model's verbosity, which is what MemEvolve-style search could in principle find, is the obvious experiment neither group has run huggingface.

Grafana Mimir Query Engine: "92% less peak memory" (originally covered 19 September 2025)

2 sources, 2 min read

What it said. TLDR DevOps summarised Grafana's post on the Mimir Query Engine (MQE). In Mimir 2.17, MQE streams query processing, reduces peak memory "by 92% in some tests," is 100% PromQL-compatible, and can be toggled via the command line grafana.

How it aged. Start with what the 92% was. Grafana's post by Jon Kartago Lamida (17 September 2025) gives a single benchmark: sum by (group) over 100k input series with 10 distinct group values. MQE used 75 MB of peak memory against 954 MB for Prometheus' engine, and ran in 4.2 s against 7 s, which the post calls 39% faster. 954 MB to 75 MB is about a 92.1% reduction, so the headline is that single query shape. The mechanism is the real claim. Prometheus' engine's peak memory grows with the number of selected series times the number of time steps, while MQE holds roughly one input series plus the output series at its peak. The post says MQE became the default in 2.17, that "we are not removing support for the Prometheus query engine", and that the team checked correctness by running both engines over PromQL test scripts and comparing live queries in staging with query-tee (Grafana).

Seven weeks later, on 3 November 2025, Grafana released Mimir 3.0, describing MQE as "100% PromQL-compatible and is the default query engine in Mimir 3.0." The marketing figure had become "reduces peak memory usage by up to 92%" (Grafana). That is a quiet widening: one benchmark's result became the upper bound for every workload. The 3.0 post claims a separate "up to 15% lower resource usage", which applies only to large clusters in Grafana's internal environment and is credited to 3.0's optimisations in general. It also introduces ingest storage, a Kafka-style buffer that decouples reads from writes, and gives no figure for the lower read-path outage risk it claims. Upgrading requires running a second Mimir cluster alongside the classic one, and Grafana says 3.0 took three years to build (Grafana).

Search results indicate the Prometheus engine still ships as an opt-out and that later release candidates added dashboard panels tracking fallback rates. Those release notes were not opened, so treat that as unconfirmed. No independent third-party benchmark of MQE memory use was found.

Why it matters now. The claim aged well as engineering. MQE went from new default to the foundation of a major version within two months and was not rolled back. As a number it aged the way vendor benchmarks usually do: "92% in some tests" became "up to 92%." Two lessons carry over to today's lead. First, streaming beats materialising, whether the stream is time-series samples or an agent's history. Nvidia's report found that most of the long-input benefit came from deciding what to read rather than loading everything source. Second, when a headline efficiency number comes from one query shape, ask for the distribution, as with the compaction report's 233.3x best-case estimate (arXiv).

Ways of thinking

Hamming's question, applied to context windows and capital

2 sources, 2 min read

Shane Parrish's Brain Food this week quotes Richard Hamming: "If you do not work on an important problem, it's unlikely you'll do important work." It pairs that with Charlie Munger on ruin ("There are only three ways a smart person can go broke: liquor, ladies and leverage") and Carl Jung on the "unlived life" source. Hamming's line is usually read as career advice. Today's news suggests a narrower version that is easier to act on: at every step, decide what deserves your working memory.

Nvidia's compaction report is, at bottom, an empirical test of a Hamming-style filter on an agent. The authors found that conventional summaries "recount prominent events" whether or not they still matter. The newsletter describes a good compaction as a briefing that preserves only what the next step requires: objective, established facts, failed approaches, next action source. In other words, history that was important when it happened is not necessarily important now. The agent that carries its whole past forward pays a tax on every token. On GPT's SWE-bench Verified runs, one rough run reached 173K tokens of context where a disciplined one needed 35K (arXiv HTML). Hamming's practice, as he described it in talks not checked for this issue, was to ask regularly what the important problems in his field were and why he was not working on them. The compaction prompt asks the same kind of question at each step: what still matters, and what is the next step.

Munger's third L fits the SoftBank story without needing the other two. SoftBank booked more than 13GW of turbines out of fear of a shortage, and its interest bill for six months was ¥676bn, more than double the prior year (TNW). The point is not that SoftBank will go broke; its loan-to-value ratio was 15.9% against a 25% cap. The point is that leverage turns a forecasting error, ordering for a shortage that did not come, into a bill that grows whether or not the forecast was right. The same applies to capacity planning in AI infrastructure. Reserving for the most context-hungry workload you can imagine is a leveraged bet on that workload.

The useful habit to take away is to keep a short, explicit "state of the problem" document for any long project, and to rewrite it rather than append to it. Rewriting forces the Hamming question; appending avoids it. The Nvidia data suggests that, for agents at least, the rewritten brief costs little accuracy. Whether it works for people has not been tested source +1.

"Generative Universal Verifier as Multimodal Meta-Reasoner": OmniVerifier (October 2025)

1 source, 2 min read

Why this is here: exploring inference infrastructure, kernels, and model tooling.

A Hugging Face daily-papers entry with 22 upvotes huggingface. The paper proposes a plug-in generative verifier that lets vision-language and unified multimodal models check and refine visual outputs during reasoning and generation. It introduces ViVerBench, trains OmniVerifier-7B, and uses it for sequential test-time scaling of image generation (arXiv).

Paper facts. arXiv 2510.13804, v1 only, 15 October 2025. Authors: Xinchen Zhang, Xiaoying Zhang, Youbin Wu, Yanbin Cao, Renrui Zhang, Ruihang Chu, Ling Yang and Yujiu Yang. No venue is listed. ViVerBench has 3,594 samples across 16 subtasks in 6 categories, annotated by 12 domain experts with balanced true and false labels. Humans score 0.932 on the rule-based metric. The best model there is Gemini 2.5 Pro at 0.745, and GPT-4o scores 0.645. On the Maze subtask, humans score 0.997 against 0.580 for Gemini. The headline "+8.3" for OmniVerifier-7B is its base model Qwen2.5-VL-7B going from 0.570 to 0.653 on the rule-based metric. On the model-based metric OmniVerifier-7B scores 0.559, below GPT-4o's 0.578, a detail the abstract's "first omni-capable generative verifier" framing does not mention.

For test-time scaling with up to 10 refinement steps, sequential OmniVerifier-TTS on Qwen-Image moved T2I-ReasonBench from 55.5 to 59.2 (+3.7) and GenEval++ from 0.675 to 0.718. Best-of-N with N=10 scored 58.1 and 0.693. On GPT-Image-1 the gains were 76.8 to 79.3 and 0.689 to 0.721, against Best-of-N at 78.1 and 0.700. The text claims sequential wins "across all three benchmarks", but only two generation benchmarks appear. The limitations section fell outside the portion read. A project page is linked; a code or weights release is not stated in the text read (arXiv HTML).

Does it hold up. The benchmark is the stronger contribution. A roughly 19-point gap between humans and the best model on simple visual verification (0.932 against 0.745) is a clear, reusable finding. The verifier itself mostly closes the gap to GPT-4o on one metric, not to humans. The test-time scaling gains over Best-of-N are 1.1 to 2.5 points, under a matched budget of 10. That is consistent with a general pattern: sequential refinement with a critic beats independent samples when the critic is good enough. For an inference-infrastructure reader, sequential refinement is latency-serial and cache-friendly, while Best-of-N parallelises across GPUs but cannot reuse a prefix the same way. That trade-off is the same one the compaction report runs into with prompt caching. No follow-up adoption was checked for this issue huggingface.
