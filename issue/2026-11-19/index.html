OpenAI ships GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol: cheaper per task, rated High for self-improvement, and a fight over coding

3 sources, 5 min read

What happened. OpenAI released two models on November 17: GPT-6.2 Astra, its frontier model for long, hard projects, and GPT-6.2 Sol, the cheaper everyday model. AlphaSignal's summary leads on cost. It says Astra costs less than a third as much per task as GPT-6 Astra, that Astra's API tokens are 20% cheaper at $8 input and $40 output per million, and that Sol keeps its $2 input and $10 output pricing while approaching Astra's coding performance at about a fifth of the cost alphasignal. Both models are available to Plus, Pro, Enterprise and Edu users in ChatGPT Work and Codex, and in the API as gpt-6.2-astra and gpt-6.2-sol alphasignal. OpenAI's own developer-forum post returned an HTTP 429 when fetched and was not read. The details below come from AlphaSignal and from TestingCatalog's write-up (TestingCatalog), which is dated November 17 and quotes OpenAI's materials.

Details. TestingCatalog gives the full API price card. Astra costs $8 input, $0.40 cached input and $40 output per million tokens. That is 20% below GPT-6 Astra, and cached input falls from $1.00 (TestingCatalog). Sol stays at $2, $0.10 and $10, the same as GPT-6.1 Sol. Ultrafast mode reaches Pro 200 subscribers for the first time, starting with Sol, and is available on both models for Pro 500 and the API. The benchmark claims all come from OpenAI or its launch partners: - Agents' Last Exam: Astra is 5.2 points above GPT-6 Astra at under a third of the cost, and 5.5 points above Claude Fable 5.2 at 5% of the cost. - Terminal-Bench-Science 0.1: Astra beats GPT-6 Astra by 9 percentage points. - Harvey's LAB-AA legal benchmark: Astra more than doubles Fable 5.2's pass rate. - AA-3D: Astra ranks first at 1,318 Elo, against 1,271 for Fable 5.2 and 1,259 for Sol. - Cognition's FrontierCode 2.0: Sol is within 0.5 points of Astra at roughly a fifth of the cost. - LifeVal: Sol outscores GPT-6 Astra at roughly a seventh of the cost.

On ARC-AGI-3, TestingCatalog reports Astra at 100.0% for $4.1K using the provider adapter and 70.3% for $24.2K under the standard setting. It quotes ARC Prize calling it "the first model to achieve a 100% score on ARC-AGI-3." It also lists 95.8% on ARC-AGI-2 at $0.50 per task and 98.5% on ARC-AGI-1 at $0.17 per task. On safety, TestingCatalog reports that both models are rated Critical for cybersecurity and High for biological and chemical risk under OpenAI's Preparedness Framework. It quotes Astra as OpenAI's "first externally released model to reach the High threshold for AI self-improvement." It notes that the tighter cyber safeguards "restrict some legitimate security research," and it describes a new "Control Room" system that can stop model output on research workloads when intervention is needed (TestingCatalog). AlphaSignal adds that Astra jumped 22 points on cybersecurity benchmarks alphasignal. Facts vs. opinions. The established facts are the prices, the model IDs, where the models are available and the Preparedness ratings; OpenAI's own materials and two write-ups agree on all of them. Nearly every capability number is vendor-run or partner-run, and many are framed as cost per task, which mixes price, the number of tokens a model uses and the harness it runs in. The curator flagged a coding caveat, and the evidence for it is thin and indirect. TestingCatalog explicitly does not report any coding gain for Astra over GPT-6 Astra. It says Astra "writes cleaner code" and gives only Sol's FrontierCode figure (TestingCatalog). On X, one user posted Artificial Analysis Coding Agent Index figures of 53 for Astra and 49–50 for Sol. Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton told CNBC: "This is not cyberattack, and this is not a coordinated effort by a nation state." TLDR's summary adds that security researchers say AI is speeding up vulnerability discovery, and that companies are struggling to ship the resulting patches without breaking services cnbc.

Details. Microsoft's explanation is the most complete. Facts vs. opinions. What is established: each organisation gave a cause that was internal, not an attack. Java4Shell: a heap overflow in OpenJDK's AVX-512 Base64 path, found with AI in an hour

2 sources, 4 min read

What happened. On November 17 the security firm Calif disclosed CVE-2026-114938, which it calls Java4Shell. It is a memory-safety bug in OpenJDK's Base64 decoder, in hand-written assembly that speeds up decoding on x86-64 CPUs with AVX-512 VBMI instructions calif. Calif's own write-up could not be read: the fetch was refused by an automated content filter. The details below come from SecurityWeek's November 18 report by Eduard Kovacs (SecurityWeek) and from the TLDR summary. Malformed Base64 input can write past the output buffer and corrupt adjacent heap data. SecurityWeek quotes Calif: "With careful exploitation, the corruption can reveal sensitive memory, as Heartbleed did…" Calif calls that memory-disclosure capability "JavaBleed."

Details. The two scores differ, and both are worth knowing: - Calif: 9.2 under CVSS 4.0. - Oracle's advisory: 8.1 under CVSS 3.1. Oracle describes the flaw as remotely exploitable without authentication but with high attack complexity.

Calif says OpenJDK 17.0.6 and later are affected, along with downstream builds: Amazon Corretto, Eclipse Temurin, Microsoft Build of OpenJDK, Azul Zulu, Red Hat OpenJDK and Oracle Java SE. The OpenJDK advisory lists Java 17, 21, 25 and 27. The bug needs AVX-512 VBMI, which means Intel Xeon from Ice Lake onward or AMD Zen 4 and newer. ARM64 systems are not affected (SecurityWeek). In Calif's tests: - Pre-authentication remote code execution with default settings: Keycloak, Jira, Confluence, JFrog Artifactory, Jenkins and PingFederate. - Authenticated remote code execution: Kafka Broker, Solr, Elasticsearch, Gerrit and Spinnaker.

That puts Elasticsearch in the authenticated group, while the TLDR summary lists it alongside the default-configuration products without the distinction calif. Oracle fixed the flaw in its November 2026 Critical Patch Update, which covers 11 Java SE flaws. Endor Labs said that as of November 17 the fix was not yet in the public OpenJDK 17, 21 and 25 update repositories, so users of other distributions must wait for vendor builds (SecurityWeek). Search results point to JVM flags that disable the Base64 intrinsic or AVX-512 as stopgaps. Those were not checked against an advisory, since Calif's page could not be read, so confirm them with your vendor before relying on them.

Facts vs. opinions. Calif says AI was central to both discovery and exploitation. SecurityWeek quotes it: "It took our own Quang Luong an hour to discover the vulnerability," then two more hours to build an exploit, and "The total token cost was less than $200." These are the discoverer's own claims and were not independently checked. Exploitability is genuinely disputed: - Calif: "there is no universal exploit." - VulnCheck: crashing the JVM is easy, but code execution needs target-specific heap grooming and an ASLR bypass. "we don't anticipate that CVE-2026-114938 will be easily or widely exploitable." - Cisco Talos: has seen no exploitation in the wild, but "we anticipate probing attempts may pick up soon after public disclosure."

The public proof of concept shows memory corruption only. Calif says "The weaponized chains for individual products stay private until everyone has had a chance to upgrade." Calif went public after news of the flaw surfaced ahead of coordinated disclosure; SecurityWeek does not say how (SecurityWeek).

Other perspectives. CISA's advice is to patch immediately, starting with internet-exposed systems, and to hunt for Java bundled inside third-party software, containers and appliances that may not appear in asset inventories (SecurityWeek). That last point is where most of the work will be. A JDK inside a vendor appliance is patched on the vendor's schedule, not yours. A useful structural observation is that the bug sits in a performance intrinsic: hand-written vector assembly added so a hot path runs faster on newer CPUs. Memory-safe languages still lean on unsafe code at the hardware boundary, and those layers get far less review than the Java around them. Oracle's 8.1 and Calif's 9.2 use different CVSS versions (3.1 and 4.0), but Oracle's high attack-complexity rating and VulnCheck's note that RCE needs target-specific heap manipulation point to the same lesson: severity depends on how hard the heap is to shape in each product calif.

Why it matters. For an infrastructure-minded reader, this is the concrete version of the outage week's story. A widely deployed runtime has a bug that, by its discoverer's account, took an hour and under $200 of AI tokens to find. The patch is out from Oracle but lagged in some OpenJDK repositories. Hardware decides exposure: the same Jira is vulnerable on Zen 4 and not on ARM64. The defensive lessons: - Inventory JVMs by CPU flags as well as by version. - Treat AVX-512 VBMI hosts as the exposed set. - Expect this kind of discovery, in optimised native code under managed runtimes, to keep coming as AI-assisted auditing gets cheaper.

OpenAI's Critical cyber rating for GPT-6.2 in today's first lead belongs in the same picture calif +1.

Claude Memory: A Different Philosophy (Shlok Khemani, September 11, 2025)

3 sources, 3 min read

What it said. Shlok Khemani's essay contrasted the two leading assistants' memory designs as they stood in September 2025. Claude "starts every conversation with a blank slate, without any preloaded user profiles or conversation history," and "Memory only activates when you explicitly invoke it" (Shlok Khemani). Claude's recall ran on two visible tools. conversation_search handled keyword and topic searches across all past chats, and recent_chats gave access by time. "There are no AI-generated summaries or compressed profiles—just real-time searches through your actual past chats." ChatGPT, by contrast, loaded every memory component automatically, "creating instant personalization with zero wait time," and built "detailed user profiles." Khemani's conclusion: the two systems were "completely opposite," and "memory in AI has a massive design space with no right answer or one-size-fits-all technique." He tied the difference to audience, with ChatGPT's mass market against Claude's technical users who, he wrote, "are comfortable with explicit control at every level" shloked.

How it aged. The opposition did not last, and Khemani himself flagged the first move. A later update on the same page notes that Anthropic launched a memory feature for Team and Enterprise plans that "looks much closer to ChatGPT's approach" (Shlok Khemani). By March 2, 2026, Android Headlines reported that Memory had come to Claude's free users, after being available only to paid subscribers. Third-party connectors such as Slack and Figma, also previously premium, came to free users at the same time. Anthropic shipped an import tool alongside: a prompt users paste into their current chatbot, which asks it to output stored memories, preferences and personal details in one code block, telling it not to "summarize, group, or omit any entries" (Android Headlines). The article reports Claude reaching first place on the US App Store free chart and Anthropic citing 60% growth in free users since the start of that year. The writer frames the move as lowering "switching costs." Google announced a similar change for Gemini a few hours earlier. Third-party guides found in search say Anthropic rebuilt Claude's memory again in August 2026 as a list of individually editable entries, close to ChatGPT's model. That was not checked against Anthropic's own release notes, and it is reported here only as a lead shloked.

What survives of Khemani's argument is the design space, not the opposition. Claude went from retrieval on demand toward ChatGPT's profile model, apparently to compete on mainstream personalisation and to win switchers, which runs against his claim that the Claude audience wanted explicit control. The import prompt also shows something about the state of the field. Memory became portable through a copy-paste prompt rather than an export standard.
