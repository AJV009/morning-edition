Anthropic lists on Nasdaq: a record raise, a 34% pop, and a day-two fade

1 source, 6 min read

What happened. Anthropic began trading on the Nasdaq under the ticker ANT on Thursday, November 19. According to Morning Brew, shares reached brokerages around noon at $111, which was 34% above the $83 price set the day before for a pool of preferred investors, and settled at $97 by the end of the day morningbrew. CNBC puts the first trade at $111.50 and the first-day close at $97.17, up 17%. It says that close gave Anthropic a market capitalisation of about $1.8 trillion, making it the seventh-most valuable U.S. company. On Friday the stock closed at $88.30, which CNBC describes as a few dollars above the IPO price (CNBC). Morning Brew calls the raise a record $81 billion, beating the $75 billion record SpaceX set in June, and says Anthropic also sold $26 billion in private shares morningbrew. CNBC gives the IPO raise as $81.3 billion, or $93.5 billion once shares sold to underwriters are included, against $85.7 billion for SpaceX on the same basis. It describes the $26 billion as a parallel share sale to tech companies (CNBC).

Details. The S-1 itself could not be read. The SEC file is larger than the fetch tool's 10 MB limit, so the filing figures below come from a secondary breakdown by Leverage Shares, dated October 30. Leverage Shares issues leveraged ETFs on Anthropic, so it has a commercial interest, and it notes the figures have not been independently verified. By its account, the public S-1 was filed on October 26, 2026, after a confidential draft on June 1. Revenue for the six months to June 30, 2026 was $16,286 million, against $1,163 million in the same half of 2025 and $4,585 million for the whole of 2025. The first-half 2026 operating loss was $693 million, down from $2,135 million a year earlier. Non-GAAP adjusted operating income was $1,618 million, against an adjusted loss of $1,849 million a year earlier. The net loss was $80,748 million, but $72,388 million of it was a non-cash change in the fair value of convertible notes, derivative liabilities and SAFEs. Net revenue retention was about 900% as of September 30, 2026. At June 30 the company held $112.4 billion in cash and cash equivalents plus $19.3 billion in short-term investments. Leverage Shares also reports that one Class F share carries a fixed 50.1% of the vote, and that the S-1 summary leaves blank the total committed spend on compute and infrastructure, as well as third-quarter revenue. That quarter's figure, $16.8 billion, comes from FT and Bloomberg reports on October 14, not from the filing (Leverage Shares). CNBC adds that Anthropic had more than $130 billion in cash and equivalents at the end of September, that it has raised more than $140 billion in its history and spent only about $10 billion of it, and it cites year-over-year growth of 1,200% (CNBC).

The market backdrop was poor, and CNBC spends much of its piece on it. Nvidia shares had fallen for a seventh straight week, losing 46% over that stretch. The VIX was up 46% for the month, including a 16% jump on Thursday to above 28.4. CNBC notes that most companies would consider shelving an IPO with the VIX above 25. Its list of headwinds includes Jensen Huang's exit from Nvidia, a semiconductor sell-off, voters rejecting AI in the midterms, rising rates, a planned Fed hike, opposition to data centres, and other IPOs (Oura, Firmus, Solidigm) postponed or pulled (CNBC). Morning Brew says the tech-heavy Nasdaq closed down 2.2% on debut day. It attributes that to "some analysts" pointing at investors cashing out IPO gains and at renewed chipmaker anxiety, and says the IPO "didn't propel other parts of the market" morningbrew.

The cap table and wealth effects come with heavy hedging. Morning Brew cites the investment platform Hill.com as estimating that there are "likely 2,800" Anthropic employees with shares worth $10 million or more, and 600 with stakes above $100 million. It says early backers include Lightspeed, Iconiq, Menlo Ventures and a VC firm co-founded by Ashton Kutcher that owns 0.5% morningbrew. On demand, CNBC reports that Altimeter placed a $5 billion order, five times its largest previous IPO investment, while also cutting its broader AI exposure to its lowest level in three years (CNBC).

Facts vs. opinions. The trading prices, the size of the raise and the dates are established by multiple reports. The two outlets differ only in rounding: $111 against $111.50 for the first trade, and $81 billion against $81.3 billion for the raise morningbrew(CNBC). The S-1 line items are only as good as the secondary reading of them used here. The interim figures are unaudited, and the issue could not check them against the filing itself (Leverage Shares). Morning Brew's phrase about Anthropic needing to grow revenue fast enough "to justify spending $900 billion on AI infrastructure" has no source in the piece morningbrew. Leverage Shares says the S-1 summary leaves the compute commitment total blank, so the $900 billion figure is not checked here and should be treated as a press number (Leverage Shares). The cash figures do not conflict: Leverage Shares' $112.4 billion plus $19.3 billion is dated June 30, and CNBC's "more than $130 billion" is dated end of September. The Hill.com employee-wealth numbers are estimates from a single platform. The rest is opinion. Bessemer's Byron Deeter says Anthropic "could have priced the IPO higher." Altimeter's Brad Gerstner calls the conditions "a very bad backdrop." Iconiq's Griffith calls the deal "truly, truly market redefining." Dario Amodei describes his pricing philosophy as "underpromise and overdeliver" (CNBC).

That announcement was not read for this issue, so the counts are not checked.

The filing as read shows a company whose operating loss shrank sharply while revenue grew roughly 14-fold half on half. That multiple is Leverage Shares' framing; $16,286 million against $1,163 million is about 14.0x. The same filing leaves the compute commitment total out of the summary, and compute commitments are the number that decides whether prices and rate limits move (Leverage Shares). The 50.1% Class F vote and the PBC charter mean public shareholders cannot force a change of direction, which cuts both ways for customers worried about squeezed margins. More than $130 billion in cash buys a long runway in a market that, per CNBC, had already pulled several other IPOs (CNBC). The day-two fade back towards the offer price is the number to watch, not the day-one pop morningbrew.

Stripe measures coding agents on real integrations: most criteria met, few runs fully right

2 sources, 4 min read

What happened. Stripe published the methodology and results of an eval suite that measures how well coding agents build Stripe integrations from realistic, well-specified scenarios. In the summary circulated by TLDR, agents met 78.1% of required criteria on average, but only 29.4% of runs met every required criterion, and all 14 agent setups scored higher with Stripe's plugin stripe. The post is dated November 19, 2026 and bylined to Kiran Prasad, Yiing Chau Mak and Manish Kurse (Stripe).

Details. The suite has 69 public scenarios: 24 one-time payments, 26 subscriptions and 19 Connect (marketplace) cases. Examples include hosted checkout, metered billing, per-seat subscriptions and marketplace payment splits. It covers 14 model and harness pairs across three harnesses (Claude Code, Codex and OpenCode), each run with and without the Stripe plugin. The plugin gives agents Stripe-authored skills and MCP tools such as an implementation planner and a documentation search. Stripe's arithmetic is 69 cases times 2 repetitions times 2 arms, or 276 runs per coding agent. A secondary agent role-plays the developer. It typically holds back two to four hidden requirements, up to a maximum of six, and stops when they are met or a turn limit is reached. Each criterion is graded separately by an LLM judge using five-candidate verbalised sampling. Criteria are labelled MUST or SHOULD, and one passes only when the transcript or workspace provides the required evidence. Stripe used "GPT 6.1 Sol for both the roleplaying model and the judge model across all evaluations." A bias probe using Opus as judge found that "the scores moved in lockstep," with Opus a uniformly more lenient grader. Each case has a 45-minute timeout that counts as an error, and "Errors are not included in the score calculation" (Stripe).

Results, going from no plugin to plugin. GPT-6 Astra on Codex went from 85.5 to 90.0 average score, with strict pass rising from 41.9 to 52.9, at $2.93 to $3.63 per run. GPT-6.1 Sol on Codex went from 85.6 to 89.6, strict 39.0 to 50.7, at $0.51 to $0.61. Opus 5.5 on Claude Code went from 83.5 to 86.2, strict 35.3 to 36.0, at $2.77 to $3.15. Fable 5.2 on Claude Code went from 81.1 to 85.2, with the largest strict jump among the top tier, 28.4 to 43.2. Haiku 5.5 went from 73.5 to 76.2 at $0.08 to $0.09 a run. Gemini 3.8 Flash on OpenCode was lowest, 64.8 to 72.7 and strict 11.2 to 21.6. Stripe says all 14 agents "scored higher with it, by roughly 3 to 8 percentage points." The plugin cut outdated API parameters from 7% to 1.3% of parameters used. Pooled web searches per eval fell from 1.26 to 0.05 and page fetches from 4.84 to 1.78, while total tool calls stayed roughly flat (59.4 to 61.4). Agents with the plugin wrote about 40% fewer test lines and ran tests in 42% of evals, against 53% without it. On categories, agents that scored highest on one-time payments and subscriptions were not the best on Connect, which also showed the largest plugin gains. For example, Fable 5.2 went from 89 to 97 and GPT-6 Astra from 85 to 92 on Connect (Stripe).

Facts vs. opinions. The numbers come from a vendor eval of the vendor's own plugin, judged by one model family. The table is useful but not independent. Stripe's own limitations section is unusually frank: "A well-shaped but non-functional integration can score full marks." "The benchmark asserts correct specification, not correct execution." It does not assess whether the agent recommended the right product, or how the code ages as the API changes, and the strict pass rate "is sensitive to any data or judgement errors" (Stripe). Two further cautions follow from the design. First, the judge and role-player are an OpenAI model, and the two top scorers are OpenAI models on OpenAI's harness. The Opus probe argues against judge bias, but the post shows only that the scores moved together, not the per-agent rankings under the Opus judge. Second, timeouts are excluded rather than counted as failures, so slower agents that hit the 45-minute limit are not penalised in the averages. The page does not say how many runs were excluded. Stripe's explanation for the gap between average and strict scores is an interpretation: "many of the models create almost correct implementations and have difficulty with the last mile."

Other perspectives. The plugin's mechanism is visible in the tool-use table. It replaces web search and documentation scraping with vendor-authored context, and Stripe reports larger gains in Connect scenarios that involve more scenario-specific integration decisions. This is the same pattern Chrome reports today for web development: agents pass 52% of its checks unaided and 91% with its Modern Web Guidance installed chrome. Vendor guidance packaged as skills lifts pass rates. Neither result shows that the resulting code runs in production. The finding that tests ran in fewer evals with the plugin (42% against 53%) cuts against the headline. Better priors appear to reduce self-verification (Stripe).

Why it matters. For anyone building on payment APIs with agents, the useful number is the strict pass rate. Even the best configuration in the table, GPT-6 Astra with the plugin, met every MUST criterion in 52.9% of runs. Roughly half of those integrations would need a human to catch a missed requirement, and the benchmark does not execute the code at all (Stripe). The design is worth copying for internal evals: hidden requirements surfaced by a role-played user, per-criterion judging, and both an average score and an all-or-nothing score. It is also an example of a vendor shipping skills and an MCP server as its official integration surface, which links to the archive's Skills item below stripe.

OpenAI's Performance Factory: an investigator, an adversarial reviewer and a human merge button

1 source, 4 min read

What happened. OpenAI's engineering blog describes the Performance Factory, which it calls "an autonomous system that uses the full capabilities of Codex and our models to find and fix regressions" in ChatGPT, from the first alert to confirmed recovery. When ChatGPT slows down, one Codex session investigates and a second tries to rule out its explanation before a fix is proposed. Engineers decide what ships, and Codex checks after deployment whether performance has returned to baseline openai. The post is dated November 19, 2026, bylined to Members of Technical Staff Okay Zed and Cyrus Forbes, and acknowledges contributions from Brendan Gregg and others (OpenAI).

Details. The system grew in stages. Engineers first investigated by hand and accumulated more than 30 performance tools. Codex then became an assistant. The team captured its playbook as a reusable skill called perf-forensics, evaluated against past regressions. At one point engineers were acting as "human proxies," pasting alerts into Codex and copying results back to Slack. Alerts then started cloud sessions automatically, and finally workflows became end-to-end with persistent state. The architecture has four parts. A task service lets engineers define workflows as config files and Markdown prompts, specifying states, allowed transitions and which steps need a human. A dispatch loop starts a Codex turn for each ready task with instructions for its current state. Codex runs in the cloud on an internal API, with access to repositories, metrics, logs, traces, profiles and memory snapshots, under a service identity with scoped permissions. Subscriptions wake waiting tasks on events such as PR comments, failed CI or completed deployments (OpenAI).

In the regression workflow, the system detects, an agent finds the root cause, an agent proposes a fix, an engineer reviews and deploys, and an agent verifies recovery. The investigator finds when the metric first worsened, compares cohorts of requests, and checks deployments, feature flags and code. The reviewer is "a separate Codex session that tries to disprove the proposed diagnosis," testing counterfactuals, and fixes get the same adversarial review. After deployment, full recovery resolves the task. Partial recovery resolves it and opens a new task for the remaining slowdown. No improvement sends it back to investigation. A daily self-repair agent scans recent runs for tool failures and access errors. In the example from the summary, an agent attributed failing conversations to image-download errors. The reviewer noticed that the service blamed for locating files had already recovered while image requests kept failing, reopened the investigation and recorded what remained unexplained (OpenAI).

Facts vs. opinions. OpenAI gives two outcome numbers. In "just over three months" the system "tracked more than 1,700 investigations and root caused over 600 cases." In a manual review of 100 root-cause suggestions, 70% were graded accurate, and the remaining 30% needed refinement or did not fully establish causality. The post gives no latency improvements, no time-saved estimates, and no comparison with the pre-factory process, so its effect on ChatGPT's actual speed is not established by the post itself (OpenAI). Evaluation is by backtesting against past incidents with answer-revealing details removed. Agents cannot see memories, incident writeups or prior runs, and transcripts are checked for spoilers. The authors acknowledge this is fragile because telemetry expires and post-recovery evidence can leak the cause. The stated limitations are worth quoting: "Getting fixes right has been harder than finding the cause." Proposed fixes were sometimes "more complicated than necessary or introduce new issues." Early agents stopped at the first plausible explanation. Broken tools or missing permissions can quietly block investigations. And "Today, the bottleneck may be waiting for an engineer to review and merge the fix."

Other perspectives. The design matches what Cognition's Walden Yan concluded in April, ten months after writing "Don't Build Multi-Agents": multi-agent systems work best "when writes stay single-threaded" and "the additional agents contribute intelligence rather than actions" (Cognition). OpenAI's reviewer writes nothing. It only tries to falsify, and the single write path, the PR, is merged by a human. The 70% accuracy figure is also a reminder that a disproof-seeking second agent does not make the first one right. On that sample, three in ten diagnoses still needed work after review (OpenAI). The next steps OpenAI lists include exploring how agents could eventually ship low-risk fixes without a human in the loop. That is a plan, not something it has done, and it would remove the one human gate described.

Why it matters. For anyone running agents against production telemetry, the post is a detailed reference architecture: states and transitions in config, event subscriptions instead of polling agents, scoped service identities, a self-repair loop for the agent's own tooling, and verification after deploy. The parts that transfer most directly are the adversarial reviewer and the "partial recovery opens a new task" rule. The part to be sceptical of is the absence of any before-and-after performance data openai(OpenAI).

Replit's swarm benchmark: 32 Opus agents reach 18.9x on NumPy, and the best solo model makes a poor team

2 sources, 4 min read

What happened. Replit's Frontier blog published "Throwing Headcount at the Problem," which tests whether teams of coding agents beat a single agent on performance-optimisation tasks. The TLDR summary reports that 32 Opus agents achieved an 18.9x NumPy workload speedup against 3.7x for one agent within two hours, using more compute. Gains depended on separable tasks and shared coordination boards, and the strongest solo model's performance declined as teams grew replit. The post is dated November 19, 2026, with nine authors including Michele Catasta (Replit).

Details. The benchmark, Optimization-Bench, is a prerelease covering 14 libraries, four of which are reported. NumPy has 13 workloads (quantiles, sorting, datetime, random numbers). SQLite has 12 (full-text search, indexing, JSON, joins). SQLGlot has 4, all through one parser and generator. Git has 7, all in a 15-file diff library. Each agent got 8 vCPUs, 16 GiB RAM, standard profilers and a two-hour window. Team sizes were 1, 8, 16 and 32. The models were Claude Opus 5.5, Claude Sonnet 5.5, GPT-6.2 Astra, GPT-6 Astra, GPT-6.1 Sol and GPT-6 Luna, all at medium reasoning effort except Luna at high. The rules forbade caching across calls, special-casing inputs, precomputation, outsourcing to external libraries and process-wide runtime changes. Network access was off and runs were audited afterwards. The frozen branch is evaluated on a separate machine the agents cannot reach, against repository tests plus hidden generated tests, and original and candidate are timed in alternating random order on a GCP c4-standard-32 with benchmarks pinned to cores 0 to 7. The authors cite reward-hacking precedents: METR catching models overwriting timers, and Sakana's reported 10 to 100x CUDA speedups that came from a sandbox flaw. Each agent is a full Replit Agent in its own microVM. Agents share a repository but not a filesystem, and coordinate through a swarm board, direct messages and readable teammate transcripts. The coordinator received no instructions on how to organise the team (Replit).

The chart labels give median speedups that appear to be 32-agent endpoints, though the page does not say so explicitly. Opus 5.5 reached 18.92x on NumPy, 1.52x on SQLite, 1.3822x on SQLGlot and 1.287x on Git. Sonnet 5.5 reached 8.63x on NumPy. GPT-6.2 Astra reached 6.53x on NumPy and 1.289x on Git. GPT-6.1 Sol came in at 0.9869x on SQLGlot, which is slower than the original. Opus went from 3.66x solo to 18.92x with 32 agents on NumPy. The TLDR summary's "3.7x" is a rounding of this figure. Opus and Sonnet swarms beat their solo runs every time, by a median of 1.30x at 16 and 32 agents. One concrete optimisation: the Opus swarm changed NumPy's searchsorted to process 256 keys at a time and skip the NaN-safe comparison when no NaNs are present, giving 8.7 to 13.1x speedups on float64 with many keys and identical output (Replit).

The coordination data is the most interesting part. Opus put 82% of its messages on the shared board, while GPT-6.2 Astra put under 40% there. Workers sent the coordinator about 22 direct messages each under GPT-6 Astra, about 20 under Sol, about 15 under GPT-6.2 Astra, and 1.4 under Opus. In the 32-agent Opus NumPy run, the coordinator never read the board or messaged anyone. Going from 16 to 32 agents, Opus direct messages per agent fell from 4.9 to 1.0 on NumPy. Sol's coordinator inbox grew from 356 to 631 on SQLGlot. GPT-6.2 Astra's inbox grew on all four tasks, about 4x on SQLite, and its scores fell on all four. GPT-6.2 Astra had the strongest solo runs on all four tasks, including more than 3x Opus solo on NumPy, but its swarms peaked at 8 agents. At 32 they fell below its solo score on NumPy, SQLGlot and Git. Luna coordinators misbehaved in several ways: one polled the board more than 200 times, another did the work itself, and some told workers to idle (Replit).

Facts vs. opinions. The speedups are Replit's own measurements on its own benchmark and infrastructure. The evaluation design (hidden tests, separate timing machine, audits) is stronger than most agent benchmarks, but the page does not state run counts or variance, and only 4 of 14 libraries are reported. "Using more compute" is understated. A 32-agent team gets 32 times the agent-hours of a solo run in the same two-hour wall clock, so the result compares equal deadlines, not equal budgets (Replit). The authors' conclusions are explicitly interpretive: "The best swarms sent few direct messages; workers claimed tasks and shared results on a common board." "The coordinator became the bottleneck, and doubling its load barely moved scores." "A model's solo score was a poor predictor of how its swarm performed." They say this aligns with Cursor's separation of planners from workers and Cognition's single-threaded writes, while cautioning that "models differ a great deal in how they organize on their own."

Other perspectives. The Git result is the counterweight. Every Git workload shares the same 15 files, Anthropic swarms struggled to beat their solo runs there, and GPT-6.2 Astra matched or beat Opus at every team size. "Highly constrained tasks often led extra agents to compete for the same code" (Replit). That fits the June 2025 Anthropic research-system post revisited below, which warned that "most coding tasks involve fewer truly parallelizable tasks than research" (Anthropic). Together AI's compiler post reports a similar finding independently. In its ablation, frequent agent communication gave 7.72x, infrequent 6.20x and none 4.82x on one MoE layer (Together AI). A search of X for builder reactions to the Replit post returned nothing as of this morning.

Why it matters. The practical lesson is about topology, not headcount. Durable, readable shared state (a board with claimed work items, such as an agent posting that it is "touching btree.c") scaled. Hub-and-spoke messaging through a coordinator did not, and that held regardless of how strong the model was solo. For anyone designing agent orchestration, the finding that model choice changes emergent team behaviour means a harness tuned for one model's coordination habits may not transfer. That echoes today's quick hit on harness swapping replit +1.

Together AI deletes its megakernels: agents rewriting source beat hand-built fusion by about 28% on B200

2 sources, 4 min read

What happened. Together AI's research team wrote up why it abandoned the hand-written megakernels it built last year and replaced them with Together Compiler, a system in which AI agents rewrite and fuse GPU kernels. The TLDR summary gives the headline: about 28% faster than its hand-written megakernels on B200, 2,118 against 1,649 tokens per second on Llama 3.2 1B. It also notes that CUDA Graphs on default Inductor kernels already recovered about 98% of megakernel performance together. The post is dated November 16, 2026 and bylined to Dylan Lim, Reyna Abhyankar and Simran Arora (Together AI).

Details. On H100 decode for Llama 3.2 1B, the authors report Inductor at 669 TPS. Adding CUDA Graphs gave 864 (87% of the megakernel), coarse programmatic dependent launch (PDL) gave 953 (96%), and fine-grained PDL gave 975 (98%), against 990 for the hand-written megakernel. The megakernel's memory preloading was worth only about 3.3%: 961 TPS without it. In their words, they "could recover 98% of megakernel performance without writing a single kernel." PDL needed manual work, because Triton did not expose the controls and griddepcontrol instructions had to be inserted directly into PTX. A launch-cost table shows why. On H100, a plain cudaLaunchKernelEx costs 2077 ns, a CUDA Graph 1051 ns, and CUDA Graph plus PDL 645 ns, against 646 ns for a megakernel's internal grid sync. The authors' conclusion is that a megakernel "just trades launch overhead for similar synchronization costs inside the kernel" (Together AI).

The post explains what was wrong with megakernels in detail. They force one launch envelope and a fixed warp count. The megakernel used 20 warps where Hopper GEMMs prefer 12, making them 1.2 to 1.5x slower. They strain the instruction cache. Splitting sometimes won: moving MoE expert dispatch to the copy engine beat a MoE megakernel by 6.9%. In production, months of effort yielded "one Llama 1B megakernel, supporting one batch size (1), one precision (BF16), and one customer." The authors also deleted a rule-based fusion compiler, a static repository of agent-written kernels, their BlockIR capture layer, a knowledge hub, prescribed expert strategies and research-DAG logging. Their stated reasons are that the knowledge hub anchored agents to known patterns, expert strategies trapped them in local minima, and the DAG logging consumed up to 43% of tool calls. What remains is parallel agents editing the forward-pass source code directly, including host-side logic, coordinated through a lightweight message board and fed by "high-fidelity measurements" from real inputs inside a real forward pass (Together AI).

A large share of the post is about how isolated benchmarks mislead. Inductor's isolated kernel timings were 39 to 58% slower than live inference. An embedding kernel ran 2.1x faster on all-zero token IDs than on random IDs. Inductor chose a Triton GEMM at 7.4 µs over cuBLAS at 6.4 µs for Llama 1B's output projection. A top-k indexer took 20 µs on random data and 62 ms on real data. Further claims: Together Compiler runs in under 24 hours on at most two nodes, beats strong baselines by up to 2.6x, reaches 1,111 TPS on MiniMax M3.1-Flash and 1,205 TPS on DeepSeek v4.1-Flash (described as state-of-the-art low latency), and achieves up to 6.4x on non-LLM codebases such as weather, PDEs, time series and biology (Together AI).

Facts vs. opinions. Every figure is self-reported on Together's blog, and the product is commercial with early access through sales. It is not stated to be open source. Some numbers do not reconcile on their face. The H100 result is given as "over 1100" TPS in the same sentence as "up to 1.7x". Against the 990 TPS megakernel, 1,100 is about 1.11x, so the 1.7x must refer to some other baseline the sentence does not name. The knowledge-hub penalty appears as a drop from 5.85x to 4.75x (about 19%) in one place and as 25 to 31% worse elsewhere. Most importantly, the post does not describe how the current system checks correctness. The old BlockIR enforced it through strict pointer matching, and that layer was deleted. The authors concede an IR "might still be important" for formal verification. The "state-of-the-art" latency claims have no third-party comparison in the post (Together AI).

Other perspectives. The 98%-without-a-kernel result is the most transferable finding, and the one most damaging to the megakernel enthusiasm of the past year. Most of the gap between stock torch.compile and a heroic hand-fused kernel on small-batch decode was launch overhead, and CUDA Graphs plus PDL removes most of it. The agent-written-kernel story connects to the March items in today's archive: Meta's KernelAgent and vLLM's single-source Triton attention source. It also connects to Replit's swarm results, since Together's agents also share a message board and also do worse without communication (Replit). The authors' general lesson is that "frameworks that help early on can later become constraints."

Why it matters. For inference engineers the advice is concrete. Benchmark kernels inside a real forward pass on real inputs. Try CUDA Graphs and PDL before writing fused kernels. Treat isolated microbenchmarks with suspicion. For agent builders, the more general lesson is that removing scaffolding (knowledge bases, strategy menus, logging DAGs) improved results once models were strong enough.
