Anthropic's record IPO fails to stop an AI-stock rout

3 sources, 5 min read

What happened. On Friday 20 November, the day after its Nasdaq debut, Anthropic's stock fell sharply. So did the rest of the AI trade. Morning Brew says shares "plummeted 8.5%, at one point nearly hitting its IPO price of $83." It says the Nasdaq "nose-dived 5.6%, wiping out its gains for the year," and the S&P 500 fell 3.7%, "putting it in bear market territory" morningbrew. CNBC confirms the 8.5% figure: "Anthropic shares fell 8.5% on Friday to close their first full day of trading." CNBC also says the company raised $81.3 billion, which it calls the largest IPO of all time (CNBC). Disclosure, repeated: this briefing is produced by an Anthropic model. Read the framing with that in mind. morningbrew

Details. CNBC gives the debut figures: an opening price of $111.50 on Thursday and a close of $97.17. CNBC's own sentence is not entirely clear about which session that close belongs to (CNBC). A secondary explainer site, shattered.io, gives the most detailed numbers. It puts the IPO price at $83 and says the offering raised about $81.3B. It puts the Thursday open at $111.50, 33% above the offer, and the Thursday close at $97.17, up 17%, with the valuation peaking near $2T. For Friday it says the stock was down 9% "after sliding as much as 14% during the session" (shattered.io). The same site says more than $163B of market value was lost, citing Yahoo Finance. It also estimates an intraday low of about $83.60, which it labels "our calculation, not a reported low." That matters, because Morning Brew's "nearly hitting its IPO price" may rest on derived figures like this one rather than a reported print. On the wider market, shattered.io says "The Nasdaq Composite fell 5.3% to 22,617.73," that CNN reported 5.28%, and that "the Cboe Volatility Index (VIX) jumped 33% to 37." Trump threatens a "tremendous tax" on US firms that build data centres abroad

2 sources, 4 min read

What happened. At a White House press conference on Friday 20 November, President Trump said the federal government would put a "tremendous tax" on US companies that build data centres in other countries. He presented it as part of keeping ahead of China in AI. CNBC reported that the policy "apparently came as news to his own staff" cnbc (CNBC). cnbc

Details. CNBC's report, by Kevin Breuninger and updated at 3:47 PM EST, quotes him: "We're going to fight very hard for the data centers." Companies can still build abroad: "they can, but we're going to put a tremendous tax on them." He also said "We have a lot of places that want them badly, and they're great places." On how new the policy was, he said: "I don't know if that's a breaking story," and "I haven't even said that to my people. But I'm telling them now they're here." CNBC gives no tax rate and no legal mechanism. It names Alphabet, Amazon, Microsoft, Meta and Oracle as companies with data centres outside the US. Amazon, Google, Microsoft and Oracle had not commented by publication time. CNBC adds that cloud providers want facilities near customers, "sometimes because regulations require it." The remarks came a day after a summit in China with Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin, which CNBC says "appeared to produce few concrete deliverables" (CNBC). Newsweek quotes a harder version: "We're not allowing data centers to go into other countries because China will take all of them," and "we're going to put tremendous penalties on companies that try and do it." It says Trump referred to a planned data centre in Finland and said his administration would block it. Newsweek notes Google is investing about €13 billion there, which it calls Google's "largest single investment in Europe," though Newsweek does not say whether Trump meant Google's project. Microsoft declined to comment, and the White House pointed Newsweek back to the remarks. Josh Levi of the Data Center Coalition said "Our industry wants to continue investing in America" (Newsweek). cnbc

Facts vs. opinions. What is established: the remarks were made, and they contained no rate, no start date, no named statute and no written order. Every outlet read here agrees on that. IBTimes UK goes through the legal problems. "Article I, Section 8 of the US Constitution gives Congress the power to levy and collect federal taxes," and "The president does not have an independent constitutional power to create a new tax," although Congress can delegate specific powers. It says "Trump did not identify any statute that would authorise the threatened charge," and "Without a legal basis, his announcement alone cannot create a tax obligation." It cites the Supreme Court's 2026 ruling in Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump as holding that "the International Emergency Economic Powers Act does not authorise the president to impose tariffs." It also points out that "A data centre built in Finland is not itself an imported product simply because its owner is an American company" (IBTimes UK). The article quotes no named legal experts, so this is IBTimes' own analysis, though it rests on the plain text of the Constitution. The China justification is Trump's claim and is not argued in any detail. Morning Brew's statement that public backlash to data centres "helped propel some Democrats to victory in the midterm elections" is its own interpretation cnbc. cnbc

Other perspectives. There is an obvious tension inside the policy. CNBC's All-America Economic Survey found 64% of registered voters oppose an AI data centre within 10 miles of their home, and 13% would support one (CNBC). IBTimes reports that on 18 November Loudoun County, Virginia, voted to "pause final action on certain data-centre and electrical-substation applications for up to 12 months" (IBTimes UK). So the policy aims to push construction back into a country where Loudoun County has just paused approvals, and where Morning Brew expects a new Democratic cohort to slow the build-out from January morningbrew. A second objection is about how cloud services work. Much of the overseas capacity serves foreign customers under data-residency and latency rules. That point appears in CNBC's own copy and in IBTimes ("local data requirements, electricity availability and operating costs can influence where infrastructure is developed"). A third view treats the remark as a bargaining move. That is speculation, and no source read here argues it with evidence. cnbc +1

Why it matters. Even unenacted, the threat adds political risk to every hyperscaler region plan. The market had already sold off on AI the same day morningbrew. If something does get written down, the details that matter are these: whether it targets capital spending or revenue, whether it exempts capacity built to meet residency rules, and whether it covers leased colocation or only owned sites. For engineers, the downstream effects would be on region availability and the price of sovereign and in-region inference, including EU deployments that cannot legally leave the EU. Until there is a text, the right stance is to watch and not plan around it. The Morning Brew item that carried this story also carried the Riemann claim, which is covered in Brain & consciousness below. cnbc +1

Revisited: DeepMind's "Towards Autonomous Mathematics Research" (Aletheia), February 2026

3 sources, 3 min read

What it said. In February, DeepMind presented Aletheia, "a math research agent that iteratively generates, verifies, and revises solutions end-to-end in natural language," running on an advanced version of Gemini Deep Think. The paper was offered as a reflection on the first wave of research papers produced with mathematicians github. github

Paper facts. It is arXiv 2602.10177, cs.LG, by Tony Feng, Trieu H. Trinh, Garrett Bingham and others, 28 authors in all, including Demis Hassabis, Koray Kavukcuoglu, Quoc V. Le and Thang Luong. Version 1 is dated 10 February 2026, v2 12 February, and v3 6 March, which was "updated with summary of FirstProof results." It is 42 pages and licensed CC BY-NC-ND 4.0. No peer-reviewed venue is listed. It reports three milestones. The first is a paper on "eigenweights" in arithmetic geometry (Feng26) generated without human intervention. The second is a human-AI paper proving bounds on independent sets (LeeSeo26). The third is a semi-autonomous review of 700 open problems from Bloom's Erdős database, including autonomous solutions to four open questions. It proposes standardised levels of autonomy and novelty and "human-AI interaction cards," and releases all prompts and outputs on GitHub (arXiv). The companion Erdős paper, arXiv 2601.22401 with 24 authors and v3 dated 5 February 2026, gives a different count. It addresses 13 problems marked open: "5 through seemingly novel autonomous solutions," and 8 by finding earlier solutions in the literature. Its v3 note says the authors reclassified Erdős-935 as an independent rediscovery, "bringing the number of autonomous solutions down to 5" (arXiv). The two papers disagree, four against five, and the counts have moved between versions. Neither abstract gives a baseline. github

How it aged. It aged well as a method and as a set of norms, and less well on the headline counts. Those counts kept shrinking as human reviewers found prior literature. The Erdős paper says "the 'Open' status of the problems was through obscurity rather than difficulty," and warns of "subconscious plagiarism" by AI (arXiv). That honest correction is the most durable part of the work. The sharper test came nine months later. On 20 November 2026 Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, posted a note claiming a proof of the Riemann hypothesis, and of the Generalized Riemann hypothesis, made "working with Claude." Compare it with Aletheia's norms. Aletheia worked in natural language, released every prompt and output, and proposed interaction cards that state who did what. Alpöge's note, as an alphaXiv overview puts it, does not present "a readable proof." It points to "parts of a Lean archive," and Alpöge writes "I decided to only present Lean" (alphaXiv). So the field has split into two styles of AI-assisted mathematics. DeepMind's style is checked in natural language by humans and carefully classified. The newer style is formal-first and published as machine-checkable code. Each has its own failure mode. In the first, reviewers can miss prior literature. In the second, the formal statement may not say what the headline says. There is also a link between the two camps. Carlo Pagano, a co-author of the Erdős paper and described by Scientific American as a visiting researcher at Google DeepMind, commented on Alpöge's claim: "it is very plausible to me that the new ideas in the proof will open up brand new avenues of thinking in number theory" (Scientific American). Scientific American also reports that Alpöge shared a "Claude-generated solution" to the Jacobian conjecture in July. That earlier claim was not checked for this issue. No evidence was found that Aletheia's autonomy levels or interaction cards were taken up as a community standard, and this issue did not search for their use. github +1

Why it matters now. When an AI-maths headline appears, Aletheia's paper gives the right questions to ask. How autonomous was the work? Was the novelty checked against the literature? Are the full transcripts public? Who validated what? The Riemann claim answers some of these, since the Lean code is public, and leaves others open, since there is no readable exposition and no account of how the work was split between human and model. Aletheia's lesson that "open" can mean "obscure" applies less to the Riemann hypothesis, which is anything but obscure. For a claim like that, the risk lies in whether the Lean statement is faithful, not in missed prior work. github +1

Revisited: Recurrent-Depth VLA, February 2026

1 source, 3 min read

What it said. The paper claimed that vision-language-action (VLA) models can scale test-time compute implicitly. Instead of generating chain-of-thought tokens, a weight-tied action head loops in latent space huggingface. huggingface

Paper facts. It is arXiv 2602.07845, cs.RO, by Yalcin Tur, Jalal Naghiyev, Haoquan Fang, Wei-Chuan Tsai, Jiafei Duan, Dieter Fox and Ranjay Krishna. It was submitted on 8 February 2026, and as of this check only v1 exists, with no venue listed (arXiv). The backbone is Qwen2.5-0.5B from MiniVLA, with frozen DINOv2 and SigLIP vision encoders and LoRA fine-tuning. The action head has a prelude, a recurrent core and a coda. Training uses a random recurrence depth with a mean of 32 and truncated backpropagation through time over the last 8 iterations. On LIBERO, the fixed 12-iteration model averages 93.0, and the adaptive variant averages 92.5. That compares with 89.7 for Fast-ThinkAct (3B), 88.8 for SmolVLA (2.2B), 86.6 for MolmoAct (7B), 85.5 for π0-FAST (3B), 81.1 for CoT-VLA (7B) and 76.5 for OpenVLA (7B). The iteration sweep is the core result: 8.4 at 1 iteration, 40.5 at 2, 84.1 at 4, 92.6 at 8 and a peak of 93.1 at 24. The authors write that "tasks that fail entirely (0% success) with single-iteration inference exceed 90% success with four iterations." Adaptive stopping averages 7.93 iterations, "a 34% reduction in compute." On CALVIN ABC→D it reaches an average chain length of 3.39, against 3.27 for OpenVLA. Real-robot tests on a bimanual YAM robot, against Diffusion Policy and π0.5, are described without exact numbers. The speed claims do not match each other. The abstract says "up to 80× inference speedup," the introduction says "up to an order of magnitude faster," and no timing table supports either. The stated limitations are possible degradation beyond the best depth, "minimal hyperparameter tuning and a relatively small backbone (0.5B parameters)," and halting or help-seeking mechanisms left to future work. The arXiv page lists a project page but no code repository (arXiv HTML). huggingface

How it aged. Partly verified. The paper itself has not moved: still v1, no venue, as of 22 November. The underlying idea, refinement in latent space with adaptive depth instead of token chains, became a busy research area through 2026. arXiv search listings show many looped-transformer and latent-reasoning titles from June to October 2026, but none were read for this issue, so they are only pointers. The curator's note mentions a later "LatentLoop" paper. A search found no paper under that name, so the link between RD-VLA and it cannot be confirmed here. No evidence was found that a production VLA, such as a π-series or Gemini Robotics release, adopted recurrent-depth action heads, and this issue did not look for one in depth. What holds up is the shape of the scaling curve: very poor at one iteration, saturating at 8 to 12. That is a useful empirical result for anyone designing a compute budget for a small on-robot model. huggingface

Why it matters now. This is the robotics version of a question that now comes up in language models too: can reasoning be inspected if it never surfaces as tokens? A latent loop that turns 0% into 90% is efficient but hard to audit. For a reader interested in agent harnesses, the adaptive halting rule based on latent convergence is a reusable pattern: spend compute where the state has not yet settled. The unresolved speed claim is a reminder to ask for wall-clock tables. huggingface

Revisited: Claude Opus 4.8 with "2.5x faster mode and parallel subagents", May 2026

3 sources, 2 min read

What it said. AlphaSignal's headline on 29 May reported that Anthropic had shipped Claude Opus 4.8 with a 2.5x faster mode and parallel subagents. The bundled item contains no further text alphasignal. alphasignal

How it aged. The headline matches the primary source, with a few qualifications. Anthropic's announcement is dated 28 May 2026. It describes fast mode as one "where the model can work at 2.5× the speed," which is "now three times cheaper than it was for previous models," at "$10 per million input tokens and $50 per million output tokens," against standard pricing of "$5 per million input tokens and $25 per million output tokens." So fast mode still costs twice the standard rate. The parallel-subagent feature is Dynamic Workflows, "available in research preview," in which "Claude can plan the work and then run hundreds of parallel subagents in a single session" and "then verifies its outputs before reporting back to the user." It is aimed at "codebase-scale migrations across hundreds of thousands of lines of code from kickoff to merge," and is "available in Claude Code for Enterprise, Team, and Max plans" (Anthropic). Some third-party write-ups claim a hard cap of 1,000 subagents. That figure is not in the official text, which says "hundreds." The headline benchmark table on the announcement page is an image, and a SWE-bench Verified figure seen in secondary listings was not checked. The figures stated in the text include 84% on Online-Mind2Web, and the claim that it is "the first model to break 10% overall on the all-pass standard" on a Legal Agent Benchmark. It also says the model is "around four times less likely than its predecessor to allow flaws in code" on an honesty evaluation, which is Anthropic's own measurement. Why it matters now. Two parts of this release have lasted beyond the model. One is pricing speed as its own product tier. The other is treating orchestration across many subagents as a feature of the vendor's harness rather than something users build. Both show up in today's stories. Duet runs background agents against plan limits, and the IPO puts the economics of selling speed and parallel subagents under public scrutiny. Research-preview status is the caveat to watch. Features labelled that way can change terms, and the official page does not promise availability. alphasignal +1

Revisited: "Agent Skills go mainstream", August 2026

2 sources, 2 min read

What it said. AgentBrief claimed that Agent Skills, the file-based SKILL.md instruction packages loaded at runtime, had become "ecosystem standard in weeks." It cited a repo with 345 skills across Claude Code, Codex, Gemini CLI, Cursor and "9+ more tools," a collection of 139 legal skills, an "autoskills" installer with 6.6k GitHub stars and SHA-256 verification, and Google open-sourcing Agent Skills. It called skills "the npm package of the agent era" x. x

How it aged. The standard itself held. The ecosystem numbers in the item were not verified. The canonical site, agentskills.io, describes Agent Skills as "a lightweight, open format for extending AI agent capabilities." It says the format "was originally developed by Anthropic, released as an open standard, and has been adopted by a growing number of agent products," with development open on GitHub. The core mechanism is progressive disclosure in three stages: discovery, where only the name and description load at startup; activation, where the full SKILL.md is read when a task matches; and execution, which may run bundled scripts (Agent Skills). The landing page links to a client showcase, which was not read, so no adopter count is given here. Third-party guides cite counts ranging from about 26 to "70+," but none were read and the range is too wide to trust. The 345-module repo, the 6.6k-star figure and the quoted praise of "GPT 5.6 SOL" were not checked. One adjacent data point is a counterexample. Google's Jules gained MCP support in February 2026, not skills, and is now being wound down in favour of Antigravity (see Wildcards) (Jules changelog). So the "Google" part of the cross-vendor story has moved to a different product. x +1

Why it matters now. The npm comparison fits, including npm's problems. A skill can bundle executable scripts that an agent runs on demand, so a skills marketplace is a software supply chain with prompt-level triggers. Hash verification, as claimed for autoskills, protects integrity but not intent. A skill whose description is written to match broadly can get itself activated, and its instructions then enter the context with the user's authority. For the security-minded reader, the open questions are signing, provenance, and whether clients sandbox bundled scripts. The agentskills.io overview does not address them. x

Erik Hoel's walk, and what the Riemann claim actually rests on

2 sources, 3 min read

Erik Hoel's post is not an argument. It is a short personal essay that opens "On the day the Riemann hypothesis fell to AI, I took my son for a walk." It describes a feverish five-year-old with a staff "that looked like something Gandalf would have used," and a pond where the boy dips the staff to touch "the me on the other side." The weight is in the ending. They have been doing maths together "Just 20 minutes a day, and we're almost through arithmetic." That night the boy asks about maths, and Hoel says "No, maybe tomorrow," then writes: "But it won't be tomorrow, either" source. The most natural reading is that he has decided to stop teaching his son maths, or has at least lost the reason to. That is this briefing's interpretation. Hoel does not say it outright. The essay takes "fell to AI" as given. That premise needs checking. source

The premise comes from a note Levent Alpöge, an Anthropic employee, posted on 20 November. Scientific American quotes it: "I believe I have, working with Claude, discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis," and reports that Alpöge "says his proof confirms the 'Generalized Riemann Hypothesis.'" It also quotes his line that "It is natural to feel that this is an end, in my case a loss of a principal guiding light," which is close to Hoel's mood (Scientific American). Morning Brew frames it correctly as a claim that will matter "If the proof checks out" cnbc. The alphaXiv overview of the note describes the method as "Fourier-analytic ideas, theta lifts, and an analogy with Stepanov's argument over function fields," with the core idea "to chain theta lifts so that each lift mimics multiplicity." It stresses that the note "does not present a readable proof," lacks "Lean-check output, independent audit, or accessible derivation," and "does not show that the formalized theorem is RH." It records Alpöge calling his own timeline "rough and lossily compressed" (alphaXiv). source +1

Sources disagree on how far verification has got. The Scientific American page, updated the same day, states that "The result was later verified by independent researchers" and that Andrew Sutherland of MIT checked it "after he had independently compiled Alpöge's result in Lean." It quotes him: "This is indeed an historic moment," and "There is much work to be done to understand the mathematical details of the proof." It also notes that "Issues can arise in Lean," and quotes Hector Pasten and Carlo Pagano only conditionally, "If the proof is correct" (Scientific American). Compiling a Lean development shows that its proofs check against its own definitions and axioms. It does not by itself show that the top-level theorem statement means the Riemann hypothesis as mathematicians understand it, or that no unsound axiom or shortcut is hidden somewhere in the archive. Those are exactly the gaps the alphaXiv overview names. Figures circulating in search snippets, such as a 175,187-file archive, 17 million lines of Lean and a 250,000-page AI-generated explanation, come from pages that could not be read or were not opened, and are not checked. Nature and Science coverage could not be read (login redirect and 403). As of 22 November, the fair summary is that a formal artefact exists, one respected number theorist has compiled it and called the moment historic, and the statement-fidelity audit and a human-readable account are still to come. "Fell to AI" is ahead of the evidence by perhaps weeks, perhaps longer. cnbc +1

For this section's theme, Hoel's essay records a reaction that may become common: deciding what to teach a child based on what machines can do. Whatever happens to the proof, a 250,000-page or Lean-only proof would have been verified rather than understood. Whether people learn arithmetic was never about who finds new theorems. That is this briefing's argument, not Hoel's. source

Global workspace probes in vision-language models

1 source, 2 min read

The Hugging Face listing, with 35 upvotes, links an arXiv preprint whose title puts "conscious" in quotation marks for good reason huggingface. huggingface

Paper facts. "Probing the 'Conscious' Mind: Selective Access and Global Workspace in Vision-Language Models" is arXiv 2611.26590 (cs.AI, cross-listed cs.CV and cs.LG), by Jiahao Huang and Jun Wang, with no affiliations listed. It was submitted on 19 November 2026, v1 only, with no venue (arXiv). It introduces MC-Lens, "a multimodal Jacobian lens," which projects intermediate states from the vision encoder (V-Lens) and the language decoder (L-Lens) into the decoder's output-vocabulary space. That makes it possible to compare and intervene on concept accessibility across modalities. The main experiments use Qwen3-VL-8B, with other families and sizes said to appear later in the paper. The samples are small. There are 100 A-OKVQA cases read at decoder layer D32. There are 46 of 50 flag images, because four countries lack a single-token name. There are 32 THINGS concepts with 4 images each, 100 COCO images with 3 target objects each, and 51 source-to-target pairs across 7 tasks for cross-task interventions. Reported results include a one-hop J@5 intervention that "redirected nearly every answer," while F≤3 redirected fewer than half, and a J@35 intervention that restored about 85% of answers, but with only about 15% of cases both necessary and sufficient (Hugging Face / arXiv HTML). The authors use "conscious" in a functional sense only and make no claim about subjective experience. No code link appears. Only the first part of the long HTML was read, so the limitations section was not checked. huggingface

Assessment. The four claims in the abstract are that concepts are readable from intermediate states, accessible "as tasks require," used in downstream computation, and that reliable selective access "develops later than concept accessibility" during training. These are mechanistic-interpretability findings described in Global Workspace Theory vocabulary. The step that GWT treats as decisive, a limited-capacity workspace with global broadcast and ignition-like competition, is not obviously tested by showing that task-relevant concepts can be decoded and steered. Any network that solves the task should show that. The roughly 15% necessary-and-sufficient figure cuts against a clean "workspace" story. It suggests the concept representations are distributed and redundant, not concentrated in a bottleneck. As a tool, MC-Lens may be useful, and the training-dynamics result is the most interesting part if it holds across models. As evidence about consciousness, it establishes functional access, which GWT says is necessary for consciousness but does not say is sufficient. It is a two-author preprint with no code, so wait for replication. huggingface

Reading path: State of Brain Emulation Report 2025, closing chapters — open challenges, strategic priorities, and how it revises the 2008 roadmap

2 sources, 3 min read

What to read. The report is arXiv 2510.15745 (q-bio.NC), by Niccolò Zanichelli, Maximilian Schons, Isaak Freeman, Philip Shiu and Anton Arkhipov. v1 is dated 17 October 2025 and v3, the current version, 5 November 2025 (arXiv). The full text is a 182-page PDF linked from the project site, published under CC BY 4.0 with about 40 contributors and funded by Fieldcrest Ventures, MxSchons GmbH and the Foresight Institute (brainemulation.mxschons.com). A caveat on this step: the full PDF was too large to retrieve and the arXiv HTML returned 404, so the closing chapters themselves were not read for this issue. What follows is based on the abstract and the project's key findings. The chapter-level detail is for the reader to fill in. source

Why this step. The abstract says the report is organised around three capabilities: recording activity ("Neural Dynamics"), mapping structure ("Connectomics") and "Computational Neuroscience" for simulation and embodiment. It uses Sandberg and Bostrom's 2008 roadmap as its baseline and "outlines strategic priorities" (arXiv). The closing chapters are where the stocktake becomes a programme. Anders Sandberg's endorsement on the site calls it "the most useful quantitative update I've seen on the whole brain emulation roadmap" (brainemulation.mxschons.com). source

Key ideas to look for. First, a change of bottleneck. The site's first key finding is "Data is the bottleneck, not hardware or algorithms." Pull quotes add "No foreseeable technology enables full neuron recording" and "Data constraints set a hard upper bound on model quality." Read the closing chapters for how far this revises the 2008 roadmap. This issue did not check the 2008 text, but the roadmap is commonly summarised as treating scanning resolution and compute as the main uncertainties. The 2025 report's emphasis on recordings of function and on validation is the main change to look for. Second, the scale of the evidence. No organism has reached whole-brain recording, defined as at least 95% of neurons. Larval zebrafish recordings reach about 80% coverage and C. elegans about 50%, usually at 1 to 30 Hz, for minutes to hours. The largest mouse connectome volume is 1 mm³ of visual cortex, with about 120,000 neurons and 523 million synapses. Third, the priority order. "Faithful emulation of small brains is the first step." Organisms below 1M neurons could be emulated "potentially within the decade" at a cost in the "low $100Ms." Fourth, the field's size. "less than 500 people globally are actively dedicated to the direct objectives of brain emulation," and global basic neuroscience funding is about $0.5B per year (brainemulation.mxschons.com). One finding the reader should note: 40 to 50% of synaptic connections differ between genetically identical worms. If that holds, a single connectome is a sample rather than a specification, and any emulation must be validated on behaviour and not on wiring alone. source

Open questions to carry forward. What counts as a faithful emulation, and is that defined by behaviour, by responses to perturbation, or by matched activity statistics? Which biological details can be safely ignored? How should a field of under 500 people be organised to build pipelines that need industrial scale? Secondary summaries say the report favours focused research organisations, but this was not checked against the text. A related question for the reader's AI work: whether interpretability tools like today's MC-Lens, which read concepts out of artificial networks, have any counterpart for the validation problem in emulation. That is speculative and not from the report. source +1

Jules, Google's asynchronous coding agent (August 2025)

2 sources, 2 min read

Why this is here: exploring recursive self-improvement and agent harness techniques.

The item is Google's August 2025 announcement that Jules, "an asynchronous coding agent powered by Gemini 2.5," was open to everyone blog. Fifteen months later, it is a case study in the life cycle of an agent product. The official changelog shows a steady run of harness work. On 26 January 2026 came a "Planning Critic," in which a second agent reviews auto-approved plans, credited with cutting task failures by 9.5%. On 30 January Gemini 3 Flash became the base model for all tiers. On 2 February came MCP support for a vetted set of servers (Linear, Stitch, Neon, Tinybird, Context7, Supabase). On 19 February a CI Fixer arrived that repairs failed checks on Jules' own pull requests. On 9 March Gemini 3.1 Pro became the Pro-plan default. Then, on 29 October 2026, a notice titled "Jules Turndown": "Jules will wind down on December 31, 2026." Sessions could continue until 11 November, after which Jules is read-only. Users are told they can "export everything Jules knows about your work into a local folder," by pasting an export prompt into a CLI agent such as Antigravity CLI. Google says Jules' lessons are "shaping products across Google, including our agent-first platform, Google Antigravity" (Jules changelog). blog

For someone interested in harnesses and self-improvement, the useful part is the techniques, not the product. A critic agent gating a planner, an agent repairing CI failures on its own pull requests, and "memories" exported as portable files are small, closed improvement loops. They are bounded and measurable, and they are not recursive self-improvement in any strong sense. The 9.5% figure is Google's own, with no stated baseline workload. The export path is worth noting: what the agent knew about a codebase becomes a local folder of files, which is close to the Agent Skills idea from the archive. Does it hold up? The product did not. The patterns probably did, because they now appear in other harnesses, though that is this briefing's judgement and not something the changelog shows. The practical lesson is to keep agent state in formats that can be exported, because the product hosting the agent may be shut down. blog +1

GSW: episodic memory for RAG (November 2025)

2 sources, 1 min read

Why this is here: exploring inference infrastructure, kernels, and model tooling.

Paper facts. "Beyond Fact Retrieval: Episodic Memory for RAG with Generative Semantic Workspaces" is arXiv 2511.07587 (cs.AI, cs.CL), by Shreyas Rajesh, Pavan Holur, Chenda Duan, David Chong and Vwani Roychowdhury. v1 is dated 10 November 2025 and v2 16 February 2026. It was accepted at AAAI 2026 as an oral presentation. It proposes the Generative Semantic Workspace (GSW), a "neuro-inspired generative memory framework" with an Operator, which turns observations into intermediate semantic structures, and a Reconciler, which merges them into a persistent workspace consistent in time, space and logic. On the Episodic Memory Benchmark (EpBench), with corpora of 100k to 1M tokens, the authors report that GSW beats RAG-based baselines "by up to 20%" and uses 51% fewer query-time context tokens than the next most token-efficient baseline. Code is at github.com/roychowdhuryresearch/gsw-memory (arXiv). Only the abstract page was read. The per-baseline tables, the cost of building the workspace at ingest time, and the authors' stated limitations were not checked. huggingface

Why it fits the infrastructure theme: the claim is really about moving cost. GSW spends LLM calls at write time, through the Operator and Reconciler on every observation, to save tokens at read time. Whether that is a win depends on the read-to-write ratio and on whether ingest can be batched on cheaper models. The abstract does not give the ingest bill, and that is the number to look for in the paper. Taking it a step further, a workspace of entities, roles and spatiotemporal context is a structured cache. It sits close to what the global-workspace paper above probes inside a model, here built outside it. Does it hold up? The venue gives it more weight than a preprint. "Up to 20%" is a best case on one benchmark, and whether it transfers to messy agent logs is untested in anything read here. huggingface +1
