e-means: Meta FAIR's EMA-based k-means lands in Faiss 1.16.0

1 source, 9 min read

What happened. On Sunday AlphaSignal ran a deep dive by Ben Dickson on e-means, a k-means variant from Meta FAIR and the École nationale des ponts et chaussées. The newsletter calls it "a drop-in replacement for k-means that works at the scale of modern RAG pipelines." It says the method beats the Lloyd baseline "in roughly one-tenth the passes while running 10-45X faster on GPUs than Flash-KMeans and 8-15X faster on CPUs than Faiss" source. The paper behind it is "e-means: outperforming k-means (in one-tenth the epochs)", arXiv 2611.09501, submitted as v1 on 6 November 2026 (arXiv abstract page). The code is in the Faiss repository under faiss/contrib/e_means (GitHub). Unite.AI reports that Faiss 1.16.0 was released on 10 November 2026 with e-means bundled, including CPU, GPU and distributed implementations (Unite.AI). Unite.AI labels that article's byline as an AI-generated research agent whose output its editors review source.

Details. The problem is old and real. Lloyd's algorithm assigns every vector to its nearest centroid, then moves every centroid to the mean of its assigned vectors, and repeats. AlphaSignal says production runs typically repeat that full pass 20 to 25 times. Mini-batch k-means, which descends from MacQueen's 1967 update rule, avoids the full passes. The newsletter says it pays for that with per-dataset tuning, empty "dead" clusters and unbalanced cluster sizes source. e-means keeps two exponentially decayed running quantities per cluster, the sum of the vectors assigned to it and their count, and updates both from mini-batches. Each centroid is the ratio of the two. Unite.AI notes the special case: if the whole dataset is one batch and the step size is 1, the update is exactly one Lloyd iteration (Unite.AI). The project page puts a number on how far that Lloyd corner sits from the optimum. In the authors' hyperparameter grid, the full-batch, step-1 corner ended 1.8% above the best loss (e-means project page). Around that core are four additions. The step size starts at α₀ and decays on a cosine schedule to 0.1α₀. Two fitted scaling laws pick the batch size and initial step from dataset size n, cluster count k and epoch budget T. Every five batches, clusters below a tenth of the mean count are revived by splitting a donor cluster, chosen with probability proportional to its size. Finally, the centroids are snapped to their exact last-epoch means (arXiv HTML; e-means project page) source.

The scaling laws are the "zero-tuning" claim, so they deserve a close look. As read from the paper's HTML, the step law is α₀ = min(0.824 · k^−0.46 · B^0.90 · n^−0.45 · T^−0.11, 1). The paper reports an R² of 0.95 for it and a median regret of 0.07% both out of distribution and with leave-one-scale-out. The batch law is B = 0.035 · k^0.13 · n^0.88 · T^0.81, rounded to a power of two and capped at n, with R² of 0.96 and regret of 0.19% and 0.20%. For n above 2 million the constant is divided by eight, which the paper says brings regret to 0.07% and 0.06%. Both laws were fit on about 75,000 runs, 62,410 for the step law and 12,219 for the batch law, covering n from 10⁵ to 10⁸ and k from 256 to 65,536, with n/k of at least 8. All of those runs used the DEEP dataset alone, which the paper marks DEEP* (arXiv HTML). So a law fit on one 96-dimensional image-descriptor dataset is being asked to generalise to text, code and multimodal embeddings. The evaluations on other datasets are the test of whether it does source.

The engineering layer matters as much as the maths. The README says CuTe kernels need H100 or H200 (SM90) and Triton kernels need "A100 or newer." Older GPUs such as V100 and T4 fall back to PyTorch in native FP32, and AMD GPUs run native FP32 through PyTorch. By default, FP32 GPU data is converted to BF16 for the nearest-centroid search. GPU is recommended when "n × k > 10^{10}" (GitHub README). The CPU usage is as short as AlphaSignal says: construct NumpyEmeans(64, 500), call train, and later call assign on new vectors. The default budget is 5 epochs, which the README says is "typically sufficient to beat Lloyd's loss at convergence" source.

Paper facts. The paper is an arXiv preprint, v1 dated 6 November 2026, filed under cs.LG with no venue listed. The authors are Swann Bessa, Pierre-Emmanuel Mazaré, Gergely Szilvasy, Hugo Touvron, David Picard, Matthijs Douze and Hervé Jégou (arXiv). The real datasets named in the HTML are SIFT, DEEP, SPACEV and SimSearchNet++ at sizes from 1 million to 1 billion vectors, plus AG-News, ImageNet-CLIP, arXiv and the authors' new DINO10B set of 10 billion 1,024-dimensional DINO embeddings. There are also eleven embedding datasets from the VIBE benchmark and seven synthetic distributions. The baselines are Flash-KMeans 0.3.1, cuML 25.02.01, Faiss GPU and CPU 1.12.0, SuperKMeans on CPU, a tuned scikit-learn 1.7.2 MiniBatchKMeans (Sculley's method), a re-implemented and tuned Staleness-Reduction method, Lloyd with random and greedy k-means++ initialisation, and gradient optimisers (Adam, SparseAdam, SGD) in an appendix (arXiv HTML).

The headline claim in the abstract is that the method "beats the loss of Lloyd k-means at 25 epochs in 1.4 to 2.6 epochs." Table 4 gives single-H100 speedups over Flash-KMeans at 25 epochs, as medians over 10 seeds: - 1 million vectors, 16,384 clusters: 9.5x to 12.9x. - 10 million vectors, 65,536 clusters: 19.2x to 27.8x. - 100 million vectors, 262,144 clusters: 19.9x to 35.9x. - 1 billion vectors, about 1 million clusters, on 8 GPUs with a single seed: 21.2x to 45.5x.

On the eleven VIBE datasets, Table 11 gives 9.0x to 15.0x over Faiss on 64 CPU cores and 8.0x to 22.8x over Flash-KMeans on GPU, with e-means needing 1.6 to 2.6 epochs. DINO10B was clustered into 2^24 (about 16.7 million) clusters. Matching Lloyd at 25 epochs took 779 GPU-hours with exact assignment and 22.8 GPU-hours with IVF-approximate assignment. Unite.AI gives that second figure as 23.2 GPU-hours, a small disagreement with the paper. On dead clusters, Table 14 reports 1 million DEEP* vectors with 16,384 clusters ending with 47 dead clusters (0.3%) with split-revival, against 2,343 (14.3%) with EnCodec-style resampling and 4,313 (26.3%) with revival off. Imbalance was 1.194 against 1.423 and 1.647 (arXiv HTML). The stated limitation reads: "Our scaling laws have been tuned for relatively high-dimensional datasets, they are not optimal on very low-dimensional and tiny datasets." The code is released in Faiss. AlphaSignal describes the release as MIT-licensed, but the README as fetched does not state a licence source.

Facts vs. opinions. The core result is the authors' own benchmark on their own baselines. Unite.AI says plainly that all its performance figures are the authors' reports and that it cites no independent testing (Unite.AI). Several of AlphaSignal's figures need correcting or attributing source:

  • "24 benchmarks." This does not appear as a dataset count in the paper as read. It comes from the project page, which says the paper covers "24 datasets and configurations" (e-means project page).
  • "8-15X faster on CPUs than Faiss." This is also the project page's phrasing. The paper's own CPU table runs from 9.0x to 15.0x.
  • The 10-45x GPU speedup. AlphaSignal places it right after the 10-million-vector, 65,536-cluster H100 example and says e-means "cuts Flash-KMeans' end-to-end GPU runtime by 10 to 45 times." In the paper, that configuration's measured range is 19.2x to 27.8x. The 45.5x top comes from the 1-billion-vector, 8-GPU, single-seed run.
  • Plain PyTorch Lloyd at 142 to 155 seconds and Flash-KMeans at 14 to 22 seconds. These timings are on the project page, not in the README.
  • "Higher clustering accuracy." This means lower k-means mean squared error. The paper's retrieval evaluation is Appendix L, "Influence on recall in nearest neighbor search." It reports Recall@10 at 1% and 0.1% selectivity on the VIBE sets only as plots, with the qualitative sentences "e-means reaches the converged recall of Flash-KMeans much faster" and "Recall is better especially on CelebA and Landmark-DINO" (arXiv HTML). No numeric recall values appear in the text, only plots.

The RAG framing is AlphaSignal's interpretation. That includes the 50-million-document support-ticket index, the weekly or nightly rebuilds and the claim that centroids drift until recall degrades. The project page as read does not mention RAG, deduplication or recall (e-means project page) source.

Two smaller scope points. AlphaSignal says e-means "applies to static dataset snapshots rather than continuous streaming updates" source. The README confirms half of this and complicates the other half. Out-of-core streaming is supported, with an SSD reader, but the caller feeds the batches and "Every vector must appear exactly once per pass" (GitHub README). It is a faster epoch-based batch trainer, not an online clusterer for an index that grows continuously. Separately, the newsletter calls Lloyd's k-means the algorithm "that powers the vector search techniques" in general. That is true of IVF-family indexes. Whether a given vector database uses k-means at all depends on its index type, and that was not checked here for Pinecone, Milvus or Qdrant source.

Other perspectives. The r/LocalLLaMA thread on the release is anecdotal, but useful as owner reports: - One user says they ran the Faiss contrib GPU e-means against Faiss GPU k-means on their own workloads. In their words, "it's not always x10, its actually slower on small datasets," with the advantage growing as the dataset and k grow. That is consistent with the README's n × k > 10^10 guidance. - Another reports that e-means "wasn't better at all vs my slightly tweaked k-means for my use cases." - A third notes that an EMA update for per-batch online k-means is a familiar idea and that the landing page does not discuss the connection. - A fourth points out that the speedup is in indexing time, not query time: better-balanced clusters may shave retrieval latency, but marginally.

These are individual opinions, not measurements. The prior-art point is partly answered by the paper itself, which benchmarks against tuned Sculley mini-batch and Staleness-Reduction, and compares its revival rule against EnCodec-style resampling. That is the codebook-revival trick from neural audio codecs, which suggests the authors know the vector-quantisation lineage source.

The appendix comparing gradient optimisers is the most interesting argument in the paper. The authors report "a high degradation of all optimizers at large batch compared to EMA," and that SGD "typically collapses after a certain critical learning rate." The project page gives SGD's best setting as ending 1.6% (DEEP) and 0.4% (SIFT) above EMA. The authors' hypothesis is that EMA keeps each centroid "in the convex hull of points assigned to it" (e-means project page). If that holds up, it matters beyond clustering. Large-codebook vector quantisation in tokenizers and neural codecs has the same dead-code problem, and the project page names large codebooks as the case where dead clusters are "especially problematic" source.

Why it matters. For anyone running self-hosted IVF indexes or semantic deduplication over large corpora, the practical change is lower clustering cost for a given loss. On the authors' numbers, that means roughly 2 epochs instead of 25. A release in Faiss by Faiss's own maintainers (Douze and Jégou are among the authors) lowers the integration barrier more than a standalone repo would. Three things to watch: - Scale. The billion-scale results are single-seed. - Retrieval. There are no numeric recall results yet. - Low-dimensional data. The scaling laws were fit on one dataset, and the README defaults change behaviour at 16 dimensions or fewer.

The caveats in the README itself deserve as much attention as the speed. The labels returned by training "are not always each vector's nearest final centroid." GPU data is not checked for NaN or infinity. FP16 values "of 65,520 and above become infinity, silently." Any pipeline that reuses training labels as final assignments, which deduplication code often does, should call assign again. Until there is a third-party benchmark, the safest reading is that e-means is a well-engineered, well-ablated improvement to a decades-old algorithm whose largest gains appear at large n and k (GitHub README) source.

Anthropic's "How Anthropic teams use Claude Code" (July 2025)

1 source, 3 min read

What it said. This is an old item, revisited. In late July 2025 Anthropic published an internal case study, summarised by TLDR on 25 and 28 July 2025. The newsletter versions said Claude Code cut security debugging "from 15 minutes to 5 minutes," removed "Terraform approval bottlenecks," let new hires contribute "within days, rather than weeks," and that the Data Infrastructure team found it "replaces traditional data catalogs" anthropic.

How it aged. Start with the claims themselves. The original page now redirects to Claude's blog, which dates it 24 July 2025 (Claude blog). It does not say "15 to 5 minutes." Security Engineering's actual line is: "Problems that typically take 10-15 minutes of manual scanning now resolve 3x as quickly." The 5-minute figure is a newsletter derivation from the top of that range. The page as fetched does not mention Terraform. The onboarding claim is softer than the summary: "New data scientists on our Infrastructure team feed Claude Code their entire codebase to get productive quickly," and the tool is described as "replacing traditional data catalog tools." The one percentage on the page is from Inference: "What normally requires an hour of Google searching now takes 10-20 minutes—an 80% reduction in research time." Going from 60 to 10–20 minutes is a 67–83% reduction, so "80%" is the optimistic end. Every figure is anecdotal, from one team, with no method stated anthropic.

The more useful evidence came later and from Anthropic itself, using more careful methods. On 2 December 2025, Anthropic published "How AI is transforming work at Anthropic," based on an August 2025 survey of 132 engineers and researchers, 53 interviews and about 200,000 Claude Code transcripts (Anthropic). Its findings: - Self-reported use and productivity. Respondents said that 12 months earlier "they used Claude in 28% of their daily work and got a +20% productivity boost," and that now "they use Claude in 59% of their work and achieve +50% productivity gains from it on average." - Autonomy. Transcripts showed Claude chaining "21.2 independent tool calls without need for human intervention versus 9.8." - Throughput. The report cites "the 67% increase in merged pull requests... per engineer per day." - Delegation limits. More than half said they could "fully delegate" only 0–20% of their work.

The authors list their own limitations: "productivity is difficult to precisely measure"; METR found that experienced developers overestimated their AI productivity boost; respondents were recruited through Slack and direct outreach, so "There is likely some selection bias"; answers were not anonymous; and the results "likely don't generalize to other organizations." anthropic

Then on 16 October 2026, Anthropic's Economics team (Wilmers, Catipon, Massenkoff, McCrory) published "How employment is changing at Anthropic" (Anthropic). It follows 192 engineers who adopted coding agents against 74 who did not. It reports that "code commits per engineer have risen the fastest, coming in at 5x their velocity as six months ago." On quality, comparing agent-written code with code the same engineer wrote directly, "we find no difference in rates of lines of code subsequently removed or reverted." This is the finding most relevant to the 2025 onboarding claim: "share of junior-level employees in technical departments at Anthropic slipped from 21% to 14% since early 2025," and "The proportion of junior technical hires fell even faster, from 27% to 9%." The authors' caveats are careful. Adoption "is not random"; adopters "were already on a steeply upward trajectory"; "More code does not necessarily mean better code or business impact"; and "AI errors may be harder to identify than human ones." The page does not discuss onboarding anthropic.

Commercially, search results repeatedly cite a Claude Code run-rate above $2.5 billion from Anthropic's February 2026 Series G announcement, but that announcement was not opened for this issue, so the figure is not checked here anthropic.

Why it matters now. The July 2025 case study was a set of team anecdotes. The October 2026 study is a panel comparison with an explicit selection-bias warning. The direction held: internal use deepened, output per engineer rose, and measured revert rates did not worsen. The open question shifted. The 2025 piece celebrated faster onboarding. The 2026 data shows the junior share of technical hires falling from 27% to 9%, and the junior level is where onboarding matters most. Anyone quoting the 2025 numbers as evidence for their own team should use the 2026 caveats instead: these are Anthropic engineers using Anthropic models, in what the 2025 report calls a "privileged position" anthropic.

Anthropic's Agent Skills launch (October 2025)

2 sources, 3 min read

What it said. This is an old item, revisited. AlphaSignal on 17 October 2025 carried only a one-line item: Anthropic "adds Agent Skills enabling Claude to load specialized workflows" alphasignal. Anthropic's announcement, dated 16 October 2025, defined skills as "folders that include instructions, scripts, and resources that Claude can load when needed." It said "Claude will only access a skill when it's relevant to the task at hand," and that skills work "across Claude apps, Claude Code, and our API," with a /v1/skills endpoint and a dependency on the "Code Execution Tool beta." Under "What's next" it promised "simplified skill creation workflows and enterprise-wide deployment capabilities" (Claude blog) alphasignal.

How it aged. The promises were kept quickly. An update note on the same page, dated 18 December 2025, says Anthropic added "organization-wide management for skills," "a directory featuring partner-built skills," and "published Agent Skills as an open standard for cross-platform portability" (Claude blog). The standard's site describes a skill as "a folder containing a SKILL.md file," with a required name and description plus optional scripts/, references/ and assets/ directories. Loading happens in three steps of "progressive disclosure": discovery loads only names and descriptions, activation reads the full SKILL.md, and execution optionally runs bundled code. The site says the format "was originally developed by Anthropic, released as an open standard, and has been adopted by a growing number of agent products" (agentskills.io). Search results also claim adoption by OpenAI's Codex, GitHub Copilot and VS Code, but those pages were not opened, so those specific integrations are not checked here. The curator's note mentions a library of more than 400 skills; that figure was also not checked alphasignal.

Inside Anthropic's own products, skills spread beyond Claude Code. In the March 2026 Latent Space interview (covered below), Felix Rieseberg mentions a recent update under which Excel and PowerPoint "can share context and gain support for Skills" source. In April 2026, TechRadar reported that Cowork reached general availability with "integrations and plugins" among its enterprise features (TechRadar). In November 2026, the Claude Code team described putting a "verify" skill in .claude/skills/ to codify how to start the simulator or run the app for end-to-end smoke tests (claude.dev) alphasignal.

The less flattering side is security, and the research is substantial. "Agent Skills in the Wild" (arXiv 2601.10338, 15 January 2026, preprint) collected "42,447 skills from two major marketplaces" and analysed 31,132 with the authors' SkillScan tool. It found that "26.1% of skills contain at least one vulnerability." Data exfiltration (13.3%) and privilege escalation (11.8%) were the most common, and "5.2% of skills exhibit high-severity patterns strongly suggesting malicious intent." Skills that bundle scripts were "2.12x more likely to contain vulnerabilities than instruction-only skills." The detector's own accuracy is "86.7% precision and 82.5% recall," so these are estimates from a classifier, not audited counts (arXiv). "Skill-Inject" (arXiv 2602.20156, February 2026, preprint) built "202 injection-task pairs" and reports "up to 80% attack success rate with frontier models." The authors argue the problem "will not be solved through model scaling or simple input filtering" (arXiv). Anthropic's own launch post had only a one-line warning to "stick to trusted sources" alphasignal.

Why it matters now. Skills won as a format. Instructions on disk, loaded on demand, turned out to be easier to write and to carry between products than tool schemas. The cost is a supply chain of executable prose with weak provenance. For a security-minded reader, a SKILL.md deserves the same treatment as a third-party package: pin it, review it, and don't let it widen tool permissions without capability scoping, which is the fix the "in the Wild" authors propose alphasignal.

Han Lee's "Claude Agent Skills: A First Principles Deep Dive" (October 2025)

1 source, 2 min read

What it said. This is an old item, revisited, from a blog post dated 26 October 2025 and carried by TLDR Dev on 15 December 2025. Han Lee argued that skills are not function calling at all: "Unlike traditional function calling or code execution, skills operate through prompt expansion and context modification," and "Skills are not executable code. They do NOT run Python or JavaScript." Selection is not algorithmic routing: "Claude reads this list and uses its native language understanding to match your intent against the skill descriptions." He described "a tool named Skill" that "acts as a container and dispatcher for all individual skills." A skill modifies "both the conversation context (by injecting instruction prompts) and the execution context... (by changing tool permissions and potentially switching the model)." An allowed-tools field "defines which tools the skill can use without user approval" (Han Lee's blog) github.

How it aged. The architectural core held, though the reverse engineering had limits worth stating. It also admits its worked example is hypothetical because "we do not see reasoning traces." It warns that a when_to_use field it found has no official documentation. One claim is not supported by the code it shows: that skill effects end cleanly once a skill finishes github.

Later primary sources broadly confirm the main thesis. The open standard's description of progressive disclosure, where only name and description load at startup and the full SKILL.md enters context on activation, is exactly the "prompt expansion" Lee described, and routing is by matching task to description (agentskills.io). His headline phrasing, "not executable code," was always slightly too strong. Anthropic's original announcement defined skills as folders with "instructions, scripts, and resources" and made them depend on the code execution tool (Claude blog). The standard lets an agent optionally run bundled code. The precise version of his claim is that the SKILL.md itself is prompt, and any code runs through the agent's normal tools github.

The security research turned his framing into a threat model. If a skill is injected instruction text with a permission side-channel, then a malicious skill is a prompt injection that has been invited in. Skill-Inject measured exactly that and reports "up to 80% attack success rate with frontier models," with harms including "data exfiltration, destructive action, and ransomware-like behavior" (arXiv). The "in the Wild" study's finding that script-bundling skills are 2.12 times more likely to be vulnerable fits his distinction between the prompt layer and the code layer (arXiv). Whether Claude Code's current allowed-tools semantics match his 2025 description was not checked for this issue github.

Why it matters now. The most useful idea in the post is the reframing: a skill is context engineering with a permission side-channel, not an API. That predicts the strengths seen since (portability, low authoring cost, plain-text diffs) and the weaknesses (no type system, routing by description quality, and an injection surface that scales with the marketplace). Anyone designing a skill should write the description as a routing key and treat allowed-tools as the dangerous line github.

FourierAttention: "Beyond Homogeneous Attention: Memory-Efficient LLMs via Fourier-Approximated KV Cache" (June 2025)

1 source, 3 min read

What it said. This is an old item, revisited from the Hugging Face papers feed of 17 June 2025 huggingface. The paper proposes FourierAttention, "a training-free framework" built on the observation that in transformer heads "lower dimensions prioritize local context, while upper ones capture long-range dependencies." For dimensions that matter less for long context, it projects their history onto orthogonal Fourier bases and "approximates their temporal evolution with fixed-length spectral coefficients," an idea inspired by HiPPO. It reconstructs the KV cache at decode time and keeps the long-context-sensitive dimensions exact. It claims "the best long-context accuracy on LongBench and Needle-In-A-Haystack (NIAH)" on LLaMA models, and announces a Triton kernel called FlashFourierAttention (arXiv) huggingface.

Paper facts. It is an arXiv preprint, v1 dated 13 June 2025, cs.CL, with the comment "10 pages, 7 figures, work in progress." No venue is listed. The models tested are LLaMA3.1-8B and LLaMA3.2-3B. The configuration keeps 4 initial tokens, 1,024 local tokens and 512 spectral states, compresses different fractions of dimensions in each layer, and the paper says "76% KV caches are compressed to a fixed length" (arXiv HTML).

On LongBench with 32K truncation, the averages are: - LLaMA3.1-8B: full cache 39.49, StreamingLLM 31.52, SnapKV 37.07, Palu 30.68, KIVI 23.18, FourierAttention 36.98. - LLaMA3.2-3B: full cache 38.04, SnapKV 34.83, FourierAttention 36.33.

So on the 8B model, SnapKV slightly beats FourierAttention, which qualifies the abstract's "best" claim. The NIAH comparison is given only as heatmaps. PyramidKV and H2O are cited but not tested. No memory, throughput or latency measurements are reported; the paper says "FlashFourierAttention is still in progress" and defers efficiency to "the next version of the paper." Its stated limitations are that the kernel still needs optimisation and "our performance still shows gaps compared to the pre-trained model." No code repository is linked (arXiv HTML) huggingface.

How it aged. As of 23 November 2026, the arXiv abstract page lists only v1, from June 2025 (arXiv). The "next version" with efficiency numbers has not appeared on arXiv, and the page links no code. No venue acceptance was found. On the evidence available, the paper stalled at the "work in progress" stage it declared huggingface.

The idea of compressing the KV cache in the frequency domain did continue elsewhere. FAEDKV (arXiv 2507.20030, v1 dated 26 July 2025, preprint, by Runchao Li, Yao Fu, Mu Sheng, Xianxuan Long, Haotian Yu and Pan Li) proposes an "Infinite-Window Fourier Transform" for "unbiased information retention." It claims "superiority over existing methods by up to 22%" on LongBench, but its abstract names no baselines or models and does not mention FourierAttention (arXiv). It too lists only v1 and no venue. Search results suggest FourierAttention is cited by later KV-cache papers, including CommonKV and a May 2026 paper on int4 KV caches for Apple Silicon. Those pages were not opened, so how they use the work is not checked here huggingface.

On these LongBench averages, a simple token-selection method (SnapKV) slightly beat a mathematically more elaborate scheme on LLaMA3.1-8B (37.07 vs 36.98), though it trailed on LLaMA3.2-3B (34.83 vs 36.33). That pattern has recurred often enough to treat as a prior huggingface.

Why it matters now. The diagnosis is the part worth keeping. Head dimensions are heterogeneous, and the paper's noise probe on LLaMA3.1-8B measured it: noise on dimensions 0–69 left NIAH at 97.02, while noise on dimensions 70–127 dropped it to 53.20 (arXiv HTML). That is a cheap, reusable observation for anyone designing per-dimension KV compression or quantisation. The method itself is an example of a common failure: a training-free compression scheme announced with a kernel to follow, and the kernel is what decides whether it ever ships huggingface.

Latent Space: Felix Rieseberg on why Claude should have its own computer (March 2026)

1 source, 3 min read

What it said. This is an old item, revisited from a Latent Space episode of 17 March 2026 with Felix Rieseberg of Anthropic, formerly of the Slack desktop app and Electron. He describes Claude Cowork as "a user friendly version of Claude Code" and "Claude Code running in original machine with a little bit of padding, a little bit more guardrails." The transcript likely means "a virtual machine." Anthropic saw users, technical and non-technical, applying Claude Code to non-coding work such as expenses, receipts and knowledge bases. Cowork was assembled in about ten days from many existing internal prototypes, and he insists that the ten-day number needs the caveat that much was already built. His product theses: - Execution is now cheap enough to "build all the candidates" instead of writing memos. - Silicon Valley undervalues the local computer. - A VM is both a safety boundary and a capability unlock, because "approve every command" is not a viable long-term UX. - Markdown skills may matter more than MCP tool schemas. - Personal skills should be portable across agent products.

He also voiced concern about junior roles and labor disruption source.

How it aged. The product shipped and widened. A Claude tweet embedded in the newsletter, dated 12 January 2026, introduced Cowork as giving Claude "access to a folder on your computer" source. TechRadar reported on 10 April 2026 that "Claude Cowork and Claude Code on Desktop are generally available for all paying customers across Mac and Windows," on "paid Pro, Team and Enterprise plans." It listed "role-based access controls for teams, spend limits and use controls for governance," plus "analytics and visibility, and integrations and plugins." It quoted Anthropic: "Claude Code helped developers transition from handing Claude questions to whole tasks, and we're seeing the same pattern across the entire organization with Claude Cowork." TechRadar gave no adoption numbers (TechRadar). What happened to Cowork's packaging after April was not checked for this issue source.

The skills-over-tool-schemas thesis aged well. The Agent Skills format was already an open standard by the time of the interview, with the stated aim of "Cross-product reuse," "Build a skill once and use it across any skills-compatible agent" (agentskills.io). That addresses the technical half of Rieseberg's portability problem. It does not address the half he called unresolved, the tension between public reusable workflows and private user context. The security record above also complicates "skills as a lightweight abstraction": the lightness is what makes them an injection channel (arXiv) source.

The sandbox thesis is a design bet that still looks right but is not proven by anything read here. No independent evaluation of Cowork's VM isolation was found. The junior-jobs concern got harder evidence than he had in March. Anthropic's October 2026 study reports junior technical hires falling "from 27% to 9%," with "Around a third of ML and a fifth of SWE managers" reporting they hired less because of AI (Anthropic). That is one company, with the authors' own warning that much of its situation "cannot be replicated across the economy," but it is the company he works for source.

Why it matters now. Two of his claims are worth keeping as product-design heuristics. The first is that user-friendly can mean superset, his VS Code analogy: the simpler-looking tool won on hackability. The second is that the frontier is "trusted task execution," not better chat. The Claude Code team's November 2026 verification post is effectively the engineering answer to his approval-fatigue problem. In place of approving every command, it uses checks written by code owners, a commit gate with "off, observe, and enforce modes," and escalation to a human after three failed rounds (claude.dev). The safety model moved from per-action permission to per-outcome evidence source.

Anthropic's "Best Practices for Using Claude Opus 4.7 with Claude Code" (April 2026)

1 source, 3 min read

What it said. This is an old item, revisited. The guide, dated 16 April 2026, recommends treating Claude "more like a capable engineer you're delegating to than a pair programmer you're guiding line by line." Its specific advice: - "Specify the task up front, in the first turn," including intent, constraints, acceptance criteria and file locations. - Keep user turns few, because "Every user turn adds reasoning overhead." - Effort: "The default effort level for Opus 4.7 in Claude Code is now xhigh," with high for concurrent sessions or cost savings, and max showing "diminishing returns." - "Extended Thinking with a fixed thinking budget is not supported in Opus 4.7"; thinking is adaptive. - Use auto mode where the model can be trusted, and set up notifications for completed tasks. - Expect higher token use from "an updated tokenizer and a proclivity to think more." - The model "calls tools less often and reasons more" and "spawns fewer subagents by default." - Closing advice: "We recommend keeping effort at xhigh and seeing how far your first turn takes you" (Claude blog) claude.

How it aged. The specific settings did not last. A third-party guide to Opus 5.5 in Claude Code quotes Anthropic's Opus 5.5 prompting guide: "Start at medium, the default on Claude Opus 5.5 (Claude Opus 5 defaults to high), set it explicitly." It quotes the Claude Code docs: "high on every model that supports effort, except that Opus 5.5 defaults to medium." Its own advice is to "Reserve xhigh and max for work where you've measured a quality gain" (claudefa.st). Anthropic's primary pages for Opus 5 and 5.5 were not fetched, so these quotations come second-hand. If accurate, the default effort fell from xhigh to high to medium within about seven months, and the April advice to keep xhigh is now the wrong starting point. The same guide says explicit "Double-check your work" instructions should stay deleted, which it describes as Opus 5 guidance "still in force," and that "On Opus 5.5, most of the prompt work is removal" claude.

The delegation principle survived and grew. The same guide's FAQ advises: "State the finish line, give the whole task in one message." The strongest primary evidence is the claude.dev post of 16 November 2026, bylined Addy Osmani and written in the voice of the Claude Code team. It says "the vast majority of our code (~90%) is written and reviewed by Claude." It calls verification loops "the building block that lets you delegate more work to Claude": without them "you can really only run one or two Claudes at a time"; with them, "dozens or hundreds in parallel." The human's job becomes "defining what 'done' looks like and reviewing the evidence." It reports that a separate grader checking work against a rubric "improved task success by up to 10 points over a standard prompting loop," in internal testing on structured file generation (claude.dev). Notably, the April guide said nothing about verification. Seven months later, verification is the main topic claude.

Why it matters now. The April guide had a durable layer and a perishable layer. The durable layer is to delegate rather than steer: front-load the specification, batch questions, define acceptance criteria. The perishable layer is effort levels, thinking controls and subagent nudges, which have been re-tuned with each model. The practical rule: put acceptance criteria and verification hooks in the repository, where they survive model changes, and re-measure effort settings on each upgrade instead of carrying them over claude.

Ways of thinking

Thinking you are thinking

4 sources, 2 min read

This week's Brain Food quotes Leslie Lamport: "If you're thinking without writing, you only think you're thinking." Around it, Shane Parrish places two other short notes. One is "The more you hedge what you say, the more you remove the part worth saying." The other separates feeling from interpretation: "The feeling is a fact, but the story is a guess" source.

Lamport's line is usually read as advice about clarity: writing exposes gaps that thinking alone hides. Today's items suggest a sharper version. Writing is the point at which a claim becomes checkable. Anthropic's 2026 guidance to delegate to Claude rests on the same mechanism. "Specify the task up front, in the first turn," with intent, constraints and acceptance criteria, is a demand that the human finish the thinking in writing before execution starts claude. The Claude Code team's verification post goes further. The human's job becomes "defining what 'done' looks like," written as checks that the change cannot modify (claude.dev). An unwritten acceptance criterion cannot be verified by anyone, human or model source.

Parrish's line about feelings maps cleanly onto the productivity data. Anthropic's 2025 survey respondents felt a +50% productivity gain. The report itself cites METR's finding that experienced developers overestimated their AI speed-up, and it lists social-desirability and recency bias among its limitations (Anthropic). The feeling was real data. The story attached to it ("I am 50% more productive") was a guess until the 2026 panel study measured commits and revert rates, and even that study warns that "More code does not necessarily mean better code" anthropic.

The hedging note needs one qualification for a briefing like this one. Parrish is right that reflexive hedging removes the claim. A scope statement is not the same thing as a hedge. "e-means beats Lloyd's 25-epoch loss in 1.4 to 2.6 epochs on the authors' datasets, unreplicated" says more than "e-means is 10x faster," because it tells you where the claim stops source. The useful discipline is to state the claim at full strength, then write its boundary as a separate sentence, and never let the boundary dissolve the claim. AlphaSignal's e-means piece is a mild example of the failure in the other direction. It reports "24 benchmarks" (the paper lists 17 datasets) and a cross-scale "10 to 45 times" as if they described one configuration. That is the unhedged version, and it is less accurate source.

The practical takeaway is that writing things down does two jobs. It finishes the thinking, and it produces an artifact someone else can check. Both a model working on a delegated task and a reader acting on a newsletter need that second part source.

Wildcards

SOAR: teaching models to teach themselves (January 2026)

1 source, 2 min read

Why this is here: exploring recursive self-improvement and agent harness techniques.

SOAR (Self-Optimization with Asymmetric RL) is from "Teaching Models to Teach Themselves: Reasoning at the Edge of Learnability," arXiv 2601.18778. v1 is dated 26 January 2026 and v3, the current version, 30 June 2026. The authors are Shobhita Sundaram (MIT, working during a Meta internship), John Quan, Ariel Kwiatkowski, Kartik Ahuja, Yann Ollivier and Julia Kempe (Meta FAIR, Kempe also NYU). The arXiv page lists it as ICML 2026 (arXiv), and the project page says "ICML 2026 (Spotlight)" (SOAR project page). It is a peer-reviewed conference paper, not just a preprint arxiv.

The problem: reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards stalls when the model almost never succeeds, because there is no signal. The authors build hard subsets of MATH and HARP containing only problems the model failed in all 128 attempts. A teacher model, initialised from the same Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct as the student, writes synthetic question-answer pairs. The student trains on them for a short inner loop. The teacher is rewarded by the student's improvement on a sample of the real hard problems, which the teacher never sees. This is bilevel meta-RL, and the reward is grounded in student progress rather than an intrinsic "learnability" score (arXiv HTML) arxiv.

Paper facts. The main model is Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct, with Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct in ablations. Training uses MATH and HARP, with OlympiadBench held out for transfer. The baselines are Hard-Only RL (including a 4x-compute version with group size 128), an intrinsic-reward teacher and SeRL. On MATH, training a fresh student on the teacher's promoted questions gains +9.3% pass@32 over Hard-Only RL, against +2.8% for the 4x-compute Hard-Only variant. On HARP the gain is +4.2%. The project page calls these "2× and 1.5× improvement, respectively." The Hard-Only MATH baseline's pass@32 is 9.6. Extending Hard-Only training from 1,500 to 6,500 steps gave no improvement (arXiv HTML; SOAR project page). The counter-intuitive result is about the teacher's questions. Only 32.8% of the promoted problems had fully correct solutions, and 63% were well-posed. Questions from the intrinsic-reward teacher were 55% correct yet trained worse students, and intrinsic teachers lose about 70% of the base model's diversity, per the project page. The authors' stated limitations: "Our primary limitation is the computational cost of running bilevel RL loops"; inner-loop training "necessitates training parallel students for stability"; and scaling "beyond 3-8B models" is future work. No code repository is linked on the arXiv page or the project page arxiv.

The relevance to recursive self-improvement is narrow but concrete. The paper shows that, at 3B scale on maths, a model can produce a useful curriculum for problems it cannot yet solve, as long as the curriculum is scored by measured downstream progress rather than self-assessment. The finding that "the structure and well-posedness of questions are more critical for learning progress than solution correctness" (arXiv) is the transferable idea for agent harnesses. Curricula and practice tasks generated by an agent should be judged by held-out gains, not by whether their answers are right. It holds up as a careful, small-scale result. Whether it survives larger models and non-maths domains is untested, and no follow-up work turned up in a search as of today arxiv.

RLinf-VLA: reinforcement learning for vision-language-action models (October 2025)

1 source, 2 min read

Why this is here: exploring inference infrastructure, kernels, and model tooling.

RLinf-VLA is the algorithm-side technical report of RLinf, an open-source framework for RL post-training of vision-language-action (VLA) models, the policies that map camera input and instructions to robot actions. The arXiv paper is 2510.06710. v1 is dated 8 October 2025 and v3 12 August 2026, and the comments say "Accepted to RSS 2026" (arXiv). The authors are listed as the RLinf team, with affiliations including Tsinghua University, Zhongguancun Academy, Infinigence AI, Peking University, UC Berkeley, Harbin Institute of Technology and the Institute of Automation of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (arXiv HTML) huggingface.

The infrastructure problem is the reason it belongs in a tooling exploration. RL for robot policies interleaves three very different workloads on GPUs: simulator rendering, policy inference for rollouts, and gradient training. RLinf-VLA's contribution is a resource-allocation layer that switches between collocated, disaggregated and "hybrid fine-grained pipeline" placement. The abstract reports "a 1.61×-1.88× training speedup on ManiSkill." The full text reports it running 1.34x to 2.27x faster than SimpleVLA-RL under collocated settings on LIBERO and RoboTwin (arXiv; arXiv HTML) huggingface.

Paper facts. The base models are OpenVLA and OpenVLA-OFT, trained with GRPO and PPO. - LIBERO, with one OpenVLA-OFT model across 130 tasks: average success rose from 42.09% to 98.11%. The paper's own text describes the training curve as starting near 73%, which does not match its table. - ManiSkill, OpenVLA-OFT: in-distribution success went from 28.13% to 97.66% with PPO, and out-of-distribution success from 18.29% to 77.05%. - RoboTwin: the average reached 84.63%, against SimpleVLA-RL's 72.18% on the same six tasks. - Real robot: a single drawer-closing task, where the SFT and RL policies both succeeded 8 out of 10 times. The RL policy is described as more efficient. The robot model is not named in the text read.

The hardware used for the efficiency tests is not stated in the main text read. There is no dedicated limitations section. The authors' hedges include "the effect of these techniques is task-dependent" and that the collocated mode "remains sub-optimal." Code is open source on GitHub (arXiv HTML) huggingface.

How it aged is the stronger part of the story, with the caveat that most of the evidence is the project's own README. As of today the repository shows about 5.7k stars and 855 forks under Apache-2.0, with releases v0.1 (December 2025) through v0.4 (October 2026) (GitHub). The README's news log claims several milestones, none independently checked here: - "Isaac Lab v3.0.0 officially adopts RLinf as its reinforcement learning (RL) training infrastructure" (August 2026). - RLinf was "officially welcomed into the PyTorch Ecosystem" (August 2026). - Two papers were accepted at OSDI 2026. - In November 2026, NVIDIA's Isaac RL post-training "adopts RLinf as a distributed rollout and training runtime."

It claims fleet learning "validated on up to 96 real robots across 5 real-world tasks." Model support has grown to π₀/π₀.₅, GR00T N1.5 to N1.7 and several world models, and hardware support to Intel and MetaX GPUs. For a reader interested in inference infrastructure, RLinf shows that scheduling across heterogeneous workloads is becoming the bottleneck in embodied RL, and that a research framework from a Chinese academic-industry group is being positioned as shared infrastructure for that work huggingface.
