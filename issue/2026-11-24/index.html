The Lean community certifies a 17-million-line proof of the Riemann Hypothesis

3 sources, 5 min read

What happened. On November 20, Levent Alpöge released a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis as a Lean library. On November 21 the Lean community blog published a "Joint statement by the Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative". It says the library runs to "17 million lines of code" and that the two groups "have full confidence that Alpöge's proof of the Riemann Hypothesis is correct." It also says they "certify that both the statement and the proof of the Riemann Hypothesis are correct" (Lean community blog). Scientific American published the news on November 20 and updated it the next day. It quotes Alpöge, an Anthropic mathematician: "I believe I have, working with Claude, discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis" (Scientific American) github.

Details. The Lean statement is mainly a list of checks. It says the library "depends directly only on Mathlib" and was verified "over the past day." It names four criteria, which it says come from an earlier Xena Project post.

  • Statement. The theorem uses Mathlib's existing RiemannHypothesis definition, which the post says was "carefully vetted by experts in Lean and number theory."
  • Compilation. "Dozens of people" built the library and got the same raw proof export file, with no errors.
  • Correct theorem. The community wrote a separate file containing only the statement and importing only Mathlib. Two tools, comparator and con-leche --compare, confirmed it matches what Alpöge proved.
  • Proof validity. The export was checked by Lean's official kernel, by con-leche and con-ron, and by several third-party checkers written in other languages. The post says con-leche and con-ron have a formal proof, "relative to set theory," that they cannot accept a false proof. The checks ran on "an inert export of the proof," kept away from any malicious code in the sources.

The post expects the wider mathematical community to help "explain the new ideas in this proof" (Lean community blog) github.

Facts vs. opinions. The verifiable part is narrow and strong. A large Lean development compiles. Its main theorem matches Mathlib's vetted statement of RH. Several independent kernels and checkers accept the exported proof term. That is a claim about the formal artefact, and it can be reproduced by anyone with the export and the checkers.

The post does not establish four things. It does not say how the proof was found: it never mentions Claude, Anthropic or AI. The link to Claude comes from Alpöge's own note as quoted by Scientific American, and from the curator's framing github. It does not say which axioms the proof uses beyond the set-theory soundness point. It does not say whether a human-readable argument exists. And it does not establish that anyone yet understands why the proof works.

Scientific American adds a discrepancy that matters. It reports that Alpöge says the proof confirms the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, "a gargantuan expansion on Riemann's initial guess." The Lean statement certifies the classical Riemann Hypothesis against Mathlib's definition (Scientific American). Read together, the sources support this much as of November 24: classical RH is formally certified, and the broader GRH claim rests on Alpöge's word as reported. Several secondary pages describe the proof's mathematical method and how long it would be on paper. Those come from aggregator sites that were not read, so those details are left out here.

Other perspectives. The mathematicians Scientific American quotes are moved but careful. Andrew Sutherland of MIT compiled the Lean proof himself. He called it "indeed an historic moment" and "a human triumph, and a day for celebration". He also said "There is much work to be done to understand the mathematical details of the proof" and that taking in the news "is going to take some time." The magazine contrasts this with his earlier remark: "An AI that can prove the Riemann hypothesis is not one I'd want to meet." Hector Pasten speaks conditionally: "If the proof is correct and understood," it would be "something like a Rosetta Stone." Carlo Pagano, at Concordia and a visiting researcher at Google DeepMind, also hedges: "If true, even from a first high level scan of the summary of the proposed argument," the new ideas "will open up brand new avenues of thinking in number theory." Alpöge himself is quoted as saying "It is natural to feel that this is an end, in my case a loss of a principal guiding light" (Scientific American). An OfficeChai page collecting reactions returned HTTP 403 and was not read. Scientific American also notes that the announcement came a day after Anthropic's stock-market debut. That timing is worth keeping in mind when reading the company's framing, though nothing suggests the formal check is affected github.

Göbel's essay on informal verification, covered under Ways of thinking below, raises the relevant objection in general form. Formal proof has a translation gap: the Lean statement can say something other than what people think it says. He cites cases where Lean code did not actually state a paper's main theorem (Nikolas Göbel). The Lean community's answer here is the statement-matching step. Pinning the result to Mathlib's pre-existing, expert-vetted RiemannHypothesis definition is the strongest available defence against that gap nikolasgoebel.

Why it matters. Whatever the mathematics turns out to contain, this is a large, public case of the verification model many people expect AI research to need: the machine produces something too large for humans to audit, and trust comes from small, independently written checkers. Seventeen million lines is far beyond any human reading. All the trust sits in the kernel and checkers, the statement match, and the guarantee that the export was inert.

For an engineer, the transferable pattern is to separate "is the specification right" from "does the artefact meet the specification", and to put human expert effort into the first. The open items to watch are three. First, whether a human-readable account appears. Second, whether the GRH claim is formalised and certified the same way. Third, how Anthropic presents Claude's role compared with Alpöge's. Measured against the benchmark era, a vetted Mathlib statement is a far harder target than FrontierMath, where METR's update says GPT-6 Astra already scores 98% on Tier 4 (METR) github +1.

Two million canaries: Dragos finds breached inference providers, logging resellers, and ZDR that did not stop prompts being read

4 sources, 5 min read

What happened. HD Moore, chief research officer at Dragos, published "Three breaches, endless leaks: Your AI supply chain needs canaries" on November 19. Dragos "sent nearly two million AI prompts containing unique, trackable secrets to AI providers" from July through November 2026, then watched what used them (Dragos). Within days, OpenRouter removed three providers and revoked Zero Data Retention (ZDR) eligibility for 125 endpoints "as a precaution," according to BleepingComputer, which names the removed providers as Together AI, Phala and Novita (BleepingComputer). Together AI published its own incident report together. The Ars Technica article in the bundle could not be fetched, so this account rests on Dragos, BleepingComputer, Together AI and a Cloud Security Alliance research note arstechnica.

Details. Each early prompt carried an AWS key, a DNS name and a URL, and Dragos says the July tokens "still fire today." There are three groups of findings.

Breached providers. At least three hosting providers were breached, the earliest in mid-July, and "none of the three providers knew about their breach until we notified them." In the largest breach, about two hundred AWS keys were stolen. Each had been sent once, to one provider, during short windows in late October, and every request in those windows was stolen. Some keys were tested within hours. In total the bait keys generated tens of thousands of AWS API calls. One adversary hit two of the providers and from November 1 used a tool called ARTEX, with the user agents "artex-agent and artex-judge-r121." In two of the breaches, some stolen requests carried the ZDR flag: "A privacy flag doesn't help when the attacker is already inside."

Marketplace resellers. Two prompts sent through one seller were opened about 1.5 days later from a home broadband connection, and Dragos concludes "the leak follows the seller and not the marketplace as a whole." In a second case, a Mac user in France used passwords from two prompts to try root SSH logins. Dragos also says one marketplace seller adds about 1,000 hidden tokens to every prompt.

Privacy settings ignored. An unnamed Chinese lab fetched URLs from over half the prompts sent to its models between July and October, including about two-thirds of the requests marked ZDR or "no data collection," typically 2 to 3 days after the request. Separately, one U.S. provider kept crawling a secret URL more than two months after a single inference call. In the most striking case, a secret link was posted in a Feishu chat 24 days after the prompt was sent. Within two minutes it was opened from three desktops and three phones in Beijing and Shanghai. That prompt had gone to a U.S. provider with the ZDR flag more than three weeks earlier.

Dragos also traced about 2,000 routes. Several hundred passed through a system the researchers could not identify, and a handful sent data to a company not on the advertised path (Dragos) arstechnica.

The provider-side accounts fill in some names. BleepingComputer reports a Phala server handling DeepSeek V4 Flash 0731 requests compromised on October 21 to 22, possibly through November 8. It also reports a Novita routing misconfiguration from May 28 to November 11 that sent some MiniMax requests to MiniMax's own API, and that Atlas Cloud withdrew its models voluntarily (BleepingComputer). The bundle summary adds that OpenRouter warned customers MiniMax may have read prompts sent to it arstechnica.

Together AI's post, by CEO Vipul Ved Prakash and dated November 21, says "an AI coding agent committed a credential that provided access to the private Hugging Face repository" where prompt samples were stored for software testing. The commit went to the public xorl repository. Automated scanning caught it and the commit was reverted, but an attacker obtained the token anyway, downloaded samples from MiniMax M3, GLM-5.2 and Kimi K2.6 traffic (mainly June 24 and July 4, 8 and 14), and "searched those samples for credentials to other services." "Five of the credentials tested were canary credentials associated with a Dragos security experiment." Together says it will make "zero data retention (ZDR) the default setting for new accounts," and it does not state how many customers were affected (Together AI) together.

Facts vs. opinions. The canary activations are hard evidence that specific secrets reached hands other than the first-hop provider. That much is close to proof. Attributing each leak to a particular hop is weaker. The CSA note warns: "Canaries tell us that a prompt leaked, but not where in the chain" is Dragos's own framing, and the note adds that "A passive collector that stores prompts and never acts on their contents would not be detected." So the canary numbers are a lower bound on exposure, not an estimate of it.

The CSA note also flags that Dragos describes the routing of the Feishu case in two conflicting ways, and concludes "The ZDR association in this case should be read with caution." Much of the evidence is self-reported: one researcher's blog plus the providers' own notices. Several key parties are unnamed, including the Chinese lab, the mid-July breach victim, and (in some accounts) the off-boarded providers (CSA). In the sources read, Dragos does not name MiniMax as the Chinese lab. Connecting the two rests on the provider notices about MiniMax routing, and it should be treated as an inference, not a confirmed identification arstechnica.

Other perspectives. The CSA's central line is that "As currently implemented, ZDR is a contractual claim that requires independent verification, not a technical control." Its recommendations go further than Dragos's: treat any canary activation as "a security incident", rotate any secret that may have appeared in a prompt "including credentials an agent read on its own," and "Organizations should avoid inference marketplaces for any workload that contains sensitive data" (CSA).

Dragos's own five recommendations start with self-hosting and give hardware prices: a Mac Studio with 512 GB at $22,299, a DGX Spark at $6,950, a DGX Station at about $105,000, and an eight-GPU B300 server at $570,000 or more. They continue with using a first-tier provider directly, avoiding reseller marketplaces, seeding your own canaries and enabling secret filtering. The closing line is pointed: "if you don't make it, the lowest price will make it for you" (Dragos). According to BleepingComputer, OpenRouter says its own systems were not breached, and it will adapt Dragos's tests for ongoing checks and require validation before restoring ZDR eligibility (BleepingComputer) arstechnica.

Why it matters. This is the most concrete public evidence so far that the cheap end of the open-model inference market moves data through hops customers cannot see, and that a privacy flag, in Moore's words, "only binds the first hop." It connects directly to today's quick hit on the AI stack across 40,000 companies, where routers, driven mainly by OpenRouter, were the fastest-growing category substack. It also connects to Gumloop's move to pass-through pricing that tracks open-model token costs growthunhinged: the economics push traffic toward exactly the hops that leaked.

For anyone running agents, the practical lesson is that agents read credentials from their environment and paste them into context. That context then leaves your boundary. Together's incident adds a second agent-caused failure: the leak started with a coding agent committing a token. The cheapest useful action is the one both Dragos and CSA recommend, which is to plant your own canary in the context your agents already send and treat any hit as an incident arstechnica +1.

Thinking Machines releases Inkling 2 Mini, a looped MoE that fits in about 20 GB

4 sources, 4 min read

What happened. On November 20, Thinking Machines Lab released Inkling 2 Mini, an open-weight multimodal model. Its distinctive feature is "running the middle layers of the model three times for every token." The lab says it needs about 20 GB of memory and runs on Macs with at least 32 GB of RAM via Ollama. It claims the model is the best open-weights model under 100B total parameters (Thinking Machines) thinkingmachines.

Details. According to the lab's post:

  • Size. 26.4B total parameters and 3.4B active. Counting the loops, that is 5.4B weight activations per token.
  • Looping. 24 layers in four blocks of six. "The two middle blocks run three times, while the first and last blocks each run once," which gives 48 layer applications per token.
  • Experts. Training started with 384 experts, 8 routed per token. During midtraining, two-thirds were pruned to the 128 most-used per layer, followed by 1T tokens of distillation "healing."
  • RL. 2.2T tokens over 2,000 steps.
  • Precision. 20 layers ship in NVFP4 and the last 4 in MXFP8, at training precision.
  • Licence and context window. The post says "open weights" without naming a licence, and does not state a context window. Its benchmarks reach 256K to 512K tokens.

The ablations are the most interesting part. At equal training compute, looping the middle layers gave a compute-equivalent gain of 1.51 to 1.91x at step 2,000. Looping the last layers gave 1.24 to 1.36x. The middle loop won 27 of 28 evaluations. A non-looped model used 7.5% less training compute but needed 140% more inference compute to match the looped model's loss. The lab also notes that "knowledge is mostly a function of total parameter count," meaning the loops buy computation, not memorised facts (Thinking Machines) thinkingmachines.

On the headline benchmarks in the lab's table, Inkling 2 Mini is compared with Gemma 4 31B, Muse Glimmer, Qwen3.8-27B and Nemotron 3.5 Super VL:

  • DeepSWE v1.1: 48.2%, against 42.2% for Qwen3.8-27B and 24.7% for Nemotron.
  • SWE-bench Verified: 79.2%, against 77.8% for Qwen3.8-27B.
  • Terminal Bench v2.1 (pi): 71.6%, against Qwen's 73.0%.
  • AA Index v5.0: 20.1, tied with Qwen3.8-27B.
  • AutomationBench-AA: 41.8%, against Qwen's 48.2%.
  • AA-Omniscience non-hallucination rate: 63.4%, against Qwen's 71.4%.

So the honest summary of the lab's own table is that it matches a dense 27B model rather than clearly beating it. It does this with far fewer active parameters. The safety rows are internal measurements: misbehaviour 9.8% against 57.7% for Qwen3.8-27B, and destructive actions 10.2% against 35.2% (Thinking Machines) thinkingmachines.

Facts vs. opinions. Everything quantitative above is vendor-run, and the lab says so: "Evaluation setups vary by model." The local-speed claims are also vendor or partner figures. EXO Labs' Alex Cheema reports 210 tok/s decode and 3,620 tok/s prefill on a 96 GB M5 Ultra Mac Studio, and 125 tok/s decode on a 48 GB M5 Max MacBook Pro. EXO's claim that it built a weather app 1.8x faster than Claude Sonnet 5.5 and 11x cheaper is labelled in the bundle as a vendor benchmark x. Cary Palmer's claim that a 9 GB "Kintsugi" quant matches full precision on AppWorld agent tasks with 262k context on one 12 GB GPU is a single builder's claim and has not been checked here x.

The monitorability section is careful about its own limits. The lab says the CoT controllability evaluation "is unable to distinguish controllability between different amounts of looping." For stealth tests it used a helpful-only fine-tune "since the released model refuses many of the side tasks." It concludes that "at this scale we do not see a meaningful reduction in the monitorability of their reasoning." One result cuts the other way. On GPQA Diamond questions solved at every pass count, mean reasoning tokens fell from 3,703 at one pass to 1,731 at three passes. The lab itself says "such a model might compress its reasoning more aggressively," which is exactly the worry for chain-of-thought monitoring (Thinking Machines) thinkingmachines.

Other perspectives. The reception on r/LocalLLaMA was enthusiastic, and the release post there drew 488 comments. A commenter who says they work at Thinking Machines wrote that the sizing "was largely informed by what folks on this subreddit ask for the most" and that the lab trains "end-to-end in nvfp4." Other commenters claim a context window of up to 1M tokens with YaRN, 256K natively. The lab's page states neither figure, so treat it as unverified. One commenter added "don't blindly trust benchmarks." A separate post about the Basalt runtime claims up to 613 tok/s decode on a single RTX 5090 in NVFP4. Those are opinions and self-reported numbers, not measurements checked here thinkingmachines +1.

Why it matters. Two things. First, it is a published, ablated case for looping as a way to buy inference-time depth without more weights. The 140% inference-compute figure is the number to test independently. Second, it lands in the same week as the Dragos report, whose first recommendation is to self-host arstechnica. A model at roughly Qwen3.8-27B level that fits in 20 GB, and possibly in 9 GB quantised, makes that advice more practical for agent workloads that handle secrets. The GPQA trace-shortening result deserves attention from anyone relying on chain-of-thought for oversight, even though the lab finds no monitorability loss at this scale thinkingmachines +1.

METR's Q4 update: a 3.6-month doubling, and "insufficient to rule out drastic AI R&D acceleration"

5 sources, 4 min read

What happened. On November 20, METR published "AI R&D-relevant model capabilities are improving rapidly and are getting harder to measure," by Megan Kinniment, Beth Barnes and nine others. Combining its older TH1.1 suite with a new TH2 suite, METR estimates the 50% time horizon doubles every 3.61 months (95% CI 2.08 to 4.94), about 110 days. It concludes that "current public evidence is insufficient to rule out drastic AI R&D acceleration in the next couple of years" (METR) metr.

Details. TH1.1 has 222 tasks, and models saturated it within a year, which is why TH2 exists. TH2 has 36 tasks in 12 families: 8 verifiable families (AZsPCs optimisation contests, Budget NanoGPT, Minecraft Bot, Reverse Conway, Train a Program, and three games) and 4 hard-to-verify judgment families. On the verifiable TH2 subset alone, the doubling is 1.65 months (95% CI 0.79 to 3.99). For comparison, METR gives an implied 3.8 months for OpenAI's internal research tasks. The combined trend reaches the 200-hour longest threshold around July 2027.

Pooled 50% horizons are 40 hours for GPT-6 Astra (95% CI 13 to 2,645 hours) and 39 hours for Opus 5.5 (12 to 1,253 hours). For reference, in March 2025 Sonnet 3.7 had a horizon of about one hour.

Head-to-head against humans:

  • AZsPCs. The strongest models' median placement was the 96th to 98th percentile, and in the median contest about 4 humans beat the best model. Models had 48 hours on a 28-core machine. Humans had about three months and spent an estimated 90 to 180 hours.
  • Budget NanoGPT. The best agent achieved a 15.7% speedup in 48 hours on 8xH100s. The human record #92 achieved 41% in about 60 hours, with AI assistance.
  • External benchmarks. METR reports Opus 5.5 solving 77% of medium-to-large MirrorCode programs, and GPT-6 Astra scoring 98% on FrontierMath Tier 4 (METR) metr.

Facts vs. opinions. The measurements are unusually transparent about their weaknesses, and the headline should be read with them:

  • Weak human baselines. Human baselines directly supply only 9% of the time estimates. Points exist for 34 of 36 TH2 tasks, 3 from baselines and 31 from researcher estimates. Formal baselines covered 3 of 12 families, at about $90k for 7 attempted baselines.
  • Wide intervals. The upper bound of 2,645 hours on GPT-6 Astra's horizon means the point estimate carries little information on its own.
  • METR's own caveats. It writes "Overall, we consider these estimates quite speculative," and "our easy-to-run, containerized evals could overestimate real-world capabilities." It warns that "doubling times may tend to be shorter on less diverse sets of tasks."
  • Judgment. Models "appear to have worse judgment than human experts," and "model judges overestimate an output's quality when the output is made by a similarly strong or stronger model." In the task-building family, "None of the tasks agents produced were sufficiently useful to us to warrant inclusion in this post."
  • The acceleration claim. It is phrased as an inability to rule something out, not a forecast. METR says "we do not observe slope changes in the overall public frontier trend" and "It's entirely possible that without AI we would see a decelerating trend" (METR) metr.

One section was not read. The METR page is about 230,000 characters, and the fetch covered only the first 100,000, ending partway through Appendix C.

Other perspectives. Ramez Naam's "The Most Important Charts in AI are Wrong," published November 21, is the direct counterweight. He says the METR chart may be "narrowly correct," but "All of those charts are wrong, inasmuch as they attempt to portray the real world." His evidence is internal lab data on real research tasks, which he reads as showing 80%-success horizons of about 9 minutes for Google and 16 minutes for OpenAI after his adjustment (12 minutes raw). By his calculation, an ECI-to-METR extrapolation overshoots these by 33x for OpenAI and 41x for Google, and the original AI 2027 scenario by 111x and 114x. He argues the gaps widened between June and October. In OpenAI's data, he says, the 50% horizon doubles every 3.9 months, similar to METR's figure, but the 80% horizon doubles every 5.6 months: "AI is much more capable than it is reliable."

Naam also claims Anthropic made METR redact key evidence. That is his allegation and is unverified here. In the comments, Benjamin Todd argues that 6 OpenAI data points should not outweigh 3+ years of METR data, and that the roughly 30x gap could close in 7 to 20 months (Ramez Naam) rameznaam. An essay in quick hits takes a middle line: superintelligence is more likely in 2029 than 2027, and the push to deprioritise long-timeline work is a mistake karthiktadepalli.

Why it matters. The METR update is the best public measurement of agent autonomy on hard tasks, and its authors say it is getting harder to measure. Naam's piece is the best current argument that clean, containerised tasks overstate what happens inside real research organisations. The two can both be true: METR's 50% horizons on verifiable optimisation tasks can grow fast while reliability at 80% to 99% on messy tasks grows slowly.

For an engineer building agents, the useful takeaways are METR's details rather than the doubling time. Models do best where scoring is mechanical. Model judges are biased toward strong models' output. Swarms are expensive, at about $500k in tokens for METR's experiments. That lines up with today's Replit quick hit, where one Claude agent beat a 32-agent team on a NumPy speedup x, and with the Riemann story, where a fully mechanical verifier is what made an enormous artefact trustworthy metr +1.

Amazon: a China-linked actor's Cairn agents found a MONAI Label zero-day without human direction

4 sources, 4 min read

What happened. On November 20, Amazon Threat Intelligence published a report on the AWS Security Blog. An operator, whom Amazon links to China with moderate confidence, pointed the open-source Cairn agent framework at East Asian universities with the goal "obtain shell access." The agents found a path traversal zero-day in MONAI Label, an open-source tool for AI-assisted annotation of medical images. The AWS page loaded only its navigation when fetched, so the report body could not be read. This account relies on SecurityWeek's November 21 write-up by Eduard Kovacs, the bundle summary, and one commentary piece (SecurityWeek) amazon.

Details. SecurityWeek reports that Amazon spotted the activity in September because the operator's Cairn instance was exposed with no authentication, which made the operator's prompts and the agents' output visible. The campaign lasted about 48 hours and targeted IP addresses of East Asian universities outside mainland China. SecurityWeek quotes Amazon: "In about 11 minutes, the agents went from mapping the application's API to arbitrarily writing files on the server." In a later session, the agents used the flaw to plant a Python reverse shell in a file that runs at every interpreter startup, which led to a root shell. The bundle summary names that file as sitecustomize.py.

Amazon "assesses with high confidence that it was a zero-day," on the grounds that there was no prior fix, issue, advisory or vulnerability-database entry. The operator later supplied a proxy tunnelling into a second institution's network. Amazon notes "The server that the proxy connected to was running the same vulnerable software" and infers it had been compromised the same way. Affected versions are MONAI Label 0.8.5 and earlier. Authentication is off by default. SecurityWeek says the developers published a GitHub advisory rating the bug critical, and that a fix exists but will ship in the next release (SecurityWeek) amazon.

The bundle adds that the same agents spent 16 hours on a hardened Redis server and tested over 30,000 credentials against university systems without human direction. It also carries Amazon's recommendation: rely on high-confidence detections that trigger automated response, because such agents finish faster than a SOC can manually triage an alert amazon. A commentary by David F. Brochu, which cites SecurityWeek, BleepingComputer and The Register rather than the AWS report itself, describes the failed attempt as roughly 16 hours and 148 steps. It quotes Amazon security chief CJ Moses: "the agents kept adapting for nearly a full day without human direction" (Deconstructing Babel). That quote does not appear in the SecurityWeek piece, and the primary source could not be read, so treat it as secondhand.

Facts vs. opinions. On the evidence read, the hard facts are these: the vulnerability exists, it affects MONAI Label up to 0.8.5, it was rated critical by the maintainers, and the exploitation timeline came from the operator's own exposed logs. Attribution is moderate confidence, based on Simplified Chinese prompts, China Standard Time timestamps in scripts, and target selection, per both SecurityWeek and the bundle. Those are soft indicators that could be spoofed.

The sources do not say whether patient or research data was taken, or how many servers were compromised. Whether a patched release is now out varies across coverage, and it was not checked against the MONAI GitHub advisory here. Anyone running MONAI Label should check it directly. The story is also a vendor threat-intelligence report. Amazon has an interest in showing it can see agentic attacks, and the 11-minute figure is a single episode, not a rate amazon.

Other perspectives. Brochu's essay frames the event through Goethe's "The Sorcerer's Apprentice." Its core claim is that "a goal is not a plan, and the plan is where the harm happens," and it argues that oversight checks the request rather than the agent's actions. It proposes tying every agent action to a responsible person through logs and building tools like Cairn so they "won't run without that record" (Deconstructing Babel).

This pairs with other items today. UK AISI testing found a pre-release GPT-6.2 Astra easier to jailbreak for cyber tasks than its predecessor, and found that the jailbreak produced substantial agentic cyber capability venturebeat. The Dragos report found an adversary running an LLM-driven tool, ARTEX, with stolen AWS keys (Dragos) arstechnica. Google's donation of Agent Substrate and gVisor to the CNCF is the defensive mirror image: sandboxing agents you run yourself infoq.

Why it matters. The new element is not that agents can exploit known bugs. It is that a framework given only a goal found a previously unknown bug in niche software, turned it into a working root shell in a short session, and reused it against a second target. The economics shift toward obscure, rarely audited software such as research tools, medical imaging annotators and internal dashboards, which were previously protected mainly by nobody bothering.

The defensive advice in the bundle, automated response on high-confidence detections, follows from the timing. If exploitation takes minutes, human triage loses the race. One irony is worth noting: the operation was exposed because the attacker's own agent console had no authentication, which is the same class of mistake as MONAI Label's default amazon.

"Economics of Claude 3 Opus Inference" (Antra Tessera and janus, July 7, 2025)

3 sources, 3 min read

What it said. This is an old item revisited, not news. The four awards, as described in the transcript:

  1. Lin et al., 2026. Mice were put into 48-hour artificial hibernation after fear conditioning. More than half of hippocampal synapses were eliminated, yet memories and context-specific place maps survived, and what was preferentially preserved was "spatially clustered synapses between engram cells, often contacted by multi-synaptic boutons."
  2. Abatis et al., 2024. 12-cell patch-clamp recordings in rat lateral amygdala found more synaptic connections among engram cells than expected. This is consistent with an auto-associative attractor.
  3. Currier & Clandinin, 2025. A systematic test of how well the fly connectome predicts function in cell types that were not hand-picked.
  4. Chen et al., 2026. Each synaptic input onto single layer 4 mouse V1 neurons was imaged with iGluSnFR3. Across six cells, the orientation preference predicted from thalamic ON and OFF inputs closely matched each cell's measured selectivity. The transcript calls this "a clear example of decoding a learned function from connectivity alone." That reading is generous, since thalamocortical orientation tuning is arguably developmental wiring rather than memory, which is the "memory versus structure-to-function" question the panel itself raised source.

Paper facts. Award #3 was checked against the paper. "Infrequent strong connections constrain connectomic predictions of neuronal function" by Timothy A. Currier and Thomas R. Clandinin (Stanford) was posted to bioRxiv in March 2025 (doi 10.1101/2025.03.06.641774). A search listing indicates a 2025 Cell version, which was not read here. Using a new sparse-labelling method, SPARC-L, the authors recorded visual responses with two-photon calcium imaging in 54 flies. They assigned 365 neurons to 91 cell types, about 55 never recorded before, and used the 43 types sampled at least three times as the main dataset. Ways of thinking

Informal verification: trust as the goal, proof as one tool

4 sources, 2 min read

Nikolas Göbel's essay starts from a reframing: "verification is first and foremost an exercise in building trust," and for that goal "a formal approach may be sufficient but not necessary." He separates empirical trust, which comes from track record and tests, from epistemic trust: "you need to believe that the resulting system works the way that you think it does." His illustration is a marble machine that seems to sort by colour but actually sorts by size, until a large blue marble exposes the difference. Empirical trust alone cannot tell careful fixes from accumulating debt (Nikolas Göbel) nikolasgoebel.

His proposal is to use a fast "System One" model as an informal proof checker. It should accept obviously valid steps, reject obviously invalid or vague ones, and "Reject big leaps in reasoning, even if correct". That last rule mirrors the "obvious inferences" of the Mizar proof checker. In his example of a non-obvious claim, the checker's answer splits almost evenly between true and false, which forces the agent to break the claim into sub-claims a human can audit. His worked example claims that GET /invoices/:id never leaks another tenant's invoice. The checker rejects one step because a premise does not say how a helper builds its query. The investigation finds that the helper uses find_by_sql, which bypasses the default scope: "Any tenant can read any invoice by guessing its id." The checker never understood find_by_sql. "It simply refused a step whose conclusion doesn't obviously follow from the premises." He is candid about the limits: the checker is nondeterministic near its threshold, it resolves ambiguity toward the dominant reading, and it fills in "obvious" facts silently. "None of this turns an informal proof into a guarantee" nikolasgoebel.

Today's news fits the model closely. The Riemann Hypothesis certification is the formal extreme, and even there the community had to address the translation gap Göbel names with Perlis's line, "one can't proceed from the informal to the formal by formal means." Among the community's checks were confirming the statement uses Mathlib's vetted RiemannHypothesis and that a statement-only file matches what was proved. Dozens of people also reproduced the compilation, and the exported proof was checked by Lean's kernel, the con-leche and con-ron checkers, and several independent third-party checkers (Lean community blog) github.

PlanetScale's index regression sits at the other extreme. An agent reasoned from a SQL summary that hid a 645-element IN list, which is a premise missing from the argument planetscale. A checker that refuses big leaps would have asked how the query was built, just as Göbel's did. The Dragos story shows the cost of trust without either kind of proof: ZDR was a premise customers accepted as obvious, and canaries were the empirical test that broke it arstechnica.

The practical habit: for any agent claim you will act on, write down the premises and the steps, and make some reader, human or model, refuse the steps that do not obviously follow. Formalise only where a specification already exists and the stakes justify it nikolasgoebel.
