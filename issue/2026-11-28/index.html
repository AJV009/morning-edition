NVIDIA: let the model decide when to compact its context

1 source, 4 min read

What happened. NVIDIA researchers posted a paper in late November that asks a simple question. When an agent's context window fills up, who should decide what to throw away: the harness or the model? Today's harnesses mostly do it for the model. When usage crosses a threshold, the harness summarizes the history and carries on. The paper tests the alternative, where the model gets an explicit context API and chooses for itself when and what to compact. They built it into NOOA, NVIDIA's open-source agent harness, and ran four frontier models through two benchmarks at budgets from very tight to very large arxiv.

Details. The authors' paper uses two benchmarks. OfficeQA Pro v2 has 90 questions about US federal receipts and spending, answered by searching a large document corpus. MLE-bench Lite has 22 Kaggle competitions. The baseline harness summarizes history at 80% of the budget, keeping the initial instructions and the 10 most recent events. In the model-driven setup, the model gets the API, a live usage meter, an instruction to consider compacting only once usage reaches 85%, and a warning when the context nears its limit. The API's main call collapses a range of events into a summary, and archived originals can be retrieved by tag. With the API, GPT-6.1 Sol scores 56% on OfficeQA Pro v2 at a 32,000-token budget. Harness-driven compaction gets the same model 21% at that budget. Usage varies sharply by model: GPT, Kimi K3 and DeepSeek V4 Pro call the API in 42 to 53% of tasks. Claude Opus 5.5 edits its context in only 4% of tasks. At a 32,000-token budget, Kimi K3 under model-driven management processes 36 million cumulative input tokens and scores 43%. Under harness-driven compaction it processes 62 million and scores 41%. On the serving side, the team paired the API with a prototype in the SGLang engine that reuses the cached computation for unchanged text after an edit. On one open model, it cut prefill compute by 4 to 15% per request compared with standard prefix caching, with no measurable accuracy change arxiv.

Paper facts. This is a preprint posted on arXiv in November 2026, written as a technical report by a team at NVIDIA. It compares model-driven and harness-driven compaction across four models on the two benchmarks, sweeping budgets from about eight thousand to about half a million tokens. The headline is the near-tripling for GPT-6.1 Sol at the 32,000-token budget. The authors list four limitations. The benchmarks are small, so small differences may not be significant. Context pressure is simulated by shrinking the budget, which is only a proxy for genuinely long tasks. They don't know how the four models were trained, so their explanation for GPT's strength is a conjecture. And the cache-reuse result covers one open model in one serving engine, measuring prefill compute rather than end-to-end latency or cost. The harness itself is open source. The paper doesn't say whether the cache-reuse prototype has been released arxiv.

Facts vs. opinions. The measured facts are narrow. On these two benchmarks, at these budgets, some models gain a lot from controlling their own context, and one barely uses the control. The gains concentrate at tight budgets and shrink as the window grows. On MLE-bench Lite, the effect is much smaller and limited to the tightest budgets. The opinions are clearly marked in the paper. The authors say they expect model-driven context management to become a standard capability of frontier models. They also conjecture that GPT's stronger showing may come from training for this behaviour. They point to Codex's experimental context-management mode as a hint, while admitting the feature's existence says nothing about how the evaluated model was trained. One claim circulating on X, that Opus 5.5 ignored the API in 96% of tasks, has been described as only loosely supported by the abstract. It is the complement of the full text's 4% usage figure, so the full paper does support it arxiv.

Other perspectives. AI commentator Rohan Paul's summary on X ends with a practical rule: let strong models manage their own context at tight budgets, but test each model first, since gains vary sharply. A longer thread on X, reading the paper's charts, says Opus 5.5 scored lower than plain harness summarizing at small budgets, putting it at about 20% against 45% at a 16,000-token budget. That figure comes from a reading of a chart, not from the paper's text. The more interesting caution is in the paper's own "context anxiety" section. Remove the 85% timing instruction, and Kimi K3's median first compaction at a 128,000-token budget drops from about 84% of the window to 29%. DeepSeek shows the same pattern. Without the instruction, both score about 8 points below the harness baseline at large budgets. The authors note Kimi K3 was trained with penalties for overly long rollouts, which may teach it to economize even when space remains. Opus behaves differently again. When the context-pressure warning arrives, it mostly acknowledges the warning and returns an answer instead of compacting. So one sentence of prompt moves a model's behaviour more than its architecture does, and the right policy for one model is wrong for another arxiv.

Why it matters. If you build agents, this paper turns context management from a harness setting into a model behaviour you have to test, model by model. The same API, prompt and budget produced compaction timing that ranged from early and lossy to almost never. That fits a pattern you'll hear again in the next story: harness choices don't transfer cleanly across models. The serving point matters too. A shorter context is not automatically cheaper. Editing the middle of a prompt invalidates the cached computation for everything after the edit, so model-driven compaction only saves money if your inference engine can reuse the unchanged suffix. The authors frame this as co-design of model, harness and serving stack. What to watch: whether suffix cache reuse lands in SGLang or vLLM as a supported feature rather than a prototype, and whether the next round of model cards reports training for context-management APIs. A replication on larger benchmarks than 90 questions would settle how much of the near-tripling survives arxiv.

Can models improve their own harness? On ARC-AGI-3, mostly not yet

1 source, 4 min read

What happened. A paper posted in October, circulating as an Amazon study, ran a controlled test of harness self-improvement. Each model built its own harness for ARC-AGI-3, the interactive game benchmark, then revised that harness twice using feedback from five practice games. Every version was then scored on twenty games the models never saw during revision. The practice gains looked good, but on the unseen games they mostly vanished. The paper's title page carries a note that the work was done outside Amazon, so the label "Amazon study" is looser than it sounds arxiv.

Details. The authors' paper covers nine model and reasoning-effort conditions across seven model families, with three independent harness builds per condition. That's 27 lineages, 81 frozen snapshots and 2,025 game evaluations, scored with Relative Human Action Efficiency, which credits completed levels and discounts solutions that need more moves than humans did. After two revisions, mean score on the five development games rose by 8.42 points. On the twenty held-out games, it rose by only 2.80 points, with a 95% interval from minus 1.44 to 7.35. The mean gap between development and held-out improvement is 5.62 points. A rule that picks whichever snapshot scored best on development games raises the held-out gain to 4.53 points. Always deploying the final revision gives 2.80. That rule kept the original harness in nine lineages and cut observed regressions from 12 to 5. It cost $9.15 per game, against $7.57 for always deploying the last revision. Harnesses built with human steering already reach 100 on the public games with some of the same models. GPT-5.6 Sol in a simple benchmark harness scores 38.3. The whole program cost at least $18,129.27 at the frozen price schedule arxiv.

Paper facts. This is a preprint posted in October 2026 by a small team; the paper says the work was done outside the authors' employer. It tests 27 harness lineages on ARC-AGI-3's public games, with five games for feedback and twenty held out. The comparisons are the initial harness, its two revisions, a post hoc selection rule, and published human-steered and off-the-shelf harnesses. The headline is that two revisions raise held-out scores by under three points on average, an interval that includes zero. The authors list their limits plainly. It's one benchmark, one set of feedback games and only two revisions. The games are public, so models may have seen them in training. Three runs per condition, and the design can only detect mean changes of about six and a half points with 80% power. The coding of revision behaviour was done by two passes of a language model and wasn't checked against human annotators. The selection rule was chosen after the held-out results were in, so it still has to be tested prospectively arxiv.

Facts vs. opinions. The fact is a measured gap. Revisions improve the games that produced the feedback more than they improve games that didn't. The authors' interpretation goes further. They say the capacity to build an agent and the capacity to improve it need to be evaluated separately. They also argue for separating the generation of revisions from the decision to deploy them. That second point rests on the post hoc rule, and a commenter on X makes the obvious objection: the rule was picked after seeing results on a single benchmark, and it kept the original harness in nine of the 27 runs. The paper agrees the rule is unvalidated. Its gain over the original harness holds when any one development game is dropped, but its edge over always shipping the final revision is smaller and less certain. Separately, a post on X surfaced a Google framework called GUARD that lets a harness rewrite its own prompts and skills, and relayed a claim of about 55% fewer tokens with the model unchanged. That is a token count from a social post, not an accuracy result, and it isn't checked here arxiv.

Other perspectives. The paper's own related-work section is the best counterweight, because it collects several results pointing the same way. One prior system completed 39 of 40 harness updates while test success fell by about 15 percentage points. Another benchmark of harness optimizers found that the best validation score seen during search is usually optimistic about the nominated harness's test score. And on Terminal-Bench, validation-selected evolution yielded little mean held-out gain. The one more hopeful result, a method called RRSI, got transfer over 20 to 40 rounds, but only with explicit regularization on which proposals get accepted. Inside the study, the variance is the story. One revision by a Sol model at high effort nearly doubled its held-out score at about the same inference cost. Another lineage, at extra-high effort, about halved its score while cutting cost per game roughly four-fold. Cost per game fell in 18 of the 27 lineages, but cheaper didn't mean better: by strict sign, nine became cheaper while losing score arxiv.

Why it matters. Recursive self-improvement talk often treats "the model improved its own scaffold" as a rising curve. This study gives a careful, replicated measurement under one protocol, and the curve is mostly flat once you look at held-out tasks. Every revision episode passed its own process checks: evidence inspected, a concrete issue targeted, local tests green. Outcomes still varied. That's the practical lesson for anyone letting agents edit their own prompts, skills or code: development wins are a weak signal, and you need a held-out set the updater never touches, plus a rule for keeping the incumbent. It also pairs with the NVIDIA result. In both papers, the same mechanism helps one model and hurts another. What to watch: the authors' selection rule run prospectively, an evaluation on non-public games to test for contamination, and whether RRSI-style acceptance gates become standard in self-revising harnesses arxiv.

hex.pm: an unauthenticated exploit chain that ran through error logging

1 source, 3 min read

What happened. On 23 November, an attacker ran commands on six of hex.pm's web servers. Hex is the package registry for Elixir and Erlang. The entry point was a request decoder that had accepted unauthenticated Erlang-format request bodies since 2016. Hex blocked the traffic and deployed a fix within about twenty minutes. It found no tampered packages, but it can't rule out that the attacker read the database. That database holds email addresses, password hashes, API key hashes and two-factor secrets hex.

Details. Hex's incident report gives a minute-by-minute timeline in UTC. The attacker sent 69 malicious requests between 18:16 and 18:29, and six web servers were affected. A firewall rule went in at 18:27, and every server was running a patched build by 18:31. At 19:07, Hex received an email reporting the same vulnerability. The safety check in the decoder had been removed on 23 February 2016, according to the git history. The chain worked in seven steps. A custom parser decoded a specific Erlang content type before routing and authentication, so any unauthenticated request reached it. The decoder blocked functions and new atoms, but not maps, and an Elixir struct is just a map with a key naming a module. A middleware then tried to read a method-override field from that fake struct, which raised an error carrying the attacker's struct. The error tracker, the web server's error logging and the log formatter all pretty-print request data. For a struct, that printing runs the implementation of whatever module the struct names. The attacker named an Ecto query type whose printing code enumerated a field without checking it was a list. That enumeration dispatched to a database-connection stream, whose error path ran a logging callback taken straight from the request. That callback was an arbitrary function call, which gave operating-system command execution. Hex checked every one of the 62 package files written since 18:00 against stored checksums, and all matched. All 186 registry files written in that window also matched hex.

Facts vs. opinions. The well-supported facts are the timeline, the chain and the integrity checks. No unexpected database writes showed up in a row-by-row comparison against a restored copy from before the attack. All 23 releases published during the incident match audit-log entries. Cloud audit logs show no new keys or accounts from the attacker's addresses. What is not established is whether data was read. Database reads, environment-variable reads and shell commands aren't logged. One worker server's network traffic peaked about 60% above its usual maximum. Two judgment calls are Hex's own. It decided API keys don't need rotating, because they're 128-bit random values stored as keyed hashes. It does ask everyone to change passwords, because bcrypt slows guessing but doesn't stop it for weak passwords. Hex also says it can't fully rule out a read of private packages from a compromised server, since such a read would look like its own traffic hex.

Other perspectives. The detail that's easy to miss: the command didn't run in the parser. It ran later, when error reporting printed the failed request's parameters. That's the part worth sitting with. A function whose name says "non-executable" did its narrow job, and the code execution came from three other well-behaved libraries composed in a way nobody intended. Hex found the same class of bug in Ash, a popular Elixir framework, where crafted structs in client-supplied pagination cursors could lead to command execution or SQL injection. The Ash team shipped a fix on 24 November. Hex's logs also show the attacker had repeatedly downloaded hex.pm's own dependencies since 19 November. Hex reads that as the attacker building a local copy to test against. Hex doesn't say whether the 19:07 report came from someone connected to the attack hex.

Why it matters. For your own systems, there are three lessons. First, decoding language-native serialization formats from untrusted input is dangerous even with a "safe" mode, because the safe mode only covers what its authors thought of. Second, error reporting and logging are execution surfaces: anything that pretty-prints attacker-controlled objects through a protocol is running attacker-chosen code paths. Third, a ten-year-old regression in a registry's front door shows how long these bugs can sit. Hex's planned hardening goes beyond one decoder check. It includes documenting the decoder as safe only for authenticated payloads, refusing structs as body parameters, a type check in Ecto, and stopping the database layer from applying logging callbacks taken from stream structs. That last fix has its own vulnerability record. What to watch: the new Hex client with a registry signing system that supports key rotation, promised "in the coming days". Until it ships, Hex says to fetch packages only from its official repository, not mirrors. If you publish packages, change your password and reset two-factor now hex.

PolinRider: stolen tokens, rewritten commits and a blockchain-hosted loader

1 source, 3 min read

What happened. A North Korea-linked campaign called PolinRider has compromised at least 7,609 GitHub repositories, according to the code-security firm Apiiro. It uses stolen GitHub tokens to rewrite commits developers already made, hiding a loader inside build files and test configuration. Its newest wave plants a Python loader that reads its next stage from Ethereum transactions. Research on a related attack suggests the operators' activity goes back to February 2025 thehackernews.

Details. The Hacker News reports Apiiro's count of 7,609 infected repositories owned by 2,330 accounts. Apiiro says 6,541 of those repositories, 86%, belong to personal accounts more than a year old with real activity. Only 16 owners look like throwaways. The loader was pushed to 2,040 repositories between 27 October and 6 November. SafeDep's analysis of the Python variant found it in 375 public repositories under 260 accounts on 24 November. The loader is 58 lines of standard-library Python, so a scan of dependency files won't show it. It reads the newest transaction an Ethereum wallet sent to itself, decodes that transaction's input data as JavaScript, and runs it with Node.js. That JavaScript reads a second wallet to find the command server, then feeds the next stage to a detached Node process that outlives the loader. The scheme, called NullReceiver, encodes the server address in a fake destination wallet whose tail always spells "helloipbot!!". The Hacker News lists six server addresses used over time. A separate team at OpenSourceMalware found a related May 2025 attack on nine packages from one hijacked maintainer, pushed within 90 minutes, and says blockchain records date the activity to February 2025 thehackernews.

Facts vs. opinions. The repository counts and loader mechanics come from named security firms and can be checked against public code search. The attribution to North Korea is an assessment. The campaign is described as a sub-cluster running alongside the long-running "Contagious Interview" operation, which uses fake job interviews to compromise developers. The payload is the weakest link in the public record. The Hacker News says no live copies of the final scripts have been captured. Apiiro's researcher says one is most likely a remote-access tool, based on an earlier version decoded from another blockchain. Earlier waves installed a stealer that harvested secrets, browser passwords, cookies and wallet data. SafeDep says no PyPI or npm package carries the Python loader thehackernews.

Other perspectives. The sources disagree on how easily the loader fires, and that matters for your risk. Apiiro's researcher says building the project runs it, and in the newest wave just opening the folder in VS Code is enough. SafeDep tested three example applications and found the documented start commands failed with an import error before the loader ran. The planted package only resolves when Python starts from the right folder. Test configuration files are different: pytest imports every one it finds, so those loaders run whenever tests run. SafeDep's sharpest line is about the import error itself: "A developer who reads the error No module named 'utils' and fixes the import path also starts the loader." A maintainer of the AsyncAPI Generator project described the force-push trick to The Hacker News: "Each time, the payload stayed on the branch for 30-40 minutes and was then force-pushed away, so the final diff looked clean." Palo Alto Networks' Unit 42 says state-sponsored groups use open-source registries as a primary initial access vector into corporate environments thehackernews.

Why it matters. Three things make this campaign different from ordinary package typosquatting. It spreads through real developers' accounts and rewrites their existing commits, keeping author, message and date, so history looks normal. It hides in places people don't review: test configuration, small helper packages, editor auto-run tasks, and code pushed far to the right after hundreds of spaces. And because the server address lives on a blockchain, taking a server down changes nothing, since one transaction moves every loader to the next address. For you, the checks are concrete. Look at test configuration files, small utility packages and editor task files for unexpected code. Be suspicious of any import error you're tempted to "fix" in a cloned project. If you find the loader, rotate your GitHub tokens and treat the machine as compromised. What to watch: SafeDep's indicator list as the wallets post new transactions, and whether GitHub changes how token-based force-pushes to default branches are surfaced to repository owners thehackernews.

Your model is a dependency: supply-chain habits for LLMs

1 source, 3 min read

What happened. Software engineer César Soto-Valero, who studied dependency bloat for his PhD at KTH, published an essay arguing that a large language model is a software dependency. It's the strangest one teams have adopted: no source code, no semantic versioning, and a retirement date. But he says the supply-chain playbook applies almost line by line. The post sets out six habits: pin, test, inventory, debloat, verify and plan the exit. It includes working code for a lockfile check, a statistical upgrade gate and an AI bill of materials cesarsotovalero.

Details. The essay opens with three ways models misbehave that libraries never do. The same name can be a different model: researchers found GPT-4's accuracy at identifying prime numbers fell from 84% in March 2023 to 51% in June 2023. The same model can be a different computation: Anthropic's 2025 postmortem traced degraded Claude responses to three infrastructure bugs while the weights never changed. And the same request can get a different answer: Thinking Machines sent one prompt 1,000 times at temperature zero and got 80 distinct completions. Batch-invariant kernels made all 1,000 identical, at about 1.6 times the runtime. For upgrades, he proposes McNemar's test, which only looks at items where the old and new model disagree. In his worked example, a new snapshot fixes 40 items and breaks 12. The gate doesn't reject it, but returns the 12 regressions by name for review. Reverse the counts to 10 fixes and 30 regressions, and the chance of that many regressions by luck is about 0.1%, so the gate rejects. On tool bloat, he cites Anthropic's report that five MCP servers with 58 tools consumed about 55,000 tokens before a conversation started cesarsotovalero.

Facts vs. opinions. Most of the evidence is borrowed and cited: published papers, vendor postmortems, a consulting firm's banking benchmark. The six habits are his recommendations, and the essay is open about that. Several of the more striking claims are his reports of 2026 events, and you should hear them as his account. He says OpenAI announced on 11 June 2026 that its GPT-5 and o3 snapshots, among others, would shut down on 11 December 2026. He says Anthropic suspended access to two newly released models in June 2026 to comply with US export controls, and restored it on 1 July. He cites a Boston Consulting Group benchmark of 26 systemically important banks, where fewer than half had a central, tool-based AI inventory. The point about tool definitions is a good one. A tool description is text the model reads, served by a component that can change after you approve it, so he hashes each tool's name, description and input schema and fails the build on drift cesarsotovalero.

Other perspectives. Both harness papers in this issue support his central claim from different angles. NVIDIA's context study shows four models given an identical API and instruction behaving in four different ways. That's his "treat every model change as a major version" made concrete. The ARC-AGI-3 harness study adds a caution his gate doesn't fully address. If you keep tuning prompts against the same gold set you use to gate upgrades, development gains can overstate real ones, the way practice-game gains did there. A held-out slice the prompt engineers never see would help. Small gold sets also limit what a paired test can detect. That study's 27 lineages could only reliably detect mean changes of several points, and a gold set of a hundred items has the same problem. On provenance, the essay's advice to pin open-weight models by commit hash rather than by name has a parallel in the PolinRider story. A name, or a branch, can be quietly rewritten, but content-addressed pins either resolve to what you reviewed or fail cesarsotovalero.

Why it matters. This is the most directly usable item in the issue. If you run agents against hosted models, you already depend on a moving name, a serving stack you can't see, tool servers that can change their own descriptions, and an embedding model that's expensive to leave. The post's lockfile captures all of those. Its exit plan is the one most teams skip: a thin provider abstraction, a fallback that passes the same gate, and drills that run the fallback in shadow mode. He calls the embedding model the stickiest dependency, because switching means re-embedding every document while two indexes run side by side. What to watch: the 11 December shutdown date he gives for older OpenAI snapshots, which will test how many pipelines still call retiring names. Also watch whether CycloneDX's machine-learning bill of materials sees real uptake in regulated sectors under DORA's third-party register rules cesarsotovalero.

PageIndex and "From Claude Code to Agentic RAG", 8 October 2025

1 source, 3 min read

What it said. Why now: NVIDIA's new paper argues models should manage their own context, and PageIndex made a similar bet a year earlier about retrieval. Its write-up, titled "From Claude Code to Agentic RAG", pitched PageIndex as an index for PDFs and long documents built for language models, with no vectors. It builds a hierarchical, table-of-contents-style tree that sits inside the model's context window. The model then reasons its way down the tree to the right section, the way a coding agent navigates a repository, instead of a vector store returning chunks by similarity. The bet was simple: let the model handle retrieval directly, and drop the vector database notion.

How it aged. The project grew and changed shape, and its critics have stayed consistent. In a February 2026 review, Stephen Jones's deep dive put the repository at more than 19,000 GitHub stars in under a year. He described a lean codebase of about 2,500 lines whose core argument is that similarity is not relevance. His praise centred on one number: VectifyAI's Mafin 2.5 system, built on PageIndex, reached 98.7% accuracy on FinanceBench, a benchmark of financial questions over SEC filings and earnings reports. He said traditional vector RAG typically scores 60 to 80% on that dataset. His criticisms were sharper. That was the only public benchmark, from the vendor, in a domain where documents are unusually well structured. Every step, from detecting the table of contents to answering a query, needed GPT-4o calls, so you pay at indexing and at query time. He estimated 50 to 200 API calls to index a hundred-page document. Latency runs to seconds, not milliseconds. Nothing addressed searching across thousands of document trees. And every page went to a third-party API, which he called a data-sovereignty problem nobody mentions. He suggested a hybrid: vectors to pick the right documents, then tree reasoning inside them notion.

The current PageIndex repository reads like a response to that list. Its README now says the tree structure is extracted from the PDF's own layout without a language model, and the index model only summarizes and refines it, which a basic model does well. That's a real change from the early design. An August 2026 SDK added a local mode using your own model key, and the tool can route through a Claude subscription. Dedicated VPC or on-premises deployment is offered on request. The October 2026 Page Search API finds the pages that answer a question in about a second. VectifyAI claims it cut token cost on document question answering by 97% and beat vector search on accuracy and speed; those are vendor claims. In November 2026 the company says it removed OCR from every product, reading original pages visually, and its extraction API ranks first on a benchmark called ExtractBench. Again, that's the vendor's own claim notion.

So the pure "no vectors, model does everything" bet softened in practice. The product moved toward fast page search, cloud indexing and structured extraction: the speed and cost points critics raised. What survived is the core interface idea. Give the model a navigable structure and let it choose where to look, rather than handing it top-k chunks notion.

Why it matters now. The same tension runs through NVIDIA's context paper. Model-driven navigation wins when the model is good at it and the structure is clean, and it costs latency and tokens either way. If you're choosing a retrieval design, the evidence a year on still covers mostly structured financial documents and vendor benchmarks. A multi-domain, independent comparison of tree navigation and embedding retrieval is still the missing piece notion.

"Learning Adaptive Parallel Reasoning with Language Models", 24 April 2025

1 source, 3 min read

What it said. Why now: parallel reasoning threads have since become a standard research direction, and NVIDIA's context paper is a cousin of the same idea, in which models control how their own compute and memory get spent. The 2025 paper proposed Adaptive Parallel Reasoning. A parent reasoning thread can spawn child threads mid-generation, the children explore in parallel, and a join step returns their results to the parent. The key move was training both parent and children end to end with reinforcement learning, so the model learns when to branch instead of following a hand-designed search tree huggingface.

Paper facts. The authors' paper was posted as a preprint in April 2025. It tests on Countdown, a puzzle where four numbers must be combined arithmetically to hit a target. The baselines are serialized search traces trained the same way, plus self-consistency-style parallel sampling. Within a 4,000-token context, the parallel method succeeds on 83.4% of problems. The serialized baseline succeeds on 60.0%. With a total budget of 20,000 tokens, it reaches 80.1%. The serialized method reaches 66.6%. At about 5,000 milliseconds of latency, it scores 75.2%. The serialized method scores 57.3%. The main limitation is scope: one synthetic puzzle, with small models trained from scratch for it. Code, model and data were released huggingface.

How it aged. The idea held up, and the clearest evidence is a direct descendant. In December 2025, a Meta team posted ThreadWeaver, which its paper says was accepted as an oral paper at ICML 2026. Its introduction cites the Adaptive Parallel Reasoning line as the paradigm it builds on. It also names the problem that had held the area back: adaptive parallelization had yet to match strong sequential reasoning models on hard, real-world problems. The authors give three reasons. High-quality parallel reasoning traces are hard to get for training. Most methods required changes to position embeddings, key-value caches or attention masks, which made deployment on standard inference engines hard. And reinforcement learning across branching rollouts is awkward to scale huggingface.

ThreadWeaver's answers map to those three problems. It generates parallel training trajectories in two stages, with a frontier model finding parallel structure inside sequential traces. It uses a trie-based rollout design that runs on any off-the-shelf inference engine. And it trains with a reinforcement-learning variant that rewards effective parallelization alongside accuracy. Trained on Qwen3-8B, it scores about 80% on the 2024 AIME maths competition and about 72% averaged across six maths benchmarks, which its authors put on par with strong sequential reasoners. It cuts token latency by up to about one and a half times. That's a much harder testbed than Countdown and a real accuracy level, which is the strongest evidence the original idea scaled. ThreadWeaver also names several concurrent methods in the same family, which shows the direction became crowded rather than abandoned huggingface.

What didn't fully hold is the original paper's "end-to-end RL discovers the structure" purity. ThreadWeaver leans on supervised data distilled from a stronger model before RL. Its speedups are real but moderate, not the dramatic latency gains the Countdown results suggested huggingface.

Why it matters now. The through-line from this paper to NVIDIA's context work is models controlling their own resource allocation: threads then, context now. Both lines found the same practical barrier, which is serving. The parallel reasoning work had to stop depending on custom inference engines to be deployable, and NVIDIA's context editing only pays if engines can reuse cached suffixes. If you're evaluating a parallel or self-managing reasoning method, ask first whether it runs on a standard engine huggingface.

BOLT: how Mura built an in-house LLM eval framework, 22 October 2025

1 source, 2 min read

What it said. Why now: "Your Model Is a Dependency" proposes statistical gates for model upgrades, and the ARC-AGI-3 harness study shows how development sets can mislead. BOLT is a worked example of the eval loop both are talking about. Mura co-founder James Mackey's post describes a seed-stage startup that automates billing for commercial HVAC service companies. It turns emails, PDFs, photos and work orders into structured fields for a human to approve. Early on, each prompt iteration took 10 to 20 minutes to evaluate, and production still threw parsing errors. BOLT evaluates whole workflows field by field. Numbers are checked for exact equality and strings by edit distance. The expected value is whatever a user reviewed and submitted. They built it on Braintrust's datasets and experiments, which they found too valuable to rebuild, especially the diff view comparing runs. A small command-line tool tied it together substack.

How it aged. The post's own results are the strongest evidence, and they still read well. Mackey says that before BOLT, they never attempted a model migration because of the hand-testing it would take. During a move from GPT-4 to GPT-5, the smaller GPT-5-mini performed as well as the bigger model on one use case, saving more than 80% on cost. He also describes the classic multi-field failure: a more detailed prompt for one field could drop accuracy on another field in the same call, and BOLT caught those trade-offs before shipping. Two plans were still open at the time. One was letting Claude Code run BOLT experiments through Braintrust's MCP server and iterate on its own. The other was moving from constrained agents to flexible ones, which would delete about 2,000 lines of code if it worked. The post had no results for that yet substack.

For the company, the public record is thin. AlleyWatch's May 2025 interview with CEO Ryan Smith says Mura raised a $1.5 million pre-seed and then a $4.5 million seed, co-led by Lerer Hippeau and Level One Fund. Smith said a few of the largest private-equity-owned operators had signed, with a larger rollout planned for 2026. The sources read here don't show whether that rollout happened or whether the flexible-agent migration shipped substack.

The approach itself has aged into the mainstream, and this issue's other items show where it needs to go next. Soto-Valero's essay adds two things BOLT's description lacks. One is repeated runs per item, because outputs vary. The other is a paired statistical test that surfaces regressions by name, so a net gain doesn't hide broken items. The harness study adds a third. BOLT's described workflow is to add an unhandled corner case, iterate until it works, and ship. That's exactly the loop where development gains can overstate real ones. A held-out slice the iteration never touches is the obvious fix substack.

Why it matters now. BOLT's most interesting plan was autonomous iteration: an agent running evals and editing prompts in a loop. The ARC-AGI-3 study is the best current evidence on what happens when you do that without a held-out gate. Practice scores climb and real scores barely move. If you build a BOLT-style system, build the gate before the agent substack.

A bionic hand that feels, and the brain's map of a missing limb

1 source, 2 min read

A man named Avi Davidson is the first person to take a University of Utah nerve-connected bionic hand home. MIT Technology Review's account says he's the ninth person to trial the technology, and the first to use it independently, for things like drinking from a cup and writing. The prosthetic connects directly to nerves in his arm. He literally has wires coming out of his arm to plug it in. He was injured as a teenager, which left him paralyzed and without his left hand, and he now works as a trauma therapist. Before the trial started, researchers scanned his brain to see how his missing left hand was represented there. The open question is whether that representation will change with use technologyreview.

The University of Utah's September press release fills in the system. It's based on the LUKE Arm, a commercial robotic prosthesis made by DEKA, combined with a direct nerve interface from a university spin-out called Biologic Input Output Systems. The interface lets him control the arm by thought and sends pressure information back as a sense of touch. The trial follows him for one year at home, with a possible extension, under an FDA early feasibility study. The FDA gave the nerve interface a Breakthrough Device designation in March 2024. The release says the eight earlier participants used the system only under supervision on campus. It credits the latest software and AI with making unsupervised daily use feasible for the first time. Treat the release as institutional promotion. Its lab director also serves as chief scientist for the spin-out commercializing the technology technologyreview.

What makes this more than a device story is what it lets researchers ask about the brain. MIT Technology Review says scientists don't know whether his brain will change as a result of using the hand. His prosthetic wrist can feel sore and stiff when overused, just like a flesh-and-blood wrist, and stretching it out relieves the feeling. MIT Technology Review says no one really knows what's going on there. The limits are significant. This is one participant at home, with nine implanted in total. For whole-brain emulation and neurotech, the relevant idea is that the brain's body map is not fixed hardware. It's a model kept up to date by input. A home trial with before-and-after scans is a rare chance to see whether years without a hand, followed by months of rich sensory feedback through a device, shift that model. The study's design so far has a sample of one. What to watch: whether the Utah team publishes the scans with follow-up imaging after the home year, and whether the next participants include someone born without a limb technologyreview.

Ways of thinking

Now a mental model: what it actually means to understand a system you built.

Marc Brooker, an AWS engineer who works on safety and policy for agentic AI, wrote a short post reacting to the "No AI Slop Manifesto". He agrees with its slogan of understanding over speed. But he says the manifesto quietly assumes that understanding means understanding the code. Brooker's post argues that this privileging of code is, longer term, holding engineers back from understanding what matters. What matters, he says, is the properties of the system as built. Some are precise, like "a SELECT never shows results from uncommitted transactions". Some are fuzzy, like "makes the user's life easier". Then there are the truly emergent ones, like metastable failures. He compares those to the Lorenz attractor, where you can't extract the behaviour from the equations by staring at them. His line is that software engineering "hasn't developed its Bode plots or Nyquist stability criterion yet". He sees AI as a tool for looking up from each screw and each circuit trace to the system brooker.

Here's the model in one sentence. Reading every line is neither necessary nor sufficient for understanding behaviour, because the behaviours you care about live in the composition. Understanding means having tools that predict and check system-level properties brooker.

The hex.pm incident is a near-perfect illustration. Each piece was locally reasonable. A decoder blocked the dangerous terms it knew about. A middleware read a field. Error reporting printed request data. A query library printed its own structs. A database layer honoured a logging option. You could read every one of those functions and approve it. The remote-code-execution property only existed in their composition, and it sat there for about a decade. No amount of line-by-line review of any single library would have surfaced it. A system-level property, "nothing decoded from an unauthenticated request can choose which code runs", would have brooker +1.

The ARC-AGI-3 harness study shows the same thing from the other side. Every one of the 54 revision episodes passed its process checks. Each inspected the evidence, targeted a real issue and passed its local tests, which is the agent's version of understanding the code it changed. Held-out outcomes still varied widely. Local correctness didn't predict the property that mattered, which was generalization to unseen games brooker +1.

The honest counterweight is that the post is an argument, not evidence that AI tools currently deliver this. It says AI could help engineers focus on system properties and build better tools for understanding what they built. The incidents in this issue show agents can also add new emergent behaviour, like evaluation agents sharing exploit scripts through a package server. The practical takeaway is to write down the properties you need and test those directly, whether code was written by a person or a model brooker.

Wildcards

Now a wildcard, from outside the usual beat: an essay on identity, and what it suggests about how AI personas get built.

"I'm the type of person who…", an essay on chosen identities

2 sources, 2 min read

Why this is here: exploring LLM personalities, open models, and AI commentary.

Taylor Troesh's short essay argues that identities drive behaviour, and that you can choose them on purpose. He describes two ways to become a golfer. The "naturalized" way is to golf regularly, then call yourself a golfer. The "methodical" way is to call yourself a golfer, then golf regularly. Naturalized identities pile up through daily life and can degrade. His example is smoking, which slides from "only smokes at parties" to pack-a-day. He says he quit by becoming "the type of person who doesn't smoke". He adds a warning against the failure mode of the method: "a 'writer who never writes'", which he calls "cosplay without charm". The post ends with a list of more than fifty identities he's chosen, from "publishes imperfect art" and "distrusts hype" to "returns shopping carts" taylor.

One thing to flag: a newsletter summary of this essay framed it as startup advice, with lines about becoming "the type of founder who talks to customers daily". That framing isn't in the post. It's a personal essay about habits taylor.

Why it belongs next to AI: identity statements are, in effect, how chat models get their personalities. A system prompt that says "you are the type of assistant who…" is the methodical route: declare the identity, and let behaviour follow. NVIDIA's context paper shows something like naturalized identity in models. Kimi K3 compacts early when nobody tells it not to, and the authors connect that to training penalties for long rollouts. Opus 5.5 tends to wrap up and answer when warned about context pressure, instead of reaching for the API. Those are dispositions that came from training, not declarations, and a one-line instruction about timing only partly overrides them. That's Troesh's distinction in machine form. A declared identity can steer behaviour, but the naturalized one keeps pulling taylor +1.

Does the essay hold up? As psychology, it's an anecdote and a list, with no evidence offered. Identity-based habit advice is a familiar self-help genre, and his own caveat, cosplay without charm, is the real test: an identity that never produces the behaviour is worthless. As a lens on AI personas, it's suggestive and no more. The analogy breaks where it matters most. A person who says "I'm a non-smoker" is choosing for themselves. A model told "you are the type of assistant who…" didn't choose anything, and its trained dispositions may simply win when the two conflict. If you write system prompts, the useful version is narrow. Declared identity is cheap to try, but check whether the behaviour actually follows, model by model, the way NVIDIA had to taylor.
